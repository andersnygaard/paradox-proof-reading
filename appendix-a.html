A guide to the Paradox sailboat

This appendix is a set of tables and is not narrated.

Appendix A

Fleet register

This register lists every Paradox hull the record can document, sorted by how confident the evidence is that a real boat actually floated. It answers, as plainly as the sources allow, the question every reader of this book eventually asks: how many Paradoxes are there?

Four categories carry that confidence, in descending order. Category A is a hull the record shows launched and actually sailed, in a firsthand, dated account: the builder's own words, a named witness, or a photograph or video treated as genuine. Category B is a hull complete or essentially complete, with no firsthand account of her sailing, or only a single untested launch. Category C is a hull whose construction began and is documented, but which the record never confirms launched, and which may have stalled or been abandoned. Category D is a name on record for buying the plans or the building manual, or stating an intention to build, with no evidence construction ever began; this short list is a visible fraction only, since Dave Bolduc has sold the plans continuously for more than thirty years at a price that has barely moved, and most buyers never post to the list again.

The honest count follows the same caution. At least 69 Paradox hulls have a documented construction start, from Matt Layden's own 1993 prototype onward: a floor, not a ceiling, since it counts only builds with a name, a place and at least one dated record. Of those 69, at least 29 are confirmed, firsthand, to have actually sailed, and that smaller number is the one the rest of this book draws on whenever it means boats that have proved the design afloat. How many of the 29 remain afloat and usable today cannot be stated with any confidence at all; a number somewhere in the twenties, not the thirties, is the most defensible guess, offered here as a guess and not a count. Bill Serjeant, who kept the fullest builders' table on record, put his own verdict plainly in 2014: hundreds of plans have been sold, but nowhere near as many boats would appear to have been built. Will Robertson's open Paradox-type hull in Texas, built from no plans at all, is listed in Table A1 for the record but is not counted in either figure.

Merges and splits

A handful of names in the tables above belong to one hull, not two, or to two hulls easily mistaken for one.

  • The Edge went unnamed for about two years before a name was inferred from another builder's photographs; Elliott himself never confirmed it in anything that survives. One boat, one name.
  • Enuf, So-So and Minnow are one hull under three names across four owners: built and named by Derek Clark, renamed Minnow under Robert Biegler and then Bill Serjeant, and sold on from there to Richard Green.
  • A 2015 suggestion that Whisper had also once been called Minnow was raised and retracted the next day. Minnow belongs only to the Enuf line; Whisper is a separate, unrelated boat.
  • Scout, ex-Tardis is one hull under two names and four owners: built and named Tardis by Lezlie Henson, who sold her unsailed, and finished and renamed Scout by Sean Mulligan.
  • Paw Paw was never named in public by her builder, Dave Skaife, who simply called her "my Paradox." Her second owner, Steve Collins, gave her the only name she has ever carried.
  • Ironsides, now Iota, was formally renamed by her second owner, André-François Bourbeau, in 2015; many on the mailing list still call her Ironsides out of habit.
  • Thor, now Pamela B, was renamed by her second owner, Mark Brothers.
  • Raffles, now Melandro, passed through at least one further owner and a further rename.
  • Planktos, now Skye, was renamed by her 2023 buyer, Kevin Williamson.
  • Swash, now Swashfrog, is the same unfinished hull under the same builder, David C. Smith, renamed by him at some point before 2021. No change of ownership.
  • Noli, built as Paranigma, is one hull throughout, cut as a related project before settling on her final name.
  • Michael Beebe's two boats are genuinely separate hulls: a first Paradox, sold on, and a second, Oogway, sold to OkieBob in 2017.
  • OkieBob's two boats are also separate: his own, unfinished Paradox 357, begun in 2010, and Beebe's finished Oogway, bought in 2017.
  • Alex McGavin's and Scotty's aluminium hulls are two separate boats built alongside each other, not one hull described twice.
  • Rebel is one hull, not two: Christian Blohm and his cousin Erik Blohm built her jointly, though each has his own page in the archive and, read apart, the two could look like separate builds.
  • Simas Butavičius and Simon B are two different Lithuanian builders who began in the same winter in the same plywood, corresponding with each other; a real coincidence, not a shared boat.
  • Petite Lady's builder appears as both "Hugh Miller" and "Hugh Millar" in different sources. One person, one boat.
