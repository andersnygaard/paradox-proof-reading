A guide to the Paradox sailboat

This appendix is a set of tables and is not narrated.

Appendix B

Chronology

This chronology runs from Matt Layden's first home-built boat to the newest hull under construction as this book goes to press. Day and month are given where the record has them, a year alone where it does not and a hedge between two competing years where the sources genuinely disagree.

Before Paradox (to 1992)

Before there was a Paradox, there was a method: Layden built and lived aboard a run of small, shoal-draft boats of his own design, each one correcting the boat before it, while an unconnected amateur sailor an ocean away argued the same hull physics from theory alone.

  • 1976. Walter Castles publishes two letters in the Amateur Yacht Research Society's journal describing bottom-mounted "vortex generators" that do a keel's job on his own catamarans, reasoned out from wave-drag theory. No source shows Layden ever knew of Castles's work; it stands as the earliest documented parallel to the chine runner, nine years before Layden built his own.
  • c. 1982. Layden works out the chine-runner idea, by Sven Yrvind's dating, written up twenty-six years later: a lifting-body hull borrowed from 1970s aviation features, combined with an aerodynamic fence idea sparked by a remark of Phil Bolger's about external battens on his own sharpies.
  • 1983 to 1984. Terrapin, Layden's first home-built boat (16 by 6 feet, a leeboard catboat), is sighted and named off Florida and Georgia, the earliest firsthand sighting of any Layden boat on record, predating the build date usually given for his next boat.
  • 1985 or 1986. Swamp Thing, Layden's first chine-runner boat (13 feet 2 inches by 3 feet 8 inches, 900 lb, 73 square feet of sail), is built. Both dates trace to firsthand witnesses: Sven Yrvind's writing gives 1985 and Dave Bolduc's spoken recollection gives 1986; nothing in the record settles which. A severe knockdown off Maine in Terrapin, caused by her leeboards and six-foot beam, had already convinced Layden they were wrong; Swamp Thing is the fix.
  • 1985 to c. 1988. Swamp Thing is sailed engineless from Connecticut to the Bahamas and back. In the Gulf Stream one night, a passing freighter's wake rolls her completely over; Layden grabs a chine runner for support, which pulls him the rest of the way overboard, then rights himself and the boat, bails out and sails on to the Bahamas.
  • c. 1987. GJAC, a 14-foot-10-inch enlargement of Swamp Thing keeping her chine runners and boom-furled lug sail, is built and cruised for about a year.
  • 1988. Little Cruiser (15 feet 2 inches by 4 feet 7 inches, 1,600 lb) is built, Layden's one departure from chine runners to a bow-mounted centreboard. He lives aboard her for about four years, sailing the Bay of Fundy to the Bahamas, and once freezes her into a Cape Cod salt pond for a winter.
  • Between 1988 and 1992. Riding out a hurricane at anchor in the Exumas aboard Little Cruiser, with gusts over 100 knots, Layden is driven into the mangroves and pushes the boat back into deep water himself; other anchored boats are driven aground and have to be salvaged later.
  • February 1991. Dave and Mindy Bolduc see two black and white photographs of "Matt Layden of Connecticut"'s boat in a magazine; the caption catches their eye, and a year's correspondence follows.
  • 1992. Dave Bolduc buys Little Cruiser from Layden, a 1,300-mile round trip to collect her. Mindy later crews six or seven winter Bahamas crossings in her own right.
  • Before 1993 (undated). Layden meets Karen, his future wife. They buy and refit a Balboa 20, replacing its heavy drop keel with a centreboard and inside ballast, and cruise the East Coast and the Bahamas together for about nine months.

The design and its first voyages (1993 to 2000)

The design proved itself before a stranger could buy it. By the time the plans reached print, Paradox had already sailed a season, crossed the Gulf Stream twice and earned a verdict from one of the few outside sailors capable of giving one.

  • 1993. Paradox is built in three or four months of full-time work in the Connecticut woods, by the Bolducs' own account. Layden has no car, and the plywood reaches the remote building site by bicycle trailer. She is launched by skidding down a steep ravine into the Salmon River. Materials cost about $2,000.
  • 1993. Her first season takes her down the Intracoastal Waterway and out to the Ragged Islands in the Bahamas, about six weeks each way by one later retrospective; Layden then crosses the Gulf Stream alone and sails back up the waterway to Connecticut.
  • January 1994. Paradox and the Bolducs' Little Cruiser cross the Gulf Stream together, Key Largo to Bimini, in confused seas. When Dave Bolduc is too seasick to steer, Mindy Bolduc takes the helm through a gale blowing over 40 knots overnight, while Layden, sailing and sculling alone, declines a tow and arrives a day later.
  • 1994. The two boats cruise the Bahamas in tandem: Bimini, Chub Cay, Nassau, the Exumas, Long Island, Conception Island, Rum Cay, Spanish Wells and Eleuthera, baking bread together on Paradox's kerosene stove at Warderick Wells.
  • July 1994. Plans Drawing #1 is dated, a year after the hull's own launch and first season: the design that reached builders was drawn up after the prototype had already proved herself, not before.
  • 15 July 1995. Messing About in Boats introduces Paradox to print, with a cover photograph of Layden sailing her in the Bahamas, the Bolducs' own account of the January 1994 crossing, and the first printed plans and specifications.
  • 15 September 1996 to 1 May 1997. Don Elliott's sixteen-part series "Building Paradox" runs in Messing About in Boats, written while he builds his own Paradox, The Edge; it sets the build-to-plan standard the design keeps for years afterward.
  • 1 May 1997. In the series' last installment, Dave Bolduc reports sailing Paradox against the centreboard-equipped Little Cruiser for over two months without finding enough difference to recommend altering Paradox, the earliest published windward verdict on the design.
  • October 1997. Sven Yrvind, a Swedish ocean voyager already credited with rounding Cape Horn and, by his own claim, landing the first outside boat at Tristan da Cunha, sails Paradox for about a month as Layden's guest on Florida's east coast, after being mailed drawings by Dave Bolduc and a second correspondent. He finds she "certainly sailed as close to the wind as my boats, probably much better."
  • 1994 to 2003. The original Paradox cruises roughly ten years near Layden's Florida home, logging about 3,000 miles and doubling as his own test bed.

The list years (2001 to 2010)

A mailing list turns scattered builders into a running conversation, and within two years the design wins its most public test: an ultramarathon race meant for canoes and kayaks.

  • 2000. Australia's actual first Paradox is built, by an anonymous retired builder known only as "Chris," sold through a classified advertisement. He sails her once before ill health stops him, and departs from the plans in several ways.
  • 7 July 2001. Glen Maxwell founds the paradoxbuilders mailing list on Yahoo Groups, introducing it together with his own Paradox to be, Zoë.
  • 11 July 2001. Four days into the list's life, Don Elliott states the rule the list will live by for the next decade: follow the plans to the letter, and clear any change with Layden first.
  • By July 2001. Only two Paradoxes are known to be sailing: Elliott's own and Layden's prototype.
  • August 2001. Chris Becker begins "Walden" in Brisbane, the first Australian build reported to the list; whether she was ever finished is never confirmed.
  • c. September 2001. Dave Bolduc founds the companion "Micro Cruising" Yahoo group as a sibling community for the design more broadly, giving the wider movement the name it still carries.
  • August to October 2001. Layden runs two chine-runner trials on his own Paradox. The first, informal, extends the chine forward and finds little difference. The second, in October, is a quantified sea trial on the Indian River, three timed circuits dipping the sail at each tack to cancel rig asymmetry: windward speeds of 2.29 and 2.21 knots, both at 51 degrees off the true wind. "Yes, she really does go to windward passably well without a centerboard," Layden writes up.
  • 13 November 2001. Zoë, Glen Maxwell's own Paradox, is launched on Friday the thirteenth.
  • December 2001. Zoë's week-long sea trial from Cedar Key to Clearwater becomes the best-documented single cruise of any builder-completed Paradox, published twice in later years.
  • 2002. Maxwell short-tacks Zoë against a 12-knot wind and a foul current for nearly five hours on Cumberland Sound, averaging 3.6 knots over 18 nautical miles. "To all those sceptics of the chine runners I can now attest to their performance," he writes.
  • February 2003. Layden cuts his Paradox a new sail, shortened in the foot and raised at the peak for a higher aspect ratio, keeping the old one as a spare.
  • 6 and 7 March 2003. Zoë sails alongside Layden's own prototype at Fort De Soto, the two days before the race start, the first time two Paradoxes had ever sailed together.
  • 8 to 11 March 2003. Layden starts the Everglades Challenge among kayaks, beach catamarans and racing canoes at Fort De Soto, and finishes first overall and first in Class 4 in a field of thirty, stopping mid-race to tow a capsized kayaker to shore. His official elapsed time, taken from WaterTribe's own results table, is 3 days 9 hours 51 minutes, a day and two hours ahead of the next monohull and about 16 hours ahead of the second-place finisher overall, a two-man proa. One secondhand mailing-list repost of the start and finish clock times instead gives an extra hour, 3 days 10 hours 51 minutes; the book uses the results table.
  • 19 April 2003. Little Jim, built by Alastair Law at Poole Harbour, is launched, the first Paradox built in the United Kingdom.
  • May 2003. Little Jim makes a 27-nautical-mile-each-way passage from Poole to Portland Harbour through the St Albans Head tidal race; green water breaks over the boat with none reaching the cabin.
  • 8 May 2003. Layden posts to the mailing list for the first time, four days after talking with builders at Cedar Key: he describes the February sail and gives his full sailmaking recipe.
  • 16 September 2003. Layden posts to the mailing list for the second and last time known to survive, on plywood scantlings: "Paradox is 10 years old and has lived 2/3 of that time afloat... She is in perfect shape barring some scratched paint."
  • 22 to 26 September 2003. Little Jim crosses the Solent from Poole to Cowes in 28.4 nautical miles and 7 hours 20 minutes, the fastest crossing on record, surviving an estimated 80 to 90 degree knockdown from a rogue wave off Hurst Narrows and coming up sailing as if nothing had happened.
  • 29 November 2003. "Zero," built by Andrew Graham in Brisbane, is launched, the first Australian Paradox fully documented under sail.
  • By Christmas 2003. "Petite Lady," built by Hugh Miller, is finished in New Zealand, that country's first Paradox.
  • 2003. David C. Smith begins "Swash" in Oregon, strictly to the plans as a matter of respect for the designer. She is never confirmed finished, and is still unfinished, renamed "Swashfrog," by 2021.
  • 11 September 2004. "Jill," built by Jack Gardiner, is launched in Rhode Island, at the historic site where the sloop Hannah grounded HMS Gaspee in 1772.
  • 2004. Chris Morejohn's "Hogfish Maximus," a roughly 38-foot boat Morejohn calls basically a scaled-up Paradox, crosses the Atlantic from Florida to Portugal by way of Bermuda and the Azores, with Glen Maxwell crewing; her chine runners "work great even at this size."
  • 2004 to 2008. Swamp Thing, sold around 2001 and fallen into disrepair, is rescued by the Bolducs in 2004 ahead of two hurricanes and fully rebuilt by 2008, her chine runners widened by about a quarter at Layden's own suggestion.
  • January 2005. Jordan Boats, a UK builder, agrees to build the first "kit" Paradox, working with Layden directly on the panel-cutting process.
  • Mid-2005. The kit attempt falls through; the plywood already cut is handed to Bill Serjeant, who builds "Faith" from the original plans instead.
  • 1 August 2005. Construction begins on "Faith," Bill Serjeant's own Paradox, in Essex.
  • 2005. Layden builds Enigma, an 11-foot-10-inch boat combining Little Cruiser's hull proportions with Paradox's furling rig, and writes up the build himself online, signed "Matt."
  • 2006. Enigma wins her class and finishes third overall in the WaterTribe Ultimate Florida Challenge, 1,200 miles including a 40-mile overland portage Layden pulls himself by shoulder harness.
  • August 2006. "Enuf," built by Derek Clark in Wolverhampton, is launched. She is put up for sale about a month later and sold at once; five or six weeks after her launch she leaves for her buyer in Norway.
  • 2006. Bill Serjeant publishes the first worldwide table of Paradox builders and owners on his website: 29 boats known to him.
  • 1 December 2006. Construction begins on "JoHanna," Pete Martin's own Paradox, in Cheltenham.
  • Winter 2006 to 2007. The Dinghy Cruising Association's Bulletin prints Alastair Law's own "Sailing The Paradox," fifteen advantages and eleven disadvantages, beside Duncan Gilchrist's account of sailing Little Jim as a declared sceptic at Chew Valley Lake, the archive's only independent, non-owner sail trial ever published. Gilchrist found her going "to windward at 45 degrees to the wind, and at close to her hull displacement speed," tacking through 90 degrees, with the sailing club's rescue boat standing by in case he was wrong.
  • 23 February 2007. Layden announces Sand Flea, an 8-foot decked pram with a single leeboard, the one later Layden design to drop chine runners: "On the theory that the less boat you have to drag around the faster you can go..."
  • 2007 and 2008. Sand Flea races the Everglades Challenge both years, finishing near the top each time.
  • 10 June 2007. "Faith" is launched at Burnham Marina, Essex, built strictly to the plans with nothing but a rip saw, a jigsaw and more than thirty clamps.
  • 18 February 2008. "Auspice," built by Jimmy Lu in Taipei, is launched in under eight months, the fastest build time on record for any Paradox.
  • May and July 2008. Faith cruises the West Country and the Isles of Scilly twice, the second time in company with Little Jim, close to two months and nearly 800 miles between them.
  • May 2008. Alberto Callejon begins "Cachivache" at Rawson, Patagonia, with master shipwright Néstor Siri's help, the plywood hauled some 2,000 kilometres to reach the coast.
  • 2008. "Small Fry," built by Geoff Davis, is launched in Western Australia.
  • October 2008. Sven Yrvind's own verdict on Paradox, reached eleven years after sailing her, appears in print: "A Small Voyaging Boat," in the Amateur Yacht Research Society's Catalyst.
  • 15 October 2008. "Ironsides," built by Stephen Yahn, is launched on Puget Sound.
  • 2008. Don Elliott dies. His widow Wanda keeps selling his companion construction booklet by PDF and PayPal for years afterward, and by the late 2010s their son Josh has built an automated ordering website for it.
  • March 2009. Bill Serjeant's UK Paradox website and registry close after five and a half years.
  • 2009. Phil Bolger dies. The mailing list pauses to weigh his influence on the design, crediting Ralph Munroe's sharpies as the closer ancestor.
  • May/June 2009. Small Craft Advisor issue 57 prints, on one leaf, Steve Isaac's "Enigma, the Matt Layden Interview" and Doug Cameron's profile "The Wizard," Layden's fullest first-person technical statement and the fullest outside biography on record. The issue is sometimes misdated 2007 elsewhere; the magazine's own date settles it.
  • 2010. Layden builds Elusion, his lightest and newest design at 9 feet, and wins her class and finishes third overall in that year's WaterTribe Ultimate Florida Challenge, behind two purpose-built paddling canoes.
  • 2010. Lezlie Henson begins "Tardis" near Houston, her first boat.
  • November 2010. Zoë is sold to a first-time sailor in the San Francisco Bay Area for $6,000.

The second wave (2010 to 2018)

A second generation of builders, scattered across four more continents, takes the design places the first generation never tried.

  • 23 January 2011. "Cachivache" is launched, the southernmost Paradox on record. One mailing-list post had earlier announced a January 2010 launch; the book uses the fuller, photographed and video-confirmed account, January 2011.
  • 22 February 2011. "Small Fry" rounds Cape Leeuwin, Geoff Davis's own account calling it the single most menacing stretch of water she had sailed, then cruises on through the ultra-shoal waters of Shark Bay, the Abrolhos Islands and Jurien Bay.
  • 12 May 2011. "Odyssey," built by Alexander Fadeev in New Jersey, is launched and sails a 250-nautical-mile, eight-day maiden cruise toward Rhode Island.
  • June 2011. Zoë burns to a total loss on the road, under an enclosed trailer carrying her from Florida toward her new California owner.
  • 11 June 2011. "JoHanna," built by Pete Martin over four and a half years, is launched at Cheltenham and wins Watercraft magazine's prize for the year's most innovative home-made boat on launch day.
  • August 2011. Alberto Callejon founds the first Paradox Facebook group, seven years before Tim McKinney's better-known one.
  • January 2012. Cachivache is formally registered, the first Argentine Paradox authorised to navigate.
  • 25 February 2012. Michael Beebe's first Paradox is launched at Rockport, Texas; she runs that year's Texas 200.
  • February 2013. Lezlie Henson sells "Tardis" almost finished and never sailed, after her marriage ends: "a reminder of a dead dream... I do wish I had been able to sail her... at least once."
  • July 2013. Three UK Paradoxes gather at Poole Harbour, Little Jim, Faith and JoHanna, probably the first time three had ever been in one place together.
  • 12 September 2013. Sean Mulligan buys Henson's nearly finished hull, finishes her over the summer and launches her as "Scout."
  • 2013. Cachivache's chine runners are reshaped shorter, wider and tilted downward, and her mast is converted from wood to aluminium.
  • 2013. Eerik Mee begins "THU" in Tartu, Estonia, scaled up 20 percent on every dimension.
  • December 2013. Dave Skaife begins documenting a from-scratch build on his own blog in Hawaii.
  • 2014. Dave Skaife's Hawaiian Paradox sails roughly 415 nautical miles, engine-free, from Hilo on the Big Island by way of Lanai, Molokai and Oahu to Kauai, in two separate four-day unbroken passages.
  • 2014. "Bye Bye Love," built by a Canadian builder known only by the handle "newingtonnate," is launched in British Columbia.
  • 2014. "Al's Boat," built by Alan Ferris in Ontario, is launched. She is relaunched in June 2018, after André-François Bourbeau buys her, as "Pequeno," and later completes a 250-kilometre circumnavigation of Lac St-Jean.
  • 2014. Rob Kellock begins "Mini" in Kirwee, Canterbury, New Zealand, building the masts and spars before the hull.
  • 2014. Sean Mulligan sails "Scout" in the Texas 200, 200 miles over five days in conditions from light air to 34 knots apparent, entered as a fundraiser for cancer survivors whose names ride her sides. Along the way she tows a fellow competitor's damaged boat to safety.
  • 8 to 13 October 2014. At Sail Oklahoma, Sean Mulligan and Lezlie Henson give a joint program on Scout's build and sailing, and Layden himself inspects and quietly approves Chris Curtis's Enigma 460.
  • June 2015. "Thor," built by Nick Bell in South Australia, is launched. He has screwed every joint dry and checked it for symmetry before gluing it, so that no fastener is ever driven into raw wood.
  • August 2015. A photograph of Tom Steinmetz's Paradox, her chine runners removed and a Bermuda rig and leeboard fitted instead, is shared by Eerik Mee, the only such conversion on record; no message from Steinmetz himself ever surfaces.
  • 2013 to 2016. Roselt Croeser builds "Planktos" in Western Australia for about $2,500, while also building a house.
  • November 2016. "Planktos" is launched.
  • 2016. "Planktos" makes her junk-rigged shakedown cruise from Perth to Shark Bay, roughly 800 kilometres, the archive's only completed and cruised junk-rig Paradox conversion.
  • 2016. Glen Maxwell, sailing a junk-rigged boat of his own, becomes the first American entrant in England's Jester Azores Challenge, finishing after losing two sail panels and his self-steering gear along the way.
  • 2016. James Hall, who had taken over sailing "Faith," dies; her fate afterward goes unrecorded.
  • 2016 through 2018. "Thor" comes back up unaided from four separate knockdowns, including a breaking wave that buries her masthead: "God bless the ballast and the rugged design," her builder writes.
  • January 2017. OkieBob buys Michael Beebe's second Paradox, "Oogway," and sails her for the first time that May.
  • 1 to 2 July 2017. "THU" is launched on an Estonian lake, the only built and sailed example of Layden's sanctioned 20 percent scale-up, carrying a purpose-designed junk sail and tacking cleanly from her first day out.
  • 2017. Dave Skaife makes a 17-hour passage to Puerto de Vita, Cuba, a cruise inland to Morón and an engine-free Gulf Stream return in which the wind dies in the strongest part of the stream, leaving him drifting for ten hours.
  • Mid-December 2017. "Mini" has her first sea trial.
  • April 2018. "Mini" is converted outright from a standing lug to a four-sided sprit rig, after a months-long side-by-side comparison. Performance improves on every point of sail, and tacking becomes reliable in all conditions.
  • 2018. Steve Collins buys Dave Skaife's unnamed Paradox and gives her the only name she has ever carried in public, "Paw Paw."
  • 2018. Cory Sellars rescues a part-built Paradox from a burn pile after the death of her original builder, Brian Prescott, towing the hull home through a snowstorm; he later renames her "Prescott Eagle" in Prescott's memory.
  • 2018. Layden enters the Everglades Challenge again, as "Wizard," fifteen years after his first win, and finishes in 3 days 14 hours 43 minutes, first again in his class. The boat is not named in the race's own results, but is widely taken to be Elusion.
  • July 2018. Pete Martin sails "JoHanna" 140 miles on UK rivers and canals over 22 nights, 90 of those miles under yuloh power alone.

Facebook and the revival (2019 to 2026)

A dying platform and a more visual, more international wave of new builders reshape the community just as the design turns into its fourth decade.

  • Winter 2019. Two Lithuanian builders begin independently in Baltic birch plywood, cheaper locally than okoume but heavier. Simas Butavičius thins his panel schedule to 12, 9 and 6 millimetres; Simon B settles on 9 and 6. Butavičius's scheme is later credited by the author as an inspiration for his own Norwegian hull, Miramis.
  • 5 February 2019. Tim McKinney's "Paradox Sailboat" Facebook group makes its first post, after he acquires an already-built Paradox from Michael Beebe the preceding autumn; it becomes the principal record of the community from here on.
  • September 2019. Rob Kellock takes "Mini" about ten nautical miles off Banks Peninsula overnight, a deliberate trial to test the blue-water question for himself. He concludes a drogue only earns its keep with active sailing, and that the boat herself never felt unsafe, only other vessels' traffic did.
  • 16 October 2019. Yahoo announces it will delete all Yahoo Groups content.
  • 5 November 2019. The migration to groups.io's "paradoxsailboat" is completed, led almost entirely by one non-builder member who scripts his own download of roughly 80 percent of the archive's nearly 9,500 messages and funds the new group himself.
  • April 2020. Rob Kellock runs a deliberate, GPS-tracked, one-mile triangle course on Lake Ellesmere to test exaggerated hull-speed claims against measurement: "I say codswallop... let's examine the facts rather than the fantasy."
  • May 2020. Alastair Law gives a retrospective talk on Little Jim to the Amateur Yacht Research Society, calling her "an old man's boat": "If it ain't broke, why fix it? It would be easy to change Paradox, a lot harder to improve it."
  • August 2020. Little Jim's rudder shears cleanly through its pivot bolt off Lepe Spit in the Solent, seventeen years after launch, in a Force 5 with a foul tide. No other damage, and the boat sails on, the one documented failure against the design's otherwise roughly 10,000-mile single-bolt service record.
  • 2020 to 2022. "Mini" makes repeated week-long South Island cruises out of Canterbury: Abel Tasman National Park, a four-day Tauranga Harbour cruise, a five-day liveaboard cruise of Queen Charlotte Sound.
  • November 2022. Christian and Erik Blohm, cousins in north-east Ohio, begin building "Rebel" together, after separately judging a two-hulled Wharram Tiki 21 too much for a first boat.
  • 2022. Mark Brothers buys "Thor," renames her "Pamela B," and answers doubts about her windward ability by sailing an unbroken circle around the Captain Cook Memorial Fountain in Canberra.
  • 9 December 2022. Small Craft Advisor's online edition publishes "The Matt Layden Interview," in which Layden names his own design influences, Bolger first, then the Herreshoffs, Clapham, Munroe, Reuben Trane, Tom Jones and Michalak, and states he started professional yacht design "as a real day job, in 1995."
  • April 2023. Really Simple Sails designs a new, larger sail for "Pamela B," retrofitting onto most existing Paradoxes with only small spar-length changes, the sail plan the book's own author later copies for Miramis.
  • July 2023. "Planktos" is sold to Kevin Williamson, who extends her mast and renames her "Skye."
  • December 2023. "Planktos" is named the Junk Rig Association's boat of the month.
  • 2023 to 2024. "Mini" gets a full repaint. Stripped back, her hull shows bare, unmarked epoxy under 90 percent of the old paint, eight years on.
  • March 2024. Tony Barlow launches "Miko" at Phuket, Thailand, fitted with twin outboards, a cut-down electric trolling motor, extra solar panels and air conditioning for the heat.
  • August 2024. "Scout" is sold to a new owner in Maine, trailered some 3,500 miles home, her fourth owner in all.
  • January 2025. The mailing list's FAQ confirms the plans still cost $35 in PDF form, paid to the Bolducs' address on record since 2001.
  • June 2025. Tony Barlow, recovering from a severe epoxy allergy developed during Miko's build, writes: "Next time Aluminium, me and epoxy can't be in the same town."
  • 19 January, 23 March and 3 December 2025. Alex McGavin builds a welded aluminium Paradox in 3-millimetre plate, with a companion hull built alongside by a friend, "Scotty," in 4-millimetre plate, the first metal Paradox hulls on record after more than two decades of the idea being argued and never tried. By the December report, McGavin's hull is about to take her mast and sail, Scotty's half done; neither is confirmed to have sailed.
  • September 2025 (a cruise made in July 2025). JoHanna's new owner, sailing from Kappeln in Germany, is revealed on Facebook leading a three-boat flotilla around the Danish island of Ærø, JoHanna first to the maritime museum at Marstal on a beam-reach leg, outpacing two larger yachts. Her original builder, Pete Martin, comments: "Good to see she's still in her element."
  • 9 April 2026. Jolyn Barley of Comox, British Columbia, registers "Team Forget Me Knot," a Paradox fitted with both a Hobie Mirage Drive and a yuloh, for the 2026 Race to Alaska. The race's own account calls her the smallest boat in the fleet: "What's a Paradox? Google it." It is her second attempt at the race, after a 2024 withdrawal on a different boat, a Hobie Tandem Island, whose hull and floats had begun to leak and flood under her.
  • 14 April 2026. Christian Blohm confirms "Rebel" is still half-finished.
  • 14 June 2026. The Race to Alaska starts. The official Stage One results record Team Forget Me Knot as never having started, the only such result among the race's 71 recorded entries; she never appears in Stage Two. No reason is given anywhere on record.
  • 14 September 2026. Sven Yrvind announces a new, smaller boat with a square midsection "very close to Paradox," 4.6 metres long and 1.4 metres wide, worked from drawings he and Layden made together in 2007, after finding his previous 5.2 metre three-masted boat too heavy at 850 kilograms against a designed 600.
  • 2026. Miramis, a Paradox scaled up by ten percent, built in Baltic birch plywood with a home-sewn sail, is under construction in Norway, the newest hull in a line that runs back to Terrapin, more than forty years before.
