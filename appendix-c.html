Appendix C

Particulars

This appendix gathers the reference tables the chapters point to: the plans' own numbers, the measurements builders have taken on finished boats and the record of where a Paradox has actually sailed. Each table carries its own source, in the same form used throughout this book, and says plainly where the record falls silent.

Reported tacking angles

Owners have reported a real scatter of tacking and pointing figures over the years, not because the boat behaves inconsistently but because not everyone who posted a number was measuring the same thing. A true wind angle, how close to the wind a single tack makes good, and a tack to tack angle, the full turn between one tack and the next, are related but different measurements, and the table keeps each figure beside the method that produced it so the two are never read against each other as if they were one.

Six ways to build a chine runner

Nobody selling a Paradox has ever told a builder how to shape the compound curve a chine runner needs, so builders have worked the problem out six separate ways, independently and usually without reading each other's mail first.

MethodHow it works
Pre-cut solid timber blocksFir blocks about 10 in long, cut to shape on a bandsaw and glued along the overhang; the method most builders converge on when working the problem alone
Kerf and bend a single timberCut to the plan's profile, then saw kerfs 4 to 6 in apart through the back and bottom edges, bend to the chine's curve and fill the kerfs with epoxy
Batten and fillLay a single batten the length of the runner and fill the gap to the hull with scrap wood and epoxy, built up in short sections rather than one length
Templates, epoxy fill and a planeFit to a template, fill low spots with epoxy, plane the high spots fair once cured
Cardboard patterns and a block built wallPattern the hull at several stations, then build the runner from small blocks, about 3 by 1 in, glued in courses like a wall, sheathed in light glass
Template traced before the bottom goes onClamp a length of bendy plywood scrap along the side panel's bottom edge to trace the chine's curve, then cut curved strips to that template once the bottom is fitted

None of the six is judged genuinely difficult by the builders who used it. The real challenge in every case is the compound curve itself, not the method chosen to cut it.
