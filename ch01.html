Part One: The designer and his boats

Matt Layden, the four boats that taught him, the fifth that he kept, the Swede who came to see for himself, the race and the boats that followed.

Chapter 1

Matt Layden

Boat was his first word

Matt Layden grew up steeped in traditional New England craft. Family legend in the Layden household holds that "boat" was his first word, and little that followed contradicted it. His father sailed, and his father's father had worked the Connecticut coast as a waterman and oysterman. On New England summer weekends the father raced traditional keelboats around the buoys with his sons crewing. Matt's own first boat was a 16-foot keelboat called a Zip. He later described it to a magazine profiler as "a kid's first keelboat," in his own dry economy. It was a working coast as much as a sailing one. Oystermen and racing sailors drew on the same water, and Layden grew up with both halves of that inheritance rather than one alone.

School held him less than boats did. He was bored enough to skip class and read in the library instead. In his junior year a guidance counselor noticed where his attention actually lived and sent him to apprentice at a custom cabinet shop. There, cutting other people's furniture rather than reading about other people's boats, he learned to draft and to work wood. These were the two skills that carried the rest of his working life: boat design first, then later yacht design for clients he would never meet.

The reading habit never left him. An uncle gave him a subscription to the magazine Cruising World when he turned thirteen. Among its contributors in those years was a young Swedish adventurer named Sven Yrvind, who was building ever smaller boats and sailing them ever further. Layden knew the name for years before the two men ever traded a word.

Tradition, not invention from nothing

Out of the cabinet shop came a way of thinking that held for the rest of his working life. Treat every problem as a new challenge rather than simply accept the way it had always been solved, but only after a firm grasp of how it had always been solved, and why. New England's working boats, in his reading of them, were shaped the way they were by the limits of wood, not by any law of the sea. The long overhangs on the classic racing yachts of the early twentieth century came from the rules that measured them, not from reason. Modern materials, free of either constraint, ought to let a designer do better. That philosophy came with a short list of named teachers. Phil Bolger headed it: a prolific American designer and writer whose plywood boats were part of nearly every home boatbuilder's reading in those years. When a magazine finally asked Layden who his teachers were, in an interview published online in December 2022, he answered without padding: "Phil Bolger of course, the original unencumbered thinker and expositor in the field... the Herreshoffs, Clapham, Monroe, on through Reuben Trane (Mon Poulet), Tom Jones, Michalak, and others." A designer who names his teachers that precisely is not claiming to have invented sailing from nothing. His own small boats, he added in the same interview, "have always been a hobby that I occasionally share with others; I haven't really tried to make them pay."

The pattern that followed was consistently the opposite of what the sailing press was doing at the time. That is how Doug Cameron, the one journalist who ever profiled him at length, later described it. When the common wisdom held that a faster boat was a longer one, he built shorter boats with less hull in the water. Where most small craft resisted leeway with a leeboard or a centerboard hung below the hull, he used wings built into the chine itself. While the sailing magazines of the time were running ever bigger cruisers on their covers, he drew microcruisers smaller than almost anyone else was building. While most designers were refining the tall, triangular Marconi sloop rig, he kept returning to a short, balanced lug that, by Cameron's account, went faster to windward as well as off the wind. None of it was contrarianism for its own sake. Each choice traced back to the same firm grasp of tradition and the same impatience with complication that the cabinet shop had taught him.

What he would not accept, in his own boats, was machinery for its own sake. Captioning a photograph on his brother's website in 2005 of a chine runner being glued up, he put the whole case for Paradox into one sentence: "A centerboard or other retractible vertical fin would do the job better, but I have a mental allergy to moving parts and complication." The runners, he went on, were easy to build. They protected the hull rather than weakening it the way a centerboard's trunk would. They took up no space inside either, "always an issue in a small cruiser." It may be the clearest sentence he has ever written about why his boats look the way they do. It sits oddly, and deliberately, beside the fact that he makes his living drafting some of the most mechanically complicated vessels afloat.

He treated an idea as unproven until he had tried it on something smaller than a full hull. Long before chine runners ever went to sea on a boat he could sit in, he dragged and sailed scale models of them to see whether the shape actually behaved the way his reading of lifting-body aircraft suggested it should. Only once a model persuaded him did he cut full-sized plywood. It is the same instinct, shrunk to a worktable, that later sent him out onto open water with a stopwatch.

A boat each year, then marriage

Through the 1980s he built a new boat most years and lived aboard her, cruising the East Coast, the Bay of Fundy and the Bahamas. Each hull was meant to improve on the one before her. About ten years of that life passed before he settled down. The method that came out of those years would outlast every boat he built in them: live aboard a design rather than just sail her occasionally, test her instead of trusting her, time what she actually does, then race her to find the edge of what she can do. When something failed at sea he mended it with whatever was at hand rather than wait for the right part. He once patched a broken rudder blade with a scrap of plywood found ashore. Another time he replaced a splintered yard with a tree branch. Dave Bolduc still remembers both repairs as ordinary life aboard one of these boats. It was not a comfortable decade by any ordinary measure: a boat for an address, whatever the sea broke mended with whatever lay to hand. But it was the only way he ever tested an idea that mattered to him.

David and Mindy Bolduc are a carpenter and a pharmacist from Greensboro, North Carolina. Two photographs of one of Layden's boats in a magazine caught their eye in February 1991, and they drove 1,300 miles to meet him. They bought Little Cruiser from him the following year and sailed to the Bahamas in his company in 1994. Dave has since sculled the original Paradox himself, and the couple went on to own two more of Layden's boats, Swamp Thing and his own Enigma. Their website, microcruising.com, documents every boat Layden has built, and Dave has sold the Paradox plans by hand for a quarter of a century.

Then he met Karen, and there was no time to build another boat big enough for two. The boat had to be cheap, so he found a Balboa 20, the kind of small production fiberglass cruiser he would spend the rest of his working life designing against. He refitted her inside and pulled the heavy drop keel, replacing it with a centerboard and ballast carried low until she drew about ten inches. Then he sailed off with Karen for nine months along the East Coast and in the Bahamas. Her father still sails that same Balboa 20 around Cedar Key. A real day job followed in 1995. Layden preferred a smaller phrase for it than "yacht designer": "participating as a cog in the slow-turning wheels of large yacht design," as he put it. He and Karen made their home on Florida's east coast. He designs megayachts to this day, does not drive and seldom wears shoes, by most accounts the same man at a drafting table for a client's hull as he is barefoot aboard his own fourteen-footer. Asked in 2022 whether small boats were closer to his heart than the large ones he draws for a living, he did not qualify the answer at all: "Of course."

His own proving ground

That fourteen-footer was Paradox, the fifth boat of his own design that Layden had lived aboard. She became the one he tested longest and hardest. She was built in the Connecticut woods in 1993. She then became the boat he lived with and sailed near his Florida home for roughly ten years afterward, testing her the whole time rather than simply enjoying her. It was a different kind of decade from the one before it: not a new hull every year to learn from and move past, but the same fourteen-footer, tested and retested until he understood exactly what she could and could not do.

Some of that testing had company. When the Bolducs sailed in company with him to the Bahamas in 1994, he spent the better part of two months teaching them the particular skills these flat-bottomed, runner-fitted boats demand. He was patient in a way his quietness did not always suggest. One of those skills was his own substitute for an engine. In a 2006 interview with the Furled Sails podcast, the Bolducs measured their own progress at the sculling oar against his: Layden, they said, could scull one of these boats for eight, nine or ten hours at a stretch when the wind failed him. Photographs taken in November 2002 show Karen and Matt sailing Paradox together in the Bahamas at Georgetown, among the small record of her as a boat the two of them shared rather than one he sailed alone.

In the autumn of 2001 Layden first tried extending the chine forward on his own boat and found it made little difference. A month later he lengthened the port runner and timed it against the stock one with a stopwatch on the Indian River.

What survives in his own hand

A design that gathers a mailing list, then a web forum, then a Facebook group around it usually belongs to its designer too, in some sense. Layden's does not. He never joined the builders' list that grew up to discuss his own boat, and he is almost entirely absent from the record those groups left behind. Only two messages survive under his own name in its archive, both from 2003: a sailmaking post on 8 May and a note on plywood scantlings on 16 September. The first is also the only place he ever explained, in his own words, why he had kept away so long: "I've been lurking on the outskirts of the group for a month or 2 and after talking with Glen, Larry and Dave at Cedar Key last weekend I thought I'd put in an appearance." The second answered a running argument about what Paradox, by then ten years in the water, was built of. He weighed cheap plywood against costlier alternatives without apology or sentiment and judged that nothing else would likely beat it on balance. He signed off, as he signed off on most things, with no more than the matter required: "Just my opinion."

What he did write down, he mostly wrote elsewhere. He posted seven build logs on his brother Andy's family website in 2005, narrating the construction of a new boat and the rigging of a cruising canoe almost plank by plank, each one signed simply "Matt." The prose is as plain and exact as everything else he has written: butt-splicing three pieces of plywood into one long panel, planing a laminated rudder blade to its airfoil section using the glue lines in the plywood as fairing guides, "pleasant work and fast if you know how to sharpen edge tools," then fairing the hull with a plastering knife and sixty-grit paper until "well over half of the filler is removed," each step set down for its own sake rather than to impress a reader. One post was addressed partly to his young nephew Shawn. Working from nothing more than a laptop and a bare 3D model, he likened the hull panels he was plotting on plywood to a cardboard model the two of them had built together on an earlier visit. He asked whether Shawn had yet met graphing points on the Cartesian plane at school: "This is a demonstration of exactly the same thing." A note written just before he left for a race that spring reads simply, "Karen home & back to work, boo."

Beyond the family website and the two list posts, the fullest record of his own voice is two interviews given more than a decade apart to the same small magazine, Small Craft Advisor. One ran in print in its May/June 2009 issue, beside Doug Cameron's profile of him. The other ran online in December 2022. The print interview opened with something close to a tribute. His friend Sven Yrvind, the magazine told him, considered the chine runner one of the more spectacular small-boat innovations of recent memory, achieved by a man too modest and uninterested in publicity for most sailors to have heard of it. Layden's answer deflected the compliment before it explained anything: "Sven is kind, but I'm not sure chine runners will go far; their niche is in moderate to heavy cruisers that haul a lot of weight. Not so great for the usual light beamy racer/cruiser or trailersailer. But you never know."

An afternoon at Cedar Key

Doug Cameron, a small-boat racer and writer from Sewanee, Tennessee, who races WaterTribe events as Ridgerunner, wrote under the byline "Sailing and Paddling with Doug." He met Layden more than once at the Cedar Key gathering and at race finish lines, and caught him at length exactly once, for a profile that ran beside the 2009 Small Craft Advisor interview. It is really one long scene, and it is worth telling as one. On the first weekend in May, small-boat builders from all over the eastern United States gather at Cedar Key, Florida, then cross the channel to the white sand beach of Atsena Otie, an uninhabited island across from the town's shops and restaurants. There they look over one another's boats and swap stories. One such weekend Layden sat there beside his Rob Royoid canoe, a boat decked like a sea kayak and rigged with a lug sail cut to just one square meter. He thought the race rule left her a little short of sail, yet she was roomy enough that he slept aboard her. Nearby stood Jim Brown, designer of the Wind Rider trimarans, with a wood-strip sea kayak of his own design. The two men talked boats and tried each other's craft through the afternoon. They kept talking that evening at the cookout fire. The conversation ranged wide before it came to rest on how a sailor might propel a small boat with leg power instead of arms: a kick-up for shallow water, and a yuloh, the long single oar worked over the stern on Chinese craft, redesigned to tack at the end of each stroke rather than stall. Both men, Cameron noted, were informed by that depth of traditional knowledge rather than limited by it. They traded phone numbers before parting, meaning to bring other designers into the idea. Cameron, watching, called it a man "neck-deep in his element," and happy there.

He was the same man at a supper table on Key Largo, by Cameron's account, during a pause in one of the longer WaterTribe races. There he worked out loud whether he could turn the next Everglades Challenge into a fishing and camping cruise instead of a race, and calculated, unprompted, the extra drag a trailed fishing line and bait would add and what it would cost him in ballast and sail area to compensate. In an Ultimate Florida Challenge not long before, he had carried sandbags and food as ballast, meaning to eat the food and dump the sand so that the boat grew lighter on schedule rather than by chance. He dumped the sand on time but still had forty or fifty pounds of food left over at the portage, which by his own account may be why a tire failed under the load. After years of running into him at the same beach, Cameron's verdict was that he is "a total minimalist without being anti-technology." In Cameron's words he hoped even then to move further into small-boat design, governed throughout by "reason with a touch of minimalism." Matt, Cameron added, is rather like the name of his newest boat at the time: an enigma.

The Wizard

Because he wrote so little, most of what is known about Layden comes from the people who sailed near him, Cameron among them. The Bolducs owned and rebuilt two of his earlier boats themselves. Sven Yrvind, the Swedish designer, sailed Paradox to windward for a month in 1997 and came back often enough over the following decade that Layden eventually drew a set of boat lines for him in return.

The Bolducs, interviewed by the Furled Sails podcast in 2006, describe him as "a very quiet and unpretentious type of fellow, but he's extremely knowledgeable and he's an excellent seaman." Later in the same conversation they call him, without much hedging, "our hero and the ultimate sailor," a sailor who "does take little catnaps and things, and he can go steady for a long time." They also describe the man behind the method: someone who "enjoys other people, but he really also enjoys his time alone," which is about as close as anyone has come to explaining why he never answered the mailing list that grew up around his own design. On the WaterTribe racecourse, where he has raced under his own first name since his first start in 2003, other competitors call him "the Wizard," a nickname attested in the builders' archive no earlier than 2006. Nobody recorded who used it first. By 2012 it had become a standing joke on the course itself: one racer, weighing whether to press on through weather that could be "35 deg and raining or 80 deg and sun," wrote to the list that "there is a standard mantra WWWD among watertribers. What would Wizard Do?" Yrvind, interviewed by the Furled Sails podcast in 2007, sums him up more simply than any of the others: Matt is such a modest man that he never speaks up for himself. At WaterTribe finish lines Yrvind noticed a habit rather than a title: "he was always, just about always, the first guy thigh-deep in the water pulling somebody's boat in as the people came in. You know, he was always right there helping people." A sailor who met neither Layden nor his legend firsthand wrote in Small Craft Advisor in 2023 about his own attempt at the Everglades Challenge. He put the reputation in a single, half-admiring line: "Sure, legends like Matt Layden can do the Challenge in tiny boats, but Matt is of a different character than normal humans: he can't be used as an example." No source anywhere in the record gives his birth year or his birthplace. Even the fullest biography ever written of him never once states his age. Cameron's own profile, written after years of running into him at the same small-boat gatherings, settles in the end for a single verdict: "At worse, Matt Layden is an eccentric; at best he is a genius. He is unconstrained by conventional wisdom."
