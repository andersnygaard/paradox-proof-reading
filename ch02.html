Four boats before Paradox

Dave Bolduc had not been to the small-boat gathering on Atsena Otie Key, across the channel from Cedar Key, Florida, in twelve years, and the ten-hour drive down from North Carolina was worth it the first weekend of May 2019, when Matt Layden stood up after dinner and told a packed crowd the story of his own boats. Bolduc wrote up what he heard. Layden opened with a photograph of himself as a boy, sailing his father's keelboat, a boat whose faults made her slow in club races and whose every attempted fix had only made her more complicated. His own first cruising boat, a Zip-class keelboat that drew two feet, taught him much the same lesson from the other side of the tiller: she, too, fell short of what he actually wanted from a boat. So, as he told the room that evening, he designed one of his own.

Bolduc brought no boat of the couple's own that year. With Mindy staying home for work, towing a microcruiser alone had not seemed worth it, so a folding dinghy carried him the half mile to the beach where everyone gathered.

Two convictions ran through Layden's talk that night, by Bolduc's account of it. The first was that the sailboat is one of mankind's most nearly perfect inventions, because it turns wind into motion with almost no moving parts at all. The second was that a boat should be built for one job and no other; his own job, as he explained it to the room, was a simple vessel that could sail in shallow water and duck under the many low bridges of the East Coast, which is why his masts come down fast and his rigs ask so little of a sailor's hands.

Terrapin

Terrapin was Layden's first home-built microcruiser: a catboat sixteen feet long with a six-foot beam, a fully battened mainsail and toed-in leeboards for lateral resistance. A reader of Messing About in Boats met her under sail as early as the winter of 1983 and 1984. Irving C. Sheldon, cruising the Herreshoff cat ketch Wings, logged two encounters with "the little Terrapin of New London, CT." The first, on 29 November 1983 in Florida's Indian River, found her "about the size of a Beetle cat but with leeboards and a fully battened sail. It could be completely closed in and oars could be stuck out the sides for auxiliary power. The helmsman sat aft with his head out a hatch." The second, on 23 April 1984 off Hilton Head, found young Layden homeward bound after a winter around Key West, in a boat that "can lie in little holes with only a few inches of water" and that "can lower his mast and pass under low bridges without much effort." Sheldon's letter is the earliest firsthand sighting of any Layden boat on record.

The Bolducs, who cruised the same seaboard for years before they ever owned one of Layden's boats themselves, describe Terrapin as a boat that "worked reasonably well." Layden's own telling, at Cedar Key in 2019, agreed with Sheldon's on every point that mattered on the water: she drew less than a foot, was fast and weatherly, and her mast lowered easily on a tabernacle, "a definite improvement" over the Zip that had come before her.

Her shortcomings, discovered the hard way, set the direction of every boat that followed her. The leeboards were cumbersome and noisy, and caught on lobster-pot lines and weed working her way up the coast toward Maine. Worse, her six-foot beam left her without enough margin to right herself after a severe knockdown in a storm off that same coast: "The boat went to 90 degrees and stayed there, supported only by her buoyant mast." Only the mast's own buoyancy kept her from going all the way over. When the wind eased, Layden righted her by hand, but the experience, as the Bolducs put it, "started him thinking about a better and more seaworthy design." He also decided that marine-grade plywood had not been worth its cost at the prices of the day.

Swamp Thing

Terrapin's failures became Swamp Thing's design brief. She was built in 1985 or 1986, thirteen feet two inches long and three feet eight inches on the beam, a third narrower than Terrapin and a boat Layden could build, launch and sail entirely alone. In place of leeboards she carried the first of his chine runners: tapered external fins fitted along the chine, the line where a flat-bottomed hull's sides meet her bottom, giving lateral resistance without anything that could catch on a lobster pot or a mat of weed the way the leeboards had. Her builders called her "one of his prettiest designs," though the accommodation for a single sailor aboard her was, in their words, "quite spartan."

Layden later traced the idea, in his 2009 interview with Small Craft Advisor, to a remark of Phil Bolger's about chine battens on two of his sharpies, and to 1970s articles on lifting-body aircraft. He showed the same audience pictures of the lifting-body aircraft that had started that thinking at the Cedar Key dinner in 2019, and Ida Little, a small-boat cruiser and writer who had photographed him sailing Swamp Thing in the Abacos for her own book, Beachcruising and Coastal Camping, sat in the crowd while he did it.

Chine runners and a simpler lug sail, rolled by hand around the boom rather than battened and sheeted like Terrapin's complicated main, let Swamp Thing do more with less. Oar ports in her sides let her be rowed from inside the cabin, much as on Phil Bolger's Birdwatcher, and Layden sailed her engineless from his home in Connecticut, down the Intracoastal Waterway and out to the Bahamas, by one account there and back again. The only real trouble on that whole voyage came one rough night in the middle of the Gulf Stream, when Layden stood on deck signalling a passing freighter with a flashlight. The ship passed too close, and her wake hit seas already confused to ten feet, knocking the little boat onto her side. Layden grabbed a chine runner for support, which only pulled her the rest of the way over and on top of him. Flooded, she still rolled upright in the warm water; he climbed back aboard, bailed her out and sailed on to the Bahamas, losing nothing worse than a few loose items in the capsize. Had he been inside the cabin, where the boat was designed to be sailed, the Bolducs reckon he would have come to no harm at all.

Swamp Thing passed out of Layden's hands. Years later she came up for sale for six hundred dollars, and Dave Bolduc, who would otherwise have bought her himself, urged his friend Dave Gatan to take her instead. Gatan was an engineless liveaboard who got everywhere in the Florida Keys by oar and scull. The Bolducs rescued her in 2004, ahead of that season's Hurricanes Frances and Ivan, and spent four years rebuilding her bottom, her rudder and the rot in her transom and stem. Chine runners earned her two different kinds of praise from the same source, which the Bolducs are careful to keep apart. She is "the most maneuverable of Matt's designs," tacking on a dime. On the separate question of pure windward speed, Dave Bolduc rates her the best-sailing boat Layden ever drew, comparing her narrow transom to an Albert Strange canoe yawl and saying she goes to windward "like a witch" and tacks "like a dream."

GJAC

After Swamp Thing came GJAC, built around 1987, a slight enlargement of Swamp Thing at fourteen feet ten inches long, four feet on the beam and drawing nine inches, keeping her chine runners and her boom-furled lug sail. Layden cruised her for about a year, long enough, in the Bolducs' words, to have "learned as much as he could" from her, before moving on in 1988 to build Little Cruiser. The Bolducs' website carries three photographs of her. Dave Bolduc separately posted a photograph of her rudder, half immersed among several other Layden rudders he had lined up for comparison.

A for-sale listing from June 2002 fills out her later history: a Connecticut owner offering her with an anchor, chain, lines, a stove, fenders and a galvanized trailer fitted with new wheels. The listing's own pictures show leeboards fitted to her hull, which might suggest Layden had quietly gone back on his own judgment about leeboards after all. He had not. The leeboards were fitted by the new owner himself, for easy daysailing, and the Bolducs took care to note as much on their own website so that nobody would mistake a later owner's choice for the designer's own change of mind. The same owner reasoned that pulling the leeboards off again and carrying a good deal more ballast instead would let GJAC's own chine runners do the whole job of holding her to windward, without any board at all, which is as near as the record comes to an experiment nobody actually ran.

Little Cruiser

Little Cruiser, built in 1988, was Layden's one real departure from the family's usual answer to leeway. Rather than chine runners, she carried a centerboard mounted all the way forward in the bow, a choice made to keep the cabin clear of a trunk running through its middle and to give a folding dinghy somewhere solid to be lashed against. Layden's own name for her was "SOST," for Sun of Swamp Thing; it was the Bolducs, who bought her in 1992, who gave her the name she has kept ever since.

At fifteen feet two inches she is two feet longer than Swamp Thing, and considerably more complex besides: a battened balanced lug with slab reefing, a deck-stepped mast that folds down on an unusual "A"-frame tabernacle, and four built-in water-ballast tanks good for thirty gallons between them. Up to two inches of foam insulation lined her hull, for dryness and for positive buoyancy if she ever flooded, and her bottom, a full inch of plywood, did double duty as ballast and as armour against hard groundings. Layden lived aboard her for four years, sailing as far north as the Bay of Fundy and as far south as the Bahamas, and found her warm enough one winter to freeze her into a saltwater pond on Cape Cod and simply stay put. The Bolducs did not learn of that winter until years afterward, stripping her paint ahead of a refit and finding bow-wave-shaped gouges cut into her bow where the ice had marked her, proof that Layden had eventually sailed her back out through the ice rather than wait for a thaw.

Her hardest night under Layden's own hand came at anchor, not under way. Riding out a late-season hurricane in the Exumas with winds over a hundred knots, Little Cruiser was driven off her mooring and blown back into the mangroves; Layden simply pushed her out again into deep water with his own hands, while other boats in the same anchorage were driven aground and stayed there until somebody came to salvage them. The Bolducs took the opposite lesson from the same storm once they owned her: better to leave any boat, however tough, in the most secure place available and go ashore themselves, a resolve only confirmed when they later cruised past what Hurricane Andrew had done to Homestead, Cat Cay and Chub Cay.

Her sail has had three lives, and the middle one is a small story of its own. Layden's original, home-sewn in 1988, had worn out by the mid-1990s and was replaced by a well-known New England sailmaker's work, a sail of "fancy" dacron that cost $700 in 1994 and turned out to be cut too flat; the boat was measurably slower for it. Dave Bolduc raised the problem with Layden himself and with Mark Balogh, a professional sailmaker and small-boat designer in his own right, and the two of them between them talked him into sewing a replacement by hand. He took careful measurements off the faded original, read what he could find on the subject, bought a cheap secondhand commercial machine to do the work on and produced a sail in 1998 that was not perfect but carried the boat as swiftly as before and corrected whatever mistakes it had as he found them. His own verdict afterward was a caution rather than a boast: sewing a sail is not for everyone, and most builders are better served finding a sailmaker who understands what a heavy-displacement microcruiser actually needs.

The Bolducs had first seen her as two black-and-white photographs in a magazine, in February 1991, her owner captioned only as a Connecticut sailor working his way up the coast to Cape Cod for the winter. Intrigued, they drove thirteen hundred miles round trip to meet him and kept up a year of letters afterward. In 1992 they drove north again and came home with the boat itself for $1,600. What they got then sailed seven more winters to the Bahamas under their own hands, eight counting Layden's own crossing before them, more than ten thousand miles in all. Her toughness held through a reef strike in 1998 that gashed her bottom eight inches long and three eighths of an inch deep, a wound a boat with no keel can simply be beached and mended rather than hauled out for. It held through a gale that built from fifteen knots to at least forty overnight on the seventy-five mile run from Chub Cay to Bimini in 1993. Dave Bolduc grew too seasick to hold the helm. Mindy Bolduc took it and brought them both through, holding hull speed and surfing in breaking seas for the whole seventeen-hour crossing. Stripped to bare wood and fully sheathed in fiberglass in 2005 to cut her yearly maintenance, she came back into service the following year when Mindy wrote up a three-week, two-hundred-and-seventy-five-mile cruise of her own through the Outer Banks, under her own byline. After twelve years of that kind of use, the Bolducs still called her "one of the best boats for the job," cheap to keep and light enough to tow behind a small four-cylinder car.

Her one departure from the family earned no such praise from them. The centerboard, in the Bolducs' words, "did not seem to improve windward performance sufficiently to merit its construction."

Dave Bolduc, who had by then sailed across Layden's family of cruising hulls, ranked them against each other in 2015: for holding a straight course hands off, Little Cruiser needed far more constant attention than her sisters, with Paradox winning that contest hands down, but for tacking quickly the order nearly reversed, and Little Cruiser came second only to Swamp Thing herself. For simple cabin comfort he tied her with Paradox at the top of the family, her foam insulation and generous stowage set against the more cramped, shallower cabins of Layden's smaller chine-runner boats.
