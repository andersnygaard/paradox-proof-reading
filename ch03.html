Chapter 3

Paradox

Layden built Paradox in about four months of full-time work, by the fullest account the Bolducs have given, working in the Connecticut woods in 1993 at a cost of around $2,000 in that year's materials. He had no car, so the plywood and everything else needed came to the building site load by load, on his own bicycle trailer. He briefly considered running carbon-fibre tow inside the mast's staves to save weight, but built a plain wood mast instead for the sake of simplicity, the same preference that runs through every other choice in the boat. When she was finished, he launched her by skidding her down a steep ravine to the Salmon River below the site, a method that prompted one builder's own dry comment years afterward: no wonder these little boats have to be built tough.

Paradox was Layden's fifth home-built boat, and in her the lessons of three earlier boats came together: Swamp Thing's chine runners and simple rig, Little Cruiser's thinking on interior room and on lines run down into the cabin where a sailor could reach every one of them, and Terrapin's hard proof that a narrow, self-righting hull mattered more in the end than a wide, roomy one. She was thirteen feet ten inches long and four feet wide, with no centerboard at all and, by the Bolducs' own count, only three running lines on the whole boat: one to raise the sail, one to reef it and one to sheet it. Little Cruiser, by comparison, carried twice as many.

She went back to the simpler boom-furled lug sail of Swamp Thing and GJAC rather than Little Cruiser's battened, slab-reefed one, but improved it with a drum so the sail could be reefed from inside the cabin, and where her mast stepped through the deck, as on his earlier boats, he built in a small self-draining well for it to sit in rather than leave the step exposed to the weather. The five things the Bolducs singled out as Paradox's own particular character, taken together, are chine runners, seventy litres of fresh-water ballast, a self-righting hull, positive buoyancy and every line led back into the cabin where a sailor need never leave shelter to work one.

Across the Gulf Stream

Her first season took her down the Intracoastal Waterway and as far as the Ragged Islands in the Bahamas. Her second, the following January, is the one told firsthand, in the Bolducs' own words, in the article that introduced Paradox to the world as "Cheating Winter." The Bolducs trailered Little Cruiser from Connecticut to Key Largo in a single day that winter (sixty-five knots to windward by road, the article jokes, now that's fast), while Layden had already spent six weeks sailing Paradox down the same distance by water. They spent a day in Key Largo rigging both boats and admiring "Matt's fifth home-built sailboat" before the two set out together the next evening, bound across the Gulf Stream for Bimini on a forecast of favourable south winds.

The forecast did not entirely hold. Out in seas running five to seven feet, the Bolducs followed Paradox from a hundred yards back and watched her, lit under her own masthead light, bob up and down "like flotsam in the confused seas," looking, in their words, more like a child's toy than a real boat. By daybreak they were becalmed twenty-five miles out, only halfway across, the wind having come from dead ahead rather than abeam as forecast. Exhausted and seasick, Dave and Mindy motored the rest of the way; Layden, "always the sailing purist," refused their offer of a tow and chose to sail or scull instead, making Bimini a full day after they did. From there the two boats cruised on together through the Berry Islands and Chub Cay and across the New Providence Channel to Nassau, where a self-appointed dockmaster at Potter's Cay collected a dollar from them to watch a boat nobody had asked him to watch. The fuller cruise that followed took in the Exumas, Long Island, Conception Island, Rum Cay, Spanish Wells and Eleuthera.

At a potluck at Warderick Wells that same cruise, Mindy and Layden baked two loaves of bread together on Paradox's tiny kerosene stove. The three of them, Dave, Mindy and Layden, often ate together aboard her too, cooking instead on the roomier Little Cruiser alongside, her cabin holding "a few close friends" for a short stint, in Dave's words, in a space he likened to sitting in a tent.

The plans that let other builders follow her did not exist yet when any of this happened. Drawing number one, dated July 1994, was drafted a full year after Paradox was built, by which time she had already proven herself for a whole season and more; the boat came first, and the plan set that reached the Bolducs, and through them every builder since, was drawn from a prototype that had already crossed to the Bahamas and back. "Cheating Winter" itself did not reach print for another year after that: it ran as the cover feature of Messing About in Boats on 15 July 1995, the first time Paradox had been shown to a readership beyond the Bolducs' own small circle.

The first builder

Dave and Mindy Bolduc had begun selling the plans by hand from their own address, and Don Elliott, who had never seen a Paradox afloat, ordered a set in 1996 and began to build. Don Elliott, of Tomah, Wisconsin, was the first builder with no personal tie to Layden at all. There was no mailing list yet to join, no forum to search and no other builder to write to, so he worked from the drawings and his own judgment alone, and when he finished he put what he had learned into a magazine series so the next builder would not have to start from nothing.

Messing About in Boats carried his "Building Paradox" series in sixteen instalments from 15 September 1996 to 1 May 1997, written as he built his own boat, The Edge, at his home in Tomah. Builders who found the series decades later still called it essential, close to a second set of plans, and remembered one claim from it in particular, still quoted today: "Think what you often get when you buy a set of plans: An empty shell of the hull, a mast, that's it... Not with Paradox, it's all there!" He sold it afterward as a standalone booklet, about fifty pages for eight dollars when the series ended in 1997, and promised to ship it the same day the payment arrived, a promise builders who ordered it found he kept.

Elliott held builders to the plans as written, bronze ring nails and all, and urged every builder after him to do the same. The Edge was one of only two Paradoxes known to be sailing anywhere by the summer of 2001, Layden's own prototype being the other.

He had a next boat of his own in mind too, a smaller design he called Illusion, left on paper and unbuilt when he died in 2008. His widow Wanda kept the booklet for sale by hand for years afterward, and their son Josh eventually built her a website that could take an order and send the files without her.

Ten years near home

Ten more years of cruising near home followed, with the chine runners timed twice on the Indian River in October 2001, a new, higher-peaked sail cut early in 2003 and, that same spring, an outright win in the Everglades Challenge. Those were also the years of his marriage and of the day job in megayacht design that began in 1995, which he described self-deprecatingly as being one small cog in a slow-turning wheel. He and Karen made their home at Jensen Beach, on Florida's east coast.

The Bolducs' own verdict, set down on their own site, was that Paradox had made something like three thousand miles "with no mishaps." She was probably a touch more seaworthy even than the much higher-mileage Little Cruiser, though they judged both of them coastal cruisers rather than boats built for the open ocean.
