Chapter 4

Sven Yrvind

Sven Yrvind was fifty-eight years old in 1997, and had already spent three decades making ocean passages in boats most sailors would call too small for the purpose. The habit of distrusting deep water started small: he had to wade ashore from a thirteen-foot boat anchored off Denmark, with no dinghy aboard and the water reaching his thighs, because her draft was seventy-five centimetres; the doubt about deep draft never quite left him. He had already crossed the Atlantic several times before 1974, the year he made his first attempt at rounding Cape Horn in a twenty-foot boat he had built in his mother's basement; he was capsized and, a week later, pitchpoled in the Southern Ocean. He turned east and ran before the westerlies for two months until he reached Tristan da Cunha, the most isolated inhabited island in the world. By his own account his boat Bris was the first boat ever from the outside world to land there; he stayed four months, teaching the islanders mathematics in exchange for food.

He went back to Sweden, built a nineteen-foot aluminium boat for a second attempt that succeeded, then cruised the wind-scoured Falkland Islands afterward, where gusts reached a hundred knots on three occasions during a four-month stay. Only there did he realise that in designing a boat so single-mindedly for deep, stormy water he had forgotten the value of a shoal draft that could simply take the ground. The same conviction found its fullest confirmation in 1997 itself, in a Japanese fishing village where he had gone to hitchhike and learn boats at first hand. There he learned to work a yuloh, the sculling oar the Japanese call ro, and came to prize the strong, flat-bottomed hulls of the local boats: shallow draft, a hull that could ground without damage and a lot of floor space inside for the length. By the time two letters reached him from America, he was already primed to take a flat-bottomed, shoal-draft design seriously.

He did not yet know that the boat's designer had read his own name for years, in the sailing magazine Layden's uncle gave him as a boy. Then came the two letters, mailed from America to Sweden, one from Dave Bolduc and one from a friend Yrvind calls Mickie, each enclosing drawings of a flat-bottomed, fourteen-foot coastal cruiser named Paradox. At first glance the boat looked impossible to sail to windward: a strong flat bottom, a draft of nine inches and nothing underwater that resembled a keel or a centreboard. "A closer look reveals her chine runners," Yrvind wrote afterwards, "small two-inch wide horizontal winglets sticking out from the chines for about a third of the boat's length. But was that enough?" He phoned Bolduc, who had sailed a winter in the Bahamas with Layden and confirmed the runners worked well, and got Layden's number from him. Layden told him he could out-point Bolduc's own slightly bigger centreboard boat, and invited Yrvind to come and see for himself.

Yrvind flew down to Layden's home on Florida's east coast that October and sailed Paradox for a month as a guest of Layden and his wife, whom Yrvind calls Kari. He had come to test the one thing he doubted, so when Layden asked where they should sail, he answered: to windward. Layden let him use the boat whenever he liked. "It was a rare opportunity to learn something special," Yrvind wrote. "I took full advantage of it and was out sailing everyday, in the weekends Matt came along." He went to windward in different strengths of wind for a month and found Paradox "certainly sailed as close to the wind as my boats, probably much better."

Three visits, weekly calls and a boat drawn together

One month in Florida became a friendship that outlasted it by decades. Yrvind came back in 2003, 2006 and 2007, about a month each time, sailing and talking boats with Layden by day; in the years between visits the two traded ideas once a week by telephone, Layden, in Yrvind's own description, "always one step ahead of me."

The days themselves were companionable rather than ceremonial, and Yrvind noticed details a written account would otherwise leave out. Sailing Layden's next small cruiser, Enigma, nearly every day on his later stays, he found her cabin, just over a yard deep and ten feet long, "plenty of space for two people," himself and Layden both being, in his own words, "not the biggest people in the world." He never did sail Layden's smallest design, Sand Flea: Florida, he said, was "far too hot for me."

The exchange ran the other way too. Layden drew the lines of a sixteen-foot cat schooner (4.9 metres), a rig of two short, unstayed masts each flying its own lug sail, for Yrvind's own next boat in 2007, a design Yrvind published the following year under a name he called, in his own words, "embarrassingly" plain: Yrvind. She carried Layden's chine runners and lifting-body hull and Layden's own reefing, furling lug rig, mounted on Swedish carbon-fibre spars; Yrvind added a forward daggerboard (a straight blade dropped through the bottom, rather than a pivoting centreboard), a large rudder and his own accommodation plan and movable ballast. Yrvind meant to sail her non-stop from Bodø, north of the Arctic Circle in Norway, around Cape Horn to Valdivia in Chile, a passage of more than twelve thousand nautical miles he expected to take six months or more; the windward passage around the Horn, he wrote, would be proof enough of her weatherliness on its own. "She is a mongrel," he wrote of the finished design, "based on Matt's and my ideas." He closed his published account of her by thanking Layden "for taking time to engage himself in my problems and doing the drawings and artwork." Ten drawings survive from that commission, dimensioned hand sheets dated 2007 and initialled ML, rudder layout, daggerboard, chine runner and bulkhead dimensions among them, plus two signed perspective sketches, one inscribed "16' Voyaging Sailboat for Sven Yrvind, Matt Layden 2007." They are the fullest set of Layden's own drawings known to survive.

Layden's hand turns up once more in Yrvind's world, this time on a page rather than in a boat. Yrvind published a Swedish-language book on his own design work, KONSTRUKTOREN, and its one piece of outside artwork, a drawing of his current project, was Layden's; the book's other illustrations were credited to the Swedish artist Martin Mörck.

The verdict in print

Yrvind's considered judgment of the chine runner went into print in October 2008, in the Amateur Yacht Research Society's journal Catalyst, in a piece called "A Small Voyaging Boat," written as his entry for the society's design prize that year. It was a verdict he had already been giving in conversation for years and would go on repeating for almost two decades more, essentially unchanged: "I am now convinced that the chine runner concept, created by Matt in 1982, is the greatest innovation for small cruising boats in the last century." The year is Yrvind's own dating; no earlier or independent account fixes it.

Few people had noticed, he added, because Layden does not try to make his voice heard above the din of a self-promoting age, and because the idea is subtler than it looks and has drawn little research. Exactly how the runners work is a question on which the two men do not fully agree; what Yrvind praised without reservation here was the system built around them. He explained how a boat with no ballast keel could still stand up to nearly 165 degrees of heel: her flat bottom shifted buoyancy to leeward as she heeled, not unlike a multihull, and her height against her beam, with her own cabin top's buoyancy, did the rest at the larger angles. He singled out Layden's furling gear too, the boom that rolls the sail away from inside the cabin at the pull of a line, as "an amazingly quick and comfortable way of varying the sail area." And he pointed to the race Layden's boat had already won five years before as evidence "that Matt's boats work."

Carrying the idea home

Yrvind did not only admire the chine runner. He fitted it to his own boats and sailed them across oceans, which is the clearest evidence anywhere in the record that the idea scales beyond a fourteen-foot coastal cruiser.

The first attempt taught him the idea's lower limit. Yrvind and Layden began designing a boat of 5.4 metres together in 2003, and while it took shape Yrvind built himself something smaller to sail in the meantime, a trial boat of 4.1 metres. She proved too small to live aboard in comfort, let alone to carry him around Cape Horn as he had once hoped. "I found my limit on the small side," he said, and gave her up.

The boat that answered the question from the other direction was bigger: a chine-runner hull he called Yrvind.com, named so that passing ships picking her up on their instruments might look up her website and report him safe. Her departure from Kinsale, on the south coast of Ireland, came with odd timing: the Fastnet Race, with several hundred boats, was due to start from the same waters within days, and Yrvind meant to be gone before that fleet caught him up. The forecast was poor, he reasoned, and only going to worsen from there, so there was no sense in waiting for a better one; he expected to average something like two and a half knots and reckoned the first leg, to Madeira, would take three to four weeks on its own. He left in August 2011 with no self-steering gear worked out and no radio beyond a satellite tracker, so the only way to rest at sea was to heave to and wait. Towed back to port once in the Stockholm archipelago before the Atlantic crossing, she had broached sharply the instant the line was let go, the one failure he had feared under sail and, in the end, never actually suffered. He had hoped, once he reached the West Indies, to sail on to Florida and talk with Layden about a boat a metre longer again, built to stand up better to the seas around Cape Horn. The voyage he did complete ran by way of Madeira to Martinique, two and a half months alone on the Atlantic, and it showed him the chine-runner system's limit from the opposite side. "Strangely, the biggest problem was that she had too much stability, she did not heel enough," he wrote afterwards. "That gave her lee helm [a tendency to turn away from the wind rather than into it] and the chinerunners did not dig in enough." The runners, he had learned firsthand, need a hull willing to lean into them.

Years later Yrvind set out the general principle in writing, in an email to Anders Nygaard: "If you have chinerunner instead of centerboard the center of lateral resistance is not so deep into the water. Hence the distance between the center of pressure on the sails and the center of lateral resistance is shorter. This means the healing force is less. It means that a boat with a chinerunner do need a bit of less stability. It is an interesting fact that not everyone understand."

Yrvind went on fitting the idea to a later boat of his own, a composite hull he called YRVIND ½, setting her runners nearly vertical and weighting them with their own ballast to compensate for the lift standing them on edge cost her. By then the idea had already found its way into one more hull: Kjell Karlsson, a Swedish acquaintance of Yrvind's since the 1970s, built a Paradox in Yrvind's own foam-and-glass method rather than Layden's plywood.

Where the two friends part

On one point, though, the two designers part company. Yrvind's own boats are built for the open ocean: a foam-sandwich hull thick enough to survive a direct hit from a drifting shipping container, double ends and a shape deliberately made unstable upside down, so a capsize rights herself quickly rather than settling there. The hulls themselves flex on purpose, built to bounce off a drifting log or a half-sunk container rather than crack against it; Paradox's plywood, by contrast, only has to be strong enough for the coastal sailing Layden built her for, and is. For that kind of sailing he has come to prefer a centreboard or leeboard over the chine runner, since it gives him one more way to control the boat running downwind in a big sea, at the cost of one more moving part to fail. He drew the boundary himself, in the same plain terms he uses for everything: "Coastal sailing like Matt does, chinerunners is fine."

Glen Maxwell, the Florida boatbuilder who founded the builders' mailing list and built his own Paradox, Zoë, with thirty-six years and over 30,000 miles of blue-water sailing behind him, took the opposite view: he said he would happily take Paradox a long way offshore himself, and felt safer sitting inside her than standing on the deck of a bigger boat. Yrvind's own boats have crossed oceans several times over; Paradox was drawn for the coast. That is why his verdict on the chine runner carries the weight it does: he is not arguing for an idea of his own, he is a sailor who tests other people's claims at sea, in his own boats, before he puts his name to them.

A new boat at eighty-seven

By March 2026, a new boat meant to cross the Atlantic once more was taking shape: 5.2 metres long and 1.4 metres wide, a Divinycell foam sandwich built for three masts and seven and a half square metres of sail, with a double keel hung beneath the hull. He meant to test her off Sweden's Baltic coast, then trailer her to Ireland and sail from there to Madeira, the route he had used before. From Madeira he meant to go on to Beaufort, South Carolina, more than three thousand one hundred nautical miles further west.

By September the boat was finished, and too heavy: eight hundred and fifty kilograms ready for sea against the six hundred she had been designed for. In a video posted to his own YouTube channel that month, Yrvind announced a change of plan. He would build instead a boat with a square midsection, "very close to Paradox," 4.6 metres long and 1.4 metres wide, forty centimetres longer and eighteen centimetres beamier than Paradox herself, about a quarter more displacement, worked up from drawings he and Layden had made together back in 2007. He was eighty-seven.
