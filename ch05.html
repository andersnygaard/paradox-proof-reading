Chapter 5

The 2003 Everglades Challenge

Matt has proven that his unusual concept works and very well at that.

Don Elliott, "A Story, a True One," reproduced in Bill's Log (Bill Serjeant), 12 September 2014

The beach at Fort De Soto

The Everglades Challenge of 2003 started at Fort De Soto, a county park on a sandy point south of St. Petersburg where Tampa Bay opens into the Gulf of Mexico. Its boat ramp held thirty entrants on the morning of 8 March 2003: sea kayaks and decked canoes built light for paddling day and night without rest, one catamaran and a tacking proa built onto a Hobie 18 hull. Among them sat Paradox, fourteen feet of plywood box, decked over, her cabin hatch closed, looking less like a racing entry than something somebody had forgotten to finish building.

WaterTribe put the rules of the Everglades Challenge in its own words, on the results page itself: "This Challenge is an expedition style adventure race for small boats. The time limit is 8 days but the winners will do it in 3 to 4 days. The distance is 260 to 300 miles depending on your course." The course ran down Florida's Gulf coast from Fort De Soto to Key Largo, and every boat had to be self-sufficient between checkpoints: no motors, no support crew, no resupply beyond what a competitor could find along the way. WaterTribe sorted every entry by hull and crew: Class 1 and 2 for paddled kayaks and canoes, Class 3 for paddled craft that also carried a small sail, Class 4 for monohull sailboats and Class 5 for multihulls. Most of the thirty-boat fleet fell into the first two classes; only seven boats, Layden's among them, carried a sail large enough to matter. The course itself threads some three hundred miles of barrier islands, mangrove bays and open Gulf water. It runs from the resort towns north of Tampa Bay down through the Ten Thousand Islands to the Everglades' own tangled coast, and it offered every sailor a standing choice between two routes: an inside passage sheltered behind the barrier islands, and the open Gulf outside them. Layden's log tracks him switching between the two as the wind allowed, outside when there was wind worth using, inside when there was not.

Entering at all was already a departure. For about ten years Paradox had done nothing more adventurous than cruise the coast near Layden's own home; this was the first time she, or he, had lined up against anyone.

Layden had registered her as "Paradox 14' coastal cruising sailboat, with chinese yuloh secondary propulsion," a "heavy, long-range expedition cruiser" that, in his own words, "stretches the limits of beach-launchability." The same entry form carried the line that would mark her ever after, about a boat that is not fast but can keep sailing in any weather; here it was the beach-launchability that mattered, and Layden meant it literally. WaterTribe's own rules allowed no shortcut around the problem: every boat had to launch from above the high-water mark without outside help, and Paradox, drained or not, was no light dinghy to carry down a beach alone.

Glen Maxwell had sailed his own Paradox, Zoë, down to Fort De Soto to see Layden off before the race, and to learn what he could about his own sail by watching Layden handle his. Maxwell was a professional boatbuilder from Live Oak, Florida, who had built charter catamarans for Gold Coast Yachts and already carried thirty-six years of sailing and over 30,000 blue-water miles behind him. He had founded the builders' mailing list on 7 July 2001 and launched Zoë that November, one of the first boats finished under the design's new life as a mailing-list project. He and Layden sailed together off Fort De Soto on the Thursday and Friday before the Saturday start, Maxwell in his own boat: the first time two Paradoxes had ever sailed in company, and a first for Layden too, who had sailed alone for a decade until Maxwell came alongside.

Layden solved the beach-launching problem with two sets of rollers, each a short cylinder of wood about six inches long and two inches across, turning on an axle fitted into a pair of trestles built from ordinary two-by-two and two-by-four stock. The whole rig stowed in the boat's own bilge between races, about thirty inches long and a foot or so wide. Each roller trailed its own line so the surf would not carry it off before he could fetch it back up the beach. He worked a block and tackle alongside them, a four-part and a six-part pulley tied together for the extra purchase, walking each roller from the stern to the bow in turn as the boat's weight came onto the one in front and the one behind went free. "Should be fun to watch," he wrote on his entry form, and by Maxwell's account it was: he watched Layden move the whole boat alone from the waterline to dry sand without another pair of hands anywhere in sight. He sketched the whole arrangement from memory for a builder back in England who wanted to know exactly how it worked, and had only half joked that the detail might need extracting under hypnosis.

"Man can he sail that boat," Maxwell wrote home afterward: Layden's windward work was "far superior" to his own. Short-tacking together with a reef in, the two men found Zoë ran a full knot slower on one tack than the other, a lug sail's familiar asymmetry that gave Maxwell plenty to think about once the weekend was over. Layden suggested changes to Maxwell's own sail, and named a sailmaker, Dabbler Sails, as one he had heard did good work on this kind of rig; Maxwell, who had not been happy with his sail's shape since it was first cut, wrote the name down to follow up once the race was behind them both. Maxwell's account of those two afternoons, written within days of the encounter, is the only time anyone recorded watching Layden actually sail Paradox. To most of the mailing list, Layden was still a name attached to a set of plans rather than a man anyone had actually met; Maxwell's letter home, for many of them, was the closest thing to meeting him at all.

Checkpoint by checkpoint

Layden's own checkpoint reports, filed to WaterTribe's tracking page and relayed to the mailing list by members following his progress online, are the fullest record of the race from inside the boat. At Venice Inlet, 41 miles out, he logged in on the first night at twenty to eleven: "Nice ene wind am, light and variable to calm pm lots of sculling-assisted kayaker off Anna Maria am. Continuing outside." He had worked the yuloh, Paradox's Chinese sculling oar, through the calm, and passed a kayaker near Anna Maria Island who was doing much the same under paddle. He later reckoned, by one account relayed on the mailing list afterward, that he had averaged about two knots sculling through the race's calmest stretches, enough, together with the boat's own momentum, to keep her moving when the paddlers around her had nothing left but their arms. The oar that made that possible was a new one, straighter and wider-bladed than the one shown in the plans, giving him finer control over each stroke at the cost of his own wrist, which he said afterward had ached through the first few days of the race.

The next morning, 67 miles out near Placida at a checkpoint the race called Grande Tours, he filed something that read strangely on a results page: "Wind light ENE veering to S by dawn. Got some sleep on the offshore back. Proceeding inside to Sanibel." Paul V., who posted the official standings to the mailing list as they came in, opened that day's dispatch with a wish rather than a report: "Matt needs some wind!" Layden was then, Paul V. went on, roughly ten hours behind the leading paddling kayaks on elapsed time, "but he reports a good night's sleep while sailing offshore! There is a long way to go." Paddlers have to land to rest. Layden had simply let the boat carry him while he did, and the mailing list, reading the standings from hundreds of miles away, had no way yet of knowing whether that would be enough.

He held the inside route past Sanibel and reached Chokoloskee, 174 miles along, early the following afternoon: "Nice wind overnight. Calmed out by morning. Napped off Naples. Easy day so far. Proceeding outside." He was, by then, the only boat to have reached that checkpoint at all. "He was the first to reach checkpoint 3!" Paul V. told the list that night. "Now headed offshore. Woohoo!" Larry Whited, a fellow competitor, needed only one sentence to size up what the news meant: "This is impressive news. I think we're talking a new paradigm for the Watertribe and adventure racing."

The ten-hour deficit of the first morning had become a lead, and the list began doing the arithmetic in public. Team Weed Warrior, the tacking proa, had reached Chokoloskee nine hours and twenty minutes behind him; Team Nootka was three hours and fifty minutes behind Weed Warrior again. "Is there a pattern developing here?" Nels Arvidson, a list member in British Columbia who had been relaying the standings, asked: by his own count the first three boats to the checkpoint were all monohull sailboats, "Class 4, single male; Class 4, double male; Class 4, double male." Nobody on the list was ready to call the race decided. "If the wind dies or is head on," Nels wrote, "Matt's alternate propulsion, chinese yuloh, is not likely to keep up with paddling Weed Warrior's outrigger canoe or Nootka's racing canoe. But Matt has built an impressive lead. Can he hold on? Stay tuned, any thing could happen."

Offshore is where the race decided itself. By the next morning, 273 miles along at Flamingo, he filed his best report of the five: "Spectacular sailing: Surfing at 6 knots with dolphins on the bow off Pavillion Key. Diminishing wind, varying to NNE overnight. Caught the tide at East Cape. Onward!" Somewhere on that outside leg he also stopped to right a capsized kayaker and tow him ashore, a detail the list learned only secondhand. "As you may have heard," Nels wrote, in the middle of an otherwise unrelated post about the standings, "he stopped and rescued an overturned kayaker and towed him into shore." Layden's own log never mentions it at all.

The gap at Flamingo settled the question the list had been asking two days before. Layden reached that checkpoint about seven and a half hours after the second-place boat had reached the one before it, and Nels allowed himself a small victory over one of the race's own competitors. Steven Isaac, who raced under the tribe name Chief, had murmured to Nels at the pre-race inspection that sailboats would be entering the Everglades Challenge "at least one more year." Nels had taken the comment for sarcasm at the time. "Chief may have to eat his words," Nels wrote now. He asked where, if the wind really favored sailors that much, the one genuine catamaran in the fleet had got to. That boat, Crazy Russian's, would not finish for another two days. By then the only real doubt left was whether anything could still go wrong, and Nels allowed that it still could: "The only thing that could hurt Matt is either a complete loss of wind or if his sailing gear has a major breakdown."

He crossed the line at Key Largo that same evening with the shortest report of the five: "Just got in - comments to follow on discussion forum a bit later tonight." The comments, when they came, were not his. A post signed "Wife/Friends/Family" reached the WaterTribe forum that night: "I received a nice collect phone call from Matt in Key Largo! He sounded tired but in good spirits. Good race Matt (you crazy sailor). Please get some sleep now." It was the only word from off the water that night, and it came from home.

Read end to end, the five checkpoint lines are the plainest thing Layden ever put in writing about any of his own voyages. There is no boasting in them, not even once the lead was clearly his, and no complaint either, not about the kayaker he stopped to help or the ten-hour deficit of the first morning. A navigational note, a weather note, once a single word of relief, "Onward!" and nothing more. It is as close as anything on record comes to hearing the man think.

For the builders on the mailing list, the race was watched rather than lived: a results page refreshed between other emails, a checkpoint report passed from one inbox to the next within the hour it was logged. Layden himself had no idea, out on the water, how closely he was being followed; the only people who knew the shape of the race while it was still being decided were several hundred miles away from it, arguing in writing about a man none of them could see. It was the first time the design had reached people who had never met Layden and never would, on the strength of a result rather than a set of plans.

The numbers

Paradox finished in 3 days, 9 hours and 51 minutes, first overall in the field of thirty and first in Class 4, WaterTribe's division for monohull sailboats. It was not close. The next boat home was WeedWarrior and Machete, 16 hours and 6 minutes later: a tacking proa that two men had built onto a Hobie 18 hull and sailed together. Glen Maxwell told the list as much when a competitor asked afterward why no Hobie-style catamarans had entered; she was, in fact, the faster of the two multihulls that raced that year. The next monohull, a single-hander racing as vanman, finished 1 day, 2 hours and 8 minutes behind Layden: more than a full day off the pace within Paradox's own class. Below the top two, the gaps closed considerably: vanman's own edge over the next monohull, Nootka and RiverSlayer, was just over ten hours, a fraction of the margin Layden had already built by the time he crossed the line. Eight of the thirty entries never finished within the eight-day limit at all, and the slowest boat that did finish took more than twice as long as Layden had taken to win.

Sixteen hours and six minutes is longer than it took the fastest paddled boat in the race, a tie between SaltyFrog and SharkChow, to cover the entire final stretch from Flamingo to the finish line. Sven Yrvind printed the same figures five years later in Catalyst, the Amateur Yacht Research Society's journal, working from WaterTribe's own results rather than from the mailing list's chatter: "Paradox won the Everglades Challenge in 2003, finishing first overall after 3 days 9 hours and 51 minutes, 16 hours and 6 minutes before the second boat. There were thirty starters." Two accounts, kept entirely separately for five years, agree to the minute. No photograph of the race has ever surfaced.

Two limits

The race was sailed in light airs. Layden's own checkpoint notes describe calm patches and following breezes for the whole three and a half days, and nothing in his log or anyone else's account of the race mentions a squall or a headwind strong enough to test the claim that she could keep sailing in anything. The win showed that she finished faster than the fleet she raced when the weather let every competitor choose when and how hard to push; it did not show what she would do in a real gale, because a real gale never came.

The result is also a sample of one: no other Paradox has entered the Everglades Challenge, or any other WaterTribe event, since 2003. Layden himself never raced that boat again; his later entries, in a sailing kayak, in Enigma, in Sand Flea and in Elusion, belonged to other designs.

So why did a fleet of serious competitors treat the result as settled the moment it happened?

Warm and dry is fast

What the fleet said about the result, in real time, answers that question better than any retrospective verdict could. Larry Whited, who sailed a 21-foot monohull, a Sea Pearl trimaran and an SP28 himself, wrote to the list the day after the finish: "Wave action really slows the light seapearl especially to windward where it is constantly thrown off the waves. The heavy paradox powers through the slop with inertia. The comfort motion of the paradox is much better on the body too." It was the argument the whole result rested on, offered by a man who owned the boats Paradox beat most decisively: weight and inertia, not sail area, had carried her through chop that slowed everyone else. A dry, steady motion let her sailor keep working instead of hanging on.

The celebration crossed an ocean within days: a member who signed himself Jeff UK wrote that if you were to write a film script based on the results of this race, no one would believe it. Whited, writing separately once the dust had settled, put the result in the plainest terms the design itself could ask for: "I am proud that Matt was so successful, and it speaks volumes for the design in terms of safety and reliability."

Nels had made the underlying case, in fuller terms, while the race was still being decided rather than after. Most of the fleet, he wrote, were "racing kayaks and canoes, with small auxiluary sails added. They are narrow, long, open and not very dry or seaworthy. You have to land and set up camp in order to eat and rest, whereas Matt just continues to sail snug as a bug, and perhaps grabbing a few zee's. He has a huge advantage crossing open bays, where the waves might swamp the competition." A boat that never has to stop, in other words, beats boats that do, whatever each one's rating says it should do on paper.

A full day

Layden went on racing for fifteen years afterward, in other boats of his own design, but never again in Paradox herself. The win's longest afterlife, though, had nothing to do with racing at all.

Don Elliott, The Edge's builder, died in 2008. His widow Wanda kept answering plans inquiries in his name for years afterward. The standard reply she sent out, word for word the one Don had written, still closed with the same line in an order reply from August 2013, five years after his death: "In 2003 Paradox won a 300 mile race against all comers, by a full day!! Cheers, Don Elliott." Sixteen hours and six minutes had become, in the telling, a full day; the legend had outgrown the clock within the first retelling and never shrunk back. What Elliott's widow was really selling, a decade on, was not the eighty-odd pages of building instructions on offer but the ending to a story. Whoever had first written the line down had known enough to keep it exactly as it was, down to the two exclamation marks.
