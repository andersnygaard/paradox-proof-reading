Chapter 6

Smaller and smaller

"On the theory that the less boat you have to drag around the faster you can go, I'm continuing my trend of several years in bringing out a shorter boat than last year's." Layden wrote that on 23 February 2007, announcing Sand Flea, his seventh boat and the smallest he had built since Paradox herself. Each boat had followed the same method: build her, live with her, test her and race her, learn what she cannot do, then build the next one smaller. Bent now toward speed instead of cruising, that formula produced four boats over the following years, each lighter and shorter than the last.

Enigma and the Enigma 460

The first of the new boats, built in 2005 and formally named Enigma 360, was a deliberate step down in both size and weight: eleven feet ten inches long, forty-three inches on the beam, built light in stitch-and-glue plywood. Her panels were wired together edge to edge and locked with fiberglass tape rather than framed up on sawn chine logs the way Paradox was. She combined Little Cruiser's hull proportions with Paradox's own furling rig, a pair of chine runners made shorter and wider than Paradox's own and a fold-down soft dodger copied in an improved form from the one by then fitted to Swamp Thing. Builders following her construction that year, on photographs posted to Layden's brother's website, knew her informally as "Mini Paradox," before the Enigma name settled on her. She was the boat his wife Karen sailed with him two-up that June, impressing Glen Maxwell enough to write home about it. Empty and without ballast she weighed only 180 pounds, light enough to be hefted, with help, onto the roof of a car.

Enigma proved herself at once. Her maiden voyage, in March 2006, was the twelve-hundred-mile WaterTribe Ultimate Florida Challenge, a circumnavigation of the state that year that included a forty-mile overland portage between St. George and the Suwannee River. Layden pulled her the entire distance himself in a shoulder harness, using the chine runners' own wings as mounting points for a pair of cart wheels built into the hull. Reading a winter frontal system's own cycle, north then east then west then north again, he is reckoned to have driven hard for the St. Mary's River while a southerly wind still held, reaching it just before it backed around; the boats behind him were left beating into headwinds and paddling twelve-hour days for miserable mileage. Enigma finished first in her class and third overall, breaking Layden's own 2003 Everglades Challenge record along the opening leg of the same course, and one onlooker's blunt summary of the result has stood ever since: she was the only sailboat to complete the Ultimate Challenge that year. Layden sold her in 2008 to Dave Bolduc, who raced as Freebyrd and went on to win his class in the inaugural North Carolina Challenge the following year, sailing on through a night the rest of the anchored fleet had stopped for, so that one competitor, lying still at Taylor Creek, logged only that "Freebyrd's Matt Layden-designed Enigma passed us during the night" before seeing him again the next morning, beating close along the beaches in winds that had already driven the observer himself out of the race. Bolduc went on to cross the Bahama Bank in a fifty-knot frontal passage during the 2010 Everglades Challenge, and to sail Enigma alone and engine-free to the Bahamas in 2013. Weighing her against the rest of the family, Bolduc judges Enigma "perhaps the most versatile of his designs, since she can be used for day sailing and for cruising." He has also put a number on the two boats' different characters. Paradox, in Layden's own words as Bolduc recalled them, is "a 40 footer shrunk down to 14 feet," fitted out for living aboard for months at a stretch. Enigma is lighter and faster to build and tow, but far more spartan inside.

What the lighter construction costs in a real accident came home to one owner when a forty-foot rowing shell, travelling at speed, struck his Enigma and tore a hole in her side roughly ten inches by six. Water poured in at once, and he had to heel the boat onto one side under tow to slow the flooding until he reached shore. Layden himself, examining the damage afterward, judged that the same impact would most likely not have pierced Paradox's own much thicker sides at all. It might just as easily have delaminated a wider stretch of her plywood instead, trading an easy, quickly fixed puncture for a harder repair. Enigma's thin sides act as a kind of crumple zone, giving way so the two craft can glide to a slower stop together. Paradox's extra thickness keeps the water out but risks spreading the damage further if it ever lets go.

Two years after Enigma, in 2007, Layden drew a larger relative for her: the Enigma 460, about three feet longer at fifteen feet one inch, and fitted with a plywood hardtop cabin like Paradox's own rather than Enigma's folding canvas dodger. He drew her for a builder, George Van Sickle, who built the prototype and had her under sea trial by the summer of 2010. Van Sickle judged the crew compartment roomier than he had expected, tall enough, by his own account, to "sit upright athwartship with several inches of clearance overhead" at six feet of height. Plans for her were never published. In their absence a small population of Enigmas has been built from photographs and forum posts alone: Dave Fahy's Goliath, launched on Australia's Gold Coast in 2021, and André-François Bourbeau's Question, an Enigma-derived ketch launched the same year. Bourbeau, who also sails two Paradoxes, says one of them feels "stable, like a train on a track."

Sand Flea

Layden's seventh boat, built after Enigma, went the other way: smaller still and simpler. He announced her to the WaterTribe community himself, on 23 February 2007, in a message relayed the same day to the messing-about.com forums, where it survives exactly as he wrote it. Sand Flea, he told them, was "an 8' decked pram, 45" beam, 85 lbs empty weight, 45 sqft sail area," and he had already given her a trial sail the day before: "she is blazing fast!" He planned, he added, to rein her in a little that year, "just out of the kindness of my heart," before sailing her to the start of that year's Everglades Challenge from his home on Florida's east coast.

Sand Flea was the one later boat in the family to drop the chine runner altogether, carrying a single leeboard on her starboard side under a sliding hatch that could close her cockpit completely. She raced the Everglades Challenge in 2007 and again in 2008, and one eyewitness from the first of those years caught her designer's method in miniature: Layden turned down an offer of a camper berth to sleep aboard in a cove instead, wheeled the boat through a parking lot the next morning rather than sail her round in the dark, poled her barefoot across the Chokoloskee mud flats in eight to ten inches of water and still found half an hour at the checkpoint to talk over a few changes he had in mind for her. He slept at anchor every night of that race. On the third night he had only four and a half hours, which his own wife, following his progress from home, called oversleeping. He stopped along the way to rescue a capsized kayaker and still finished less than half a day behind a crewed boat.

Sand Flea won the Ultra Marathon outright in 2012, by Dave Bolduc's own firsthand account of watching his friend sail her to that finish; a photograph from that same race caught Layden smiling at the start, rare enough that one onlooker wrote it was the first time he had ever seen it. Bill Serjeant, a Paradox builder himself who set out to catalogue Layden's whole range of designs, called her "similar, but shorter and wider" than Paradox, "an audacious little cruiser, one that stretches the imagination, not the budget," and in Layden's hands she sailed hundreds of miles over the shallow waters of the Everglades. Like Enigma's, no plans for Sand Flea were ever published.

At the Cedar Key gathering in 2019, Dave Bolduc finally got to sail Sand Flea himself, and found her handling much like his own heavier sharpies despite her leeboard and her wider proportions. With her simple lug rig, her handy roller-furling boom and a familiar wrap-around rope-to-tiller steering, he wrote, "I was right at home!"

Elusion

Layden's newest and lightest boat at the time followed in 2010: Elusion, nine feet long and thirty-eight inches on the beam. She was built along much the same lines as Enigma but lighter still, at around 120 pounds empty, light enough to go onto a car roof without help. All of Layden's other boats carry a sculling oar or a yuloh for calm-weather propulsion; Elusion carries a stand-up paddle instead. Her sail area is not recorded.

She proved herself the same year she was built, entered in the twelve-hundred-mile WaterTribe Ultimate Florida Challenge, the same circumnavigation Enigma had already won her class in four years before. Elusion finished first in her class and third overall, behind two purpose-built paddling canoes, and was photographed with them at the finish back on the beach at Fort DeSoto. Paradox, Enigma and Elusion are sometimes spoken of together as three sisters, Paradox the eldest and heaviest of the three, Elusion the youngest and smallest; each one ballasts herself a different way: Paradox with lead and fresh water, Enigma with bagged sand and whatever cruising gear happens to be aboard, Elusion with nothing fixed at all, trusting entirely to her gear, her water and the weight of her own crew. Weighed against Paradox by an owner who has sailed one of Layden's designs for years, Elusion comes out "a little easier to manage," light enough to live on a car roof and simple to launch without a trailer, but "not as foolproof when at sea as Paradox, on account of being more dependent on the ability of the crew for keeping her upright when the going gets tough..."

The small racers

Alongside the cruising boats, Layden built at least two small paddling craft purely to race. The first was a decked canoe named Rob Royoid, after the Rob Roy type of canoe he is on record as loving outright; her twenty-eight-inch beam, like her length and her sail, was sized to fit WaterTribe's own rules. He wrote to his brother's website about her himself in February 2005: "Took her out in the River yesterday in 18-20 kt warm southeasterly; that little 10 sq. ft. sail makes her fly!" He steered her at first simply by shifting his own weight, and later fitted a trim tab for the job instead; narrow as she was, he could sleep and cook aboard her. The second was a twelve-foot plywood expedition kayak, twenty-four inches wide, narrow enough that when Dave Bolduc tried lying down inside her at Cedar Key in 2019 he was not sure he could have stayed comfortable there for a whole night.

One account from the time has Rob Royoid finishing the 2005 Everglades Challenge within thirty miles of the end; Bolduc remembers it differently, as the kayak that took second place in Class 1 that year. By 2010, a competitor counted the Layden-designed craft drawn up on the beach at the start of that year's Everglades Challenge. Elusion and Enigma stood alongside a trimaran he had helped a fellow racer design, the Tridarka Raider, and an expedition sailboard sailed by yet another competitor: four of his designs on one stretch of sand at once. By one rough tally of his whole racing life, on this handful of small craft and the cruising boats before them, Layden has sailed, rowed, sculled and paddled more than twenty-five thousand miles.

Boats on paper

Not every Layden design was built. "Big Cruiser," an enlarged Paradox he sketched out in cartoon form in 2003, survives only as a name and a handful of drawings in an old mailing-list file archive; no dimension or description of her was ever written down, and Layden never carried the idea through to finished plans. A half-remembered "Lugger #2" is recalled by one reader as a larger Swamp Thing. A third, the cat schooner he drew for Sven Yrvind in 2007, survives only as a commission. Official plans, in the end, exist for one Layden design only: Paradox herself.

Fifteen years on the same course

Layden kept racing for fifteen years after that first win, always afterward in a smaller boat of his own and never again in Paradox herself. None of the later results drew anything like the same attention, but the table below shows how rarely he finished outside the first few boats home.

He also turned up that same first year in the Michigan Challenge, in the West Marine kayak rather than a boat of his own design; a list member who found the result months later had to ask which race it even was, then wondered, half in earnest, "Is there anything he can't place well in?"

Layden closed his own telling of all this, at Cedar Key in 2019, the way he had answered the same question in private for years: if you want a boat like one of these, design and build your own, or find someone willing to do it for you. It was advice from a man who had spent thirty-four years taking it himself, one hull at a time, moving from leeboards to chine runners to a centerboard and back again, each boat built and sailed and judged before the next one was begun, and who had let almost nobody stand close to the process while he did it.

Thirty years after that first boat left the Connecticut woods, taking his own advice is still exactly what a stranger has to do. The plans have had one seller the whole time, Dave Bolduc, and the price has come back to exactly where it started: thirty-five dollars, the same figure a buyer paid that first year, confirmed again in January 2025 when a Minneapolis builder paid it by PayPal and reported the plans had arrived. No company stands behind him, no catalogue and no showroom, only the same family answering the same kind of letter for a quarter of a century. What a stranger gets back for the money is a stack of drawings and nothing else: no boat, no promise, only lines on paper waiting for someone willing to cut plywood, until, sooner or later, one more set of them becomes an actual hull sitting on a trailer.
