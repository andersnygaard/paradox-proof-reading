Chapter 7

Meeting a Paradox

A visitor meeting a Paradox for the first time usually meets her on a trailer, in a yard or at the top of a launch ramp, well before meeting her afloat.

On the trailer

Up on her bunks, a Paradox sits low and square: a small box of a hull with hard chines and a flat bottom, broken only by the two chine runners standing off the chine and the big rudder hung on the transom. The mast is stepped off center to port. On the road the whole rig, mast, boom, yard and furled sail, lies lashed along the port side deck in a brass tube loop built for the purpose. A matching loop on the starboard side holds the yuloh, the sculling oar carried as standard equipment and diagrammed on sheet 12 of the plans. Nothing projects past the trailer's own width. It is an unglamorous shape, and the numbers that go with it are equally plain.

The plans' own specifications, as first printed in Messing About in Boats on 15 July 1995 and reprinted two years later alongside Don Elliott's construction series, under their own title block, "Paradox, 4.2 m Coastal Cruiser, Matt Layden, Drawing #1," read like this.

Length overall13 ft 10 in (4.2 m)

Beam, without the chine runners4 ft 0.5 in (1.23 m)
Draft, at full cruising displacement9 in (23 cm)

Displacement, to the designed waterline1,410 lb (640 kg)
Prismatic coefficient0.58
Displacement/length ratio270

The prismatic coefficient and the displacement/length ratio need a word each, since no other source repeats them and they are not self-explanatory. The prismatic coefficient compares the hull's actual underwater volume with the volume of a prism the same length and the same cross-section as her widest point; a low figure describes a hull that tapers to fine, narrow ends, a high one a hull that carries its volume the full length of the boat. At 0.58, Paradox's ends stay full and square, which is exactly what her flat sides and sharp bow and stern suggest to the eye. The displacement/length ratio measures how heavy a boat is for her waterline length; anything above roughly 300 is considered a heavy-displacement hull, anything under 100 a light one. At 270, Paradox sits toward the heavy end. She is built to carry ballast and stores rather than to go fast for her length. Both numbers describe the same trade from two different angles: a hull built to carry weight through the water rather than to slip quickly across it. The plans themselves are drawn entirely in millimeters, and the magazine's own plans-ordering notice suggested a builder invest in a metric tape measure and a metal rule before starting.

The plans run to fourteen metric sheets and call for no lofting and no building frame. She is built from ten sheets of plywood in four thicknesses, one of 6 mm, three of 9 mm, four of 12 mm and two of 18 mm, a bill read directly off the plans by two different builders, in 2001 and again in 2003, whose counts match exactly. Not all of it need be marine grade: Glen Maxwell built Zoë in an inexpensive exterior grade, for a plain workboat finish he later wished he had made nicer.

The bottom itself is 18 mm plywood under three layers of 24 oz woven roving, about an inch of total thickness and heavier than a bare structural minimum needs. The record gives three separate reasons for the extra weight, not plain conservatism. It adds to the boat's self-righting range before a pound of lead or water ballast is counted. It gives the bottom panel enough stiffness on its own, without extra internal bracing. And it is a margin of safety against the rocks and debris a shallow-draft boat is tempted into. The same bottom is meant to dry out upright on a falling tide rather than lean over on her bilge. Bill Serjeant found that out the hard way.

Serjeant was a small-boat sailor from Essex, on the River Crouch. He took delivery of the plans in October 2003 and went on to run the UK's own Paradox website and its closest thing to a builders' registry for five and a half years. He built his own Paradox, Faith, over about two years and launched her at Burnham Marina in June 2007. He cruised her along the south coast to the Isles of Scilly, then sold her in 2009. He kept a build-and-cruise blog, Bill's Log, throughout. He later took on a second Paradox, kept her existing name Minnow and refitted her.

His own boat once dried out squarely on top of a submerged concrete mooring sinker, her bow left about 3 ft in the air, and he simply waited out the tide aboard her. Once she was back on her trailer, no damage to the bottom could be found. The plans allow 5 to 7 US gallons of epoxy to put it all together; real builds have used anywhere from that figure to well over it, depending on how the bottom and glass are laid up.

Afloat: the system in one view

On the trailer, the chine runners and the rudder are just two more flat shapes bolted to a flat hull. Afloat, with the boat heeled a little to her working sail, they begin to matter, and so does everything else that was lashed down or closed up a moment before.

The rig is a lug sail that rolls up on its own boom, worked entirely from inside the closed cabin with no need to go on deck. In place of a deep keel, centerboard or leeboards, Paradox carries two fixed chine runners: stout fins about two inches wide, running along roughly the middle third of each immersed chine. Together with the heeled hull they supply her lateral resistance. A large, balanced rudder hung on the transom, with no skeg ahead of it, carries a real share of that same side load. Neither part does the job alone; together they let a boat with nothing hanging under her point to windward at all. The vent trunk is a single structure that steps the mast, drains the foredeck and gathers every control line into the cabin while still letting the cabin breathe. Lead and water ballast, stowed low and only partly fixed in advance, keep the narrow hull on her feet and bring her back upright if she is ever knocked flat.

Stowage is spread through thirteen separate compartments, in a boat only 13 ft 10 in long, a figure Don Elliott thought worth remarking on in his own construction series. Each panel is removable by a few screws for cleaning, inspection or painting. The sliding hatch and the continuous side windows, with a structural rear window that is part of what keeps the boat watertight in a bad knockdown, close the cabin up entirely. The yuloh, a curved, Chinese-style sculling oar carried as the boat's standard engine-free auxiliary, and the steering line both let her be worked without going on deck at all. The steering line runs on four corner blocks, so the sailor can steer sitting anywhere in the cabin, and it locks off to sail hands-free. Under the yuloh alone, Layden averaged about 2 knots on the one calm stretch of the race he won.

None of this is separable in practice: the ballast gives the hull the heel the chine runners need, and the rudder carries the share of the side load the runners cannot. Her reported top speed, about 6 knots, comes from Glen Maxwell's own boat Zoë, which beat that figure off Clearwater in 2002.

Below: weight, ballast and headroom

This is where the trade stops being an abstraction and becomes a figure a builder has to account for, pound by pound and inch by inch. Builders learn early to keep two different weights apart. "Light ship" is the bare, empty hull; "displacement" is the boat loaded and sitting on her designed waterline, ready to sail. Confusing the two is easy: one builder, asked how heavy a fully loaded and ballasted Paradox actually is, guessed "maybe 1000 lbs?" Alastair Law answered with the one actual weigh-in the record holds.

Law is a CAD engineer from Yeovil, England, though Scottish by nationality. He built Little Jim almost single-handed, in a garage only two feet longer than the hull, over two years from 2001, and launched her at Poole Harbour in 2003, the first Paradox built in Britain. He has sailed her every season since. He wrote an owner's appraisal of the design for the Dinghy Cruising Association's own Bulletin, kept a build-and-cruise website from 2002 and gave a talk to the Amateur Yacht Research Society in 2020.

His own hull, glassed but with no cabin, interior or ballast yet fitted, came out at just under 600 lb on the scale, about half of the finished boat's 1,410-lb design displacement. Everything else, the lead, the water, the stores, the sailor, still had to go in.

Don Elliott broke his own loaded boat down to a total of 1,365 lb, close to the plans' own figure, most of it carried low: a small, heavy boat built for stability rather than speed.

The plans leave the lead itself as a range rather than a fixed number: 70 to 180 kg (155 to 400 lb), since how much a builder needs depends on how much else is being carried. Most builders actually use 400 lb, in ten forty-pound pigs. Water ballast adds a further 70 liters, about 154 lb, carried in two built-in tanks level with the chine logs, each vented through its own copper breather tube. None of this is for show: the ballast is what lets so narrow a hull stand up to her sail.

Headroom is the dimension every prospective builder asks about first, and the two figures belong together. Answering a builder's plain question in December 2006, Law gave both in one breath: 940 mm under the sliding hatch, where the sailor sits underway with the whole boat worked from that one seat, reducing to 860 mm forward of it, where a passenger or a second berth would be. Both figures are one measurement of one boat, Little Jim. The sleeping space below is 930 mm wide by 1,900 mm long as drawn, tapering to 780 mm at the narrow end by the mast step, confirmed independently against Law's own boat and against Enuf, Derek Clark's Paradox, built in Wolverhampton, England, in 2004 to 2006 to the same lines an ocean away. A builder willing to give up the side bins can gain about another 100 mm of length; one willing to give up some flotation volume as well can gain 200 mm, at the cost of some of the buoyancy that helps bring her upright if she is ever knocked down. Glen Maxwell's Zoë gives the clearest as-built figures for the cabin's height: 67 cm from the top of the bunk seat to the underside of the closed hatch, 91 cm from the cabin sole to the same point. Together, the numbers describe a cabin sized for one sailor of close to average height, with real but not generous room to spare.

Honest limits

These limits are the ones owners themselves report, each with the name of the owner who reported it, since a limit without a name is just a rumor. None of them stopped sailing their Paradox over it.

The tack from starboard onto port is the one every owner eventually notices: Bill Serjeant named it plainly as a hesitancy going about from starboard to port, and Alastair Law found the same thing aboard Little Jim, tacking to starboard more easily than to port. Launching and recovering her alone is a second limit Serjeant named: day sailing from a road trailer was "not really viable" with the trailers he had used, though he allowed that other owners manage it with trailers built more to the purpose.

Headroom sets a real ceiling. David Beard, an early owner who had rebuilt a secondhand hull into Whisper in 2001, stood 5 ft 11 in and found her "just enough height to sit up straight with a little to spare." The stock boat, as drawn, suits a sailor up to about six feet with a little margin and grows tight beyond it. Mark Waters, who built his own Paradox, Raffles, in Los Angeles, sailed her at 5 ft 10 in and 160 lb and found the stock size suited him with no changes at all, though he suggested, from the other side of the same question, that sailors over six feet think twice.

The cabin is sized, in the end, for one. Dave Bolduc, Little Cruiser's owner, put it plainly to a builder who asked the question "many" times over on the list: there is room for two aboard, but it is not spacious, since Layden's boats were "designed primarily to sail 1 person full-time," with room for a guest only on a short trip. Bolduc knew this firsthand: cruising in company with Layden in the Bahamas, the two boats' three crew cooked aboard Little Cruiser and ate together aboard the original Paradox, a boat Bolduc likened to a tent. Alastair Law took his own son day-sailing aboard Little Jim on Poole Harbour, and the two managed fine around each other all day, though the single seat was not big enough for two to share "unless you want to be really cosy." Two can day-sail a Paradox well enough, and two can even stand watches on a longer passage, but sleeping two aboard is a different matter, tight enough that one later couple chose to pitch a tent ashore rather than share the one berth.

Going aboard

None of these figures quite prepares a visitor for the moment of actually getting in. There is no cockpit to step down into, no companionway to duck through at a walking pace, and no separate hatch door either, a fitting left out of the design on purpose because a door is always the weak point in a two-part hatch. In an emergency the whole boat can be worked from inside: a sailor can duck below and slam the single sliding hatch shut without moving more than a foot, closed up entirely out of the wind and the spray in one motion. Once aboard, the hatch closes behind the sailor over a seal built in three parts: a strip of compressed foam along the top, two separate seals at the front to shed spray coming over the bow and a traversing beam at the rear that the hatch lifts over and drops behind as it slides home. With all three engaged, the boat is tight enough that Don Elliott, describing his own finished hatch in the same construction series, wrote simply that "Paradox looks like a submarine."

Elliott put the climb itself in one line, set off from the rest of his text as its own small warning to anyone about to try: boarding her is nothing like stepping into an open dinghy's accessible cockpit. "Entering Paradox is more like climbing into the cockpit of a jet fighter."
