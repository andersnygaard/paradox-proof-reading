Chapter 8

What kind of boat she is

A boat with a flat bottom and nothing fixed below the waterline to resist leeway still has to answer to some type or other, and the clearest way to place her is to watch sailors who already know her nearest neighbours draw the line themselves.

A microcruiser, not a pocket cruiser

The word for her own kind, microcruiser, is not a borrowing from a yachting catalogue. It has a birth date: a sibling mailing list that Dave Bolduc, whose own Little Cruiser has logged more sea miles under a Layden rig than any other boat afloat, opened in September 2001, for a kind of boat that did not yet have a settled name of its own.

Builders have kept that name apart from an older one, pocket cruiser, ever since, and two who have owned both kinds of boat are the clearest witnesses to the difference.

Nickolas Bell is a builder and sailor in South Australia who built the Paradox Thor and launched her on the Murray River in June 2015. He sailed her hard along the Yorke Peninsula and Spencer Gulf coast for seven years before selling her in 2022 to Mark Brothers, who renamed her Pamela B.

Nick Bell learned his trade first on a Stevensons Pocket Cruiser, the same 14 feet as a Paradox but considerably wider and open where Paradox is closed. He found her a lakes-and-rivers boat, wet and uncomfortable in water his own Paradox took in stride, even with the pocket cruiser's own built-in buoyancy to fall back on. Sean Mulligan, a firefighter from Lake Havasu City, Arizona, crossed the same line from the other direction: he had already founded the Havasu Pocket Cruisers Convention around the Stevensons design before he took over a nearly finished Paradox in 2013. The two boats share a trailer and a length. They do not share a kind of day on the water.

The sharpie question

The type itself began as a working boat. Sharpies started out as fishing boats in the shallow coastal waters of the eastern United States, their flat bottoms suited to water too thin for anything with a deep keel and beachable at the end of a day's work the same way Paradox still is. On the label itself, nobody who has built one has ever argued. Don Elliott, writing the Messing About in Boats series that documented Paradox's construction with Layden's own cooperation, proposed a name for the type outright in 1996: "For Paradox, I suggest using 'Chine Runner Sharpie', to differentiate from others. Then if someone would ask you what kind of boat Paradox is you could say; 'Its a chine runner sharpie'." His series pointed builders toward a real literature behind the label, naming Reuel Parker's The Sharpie Book and Howard Chapelle's American Small Sailing Craft as the standard references. A community-written build manual, independent of Elliott, calls her the same thing in plainer words: "a heavy displacement sharpie type."

What she keeps from two centuries of sharpie practice is real: a flat bottom, a narrow beam just under 48 inches (122 cm), hard chines and a habit, shared with the type generally, of beating her own theoretical hull speed. Builders of the old working sharpies, Elliott wrote, were practical people who kept the hard chine because they saw no advantage in curved hulls: "a sharpie was good enough with hard chines." What Paradox discards from the same type is just as deliberate. A working sharpie carries a leeboard or a centerboard; Paradox carries neither, relying on her chine runners instead. A working sharpie is sailed from an open deck or cockpit; Paradox has no cockpit at all, because, as Elliott put it, "a small boat cannot spare the precious room required for it." And where a traditional sharpie stays upright through low sheer and a low centre of gravity, Paradox does the opposite. She carries heavy ballast low in a comparatively tall hull, built to self-right from a genuine knockdown rather than to avoid one in the first place.

That last departure is also where the family resemblance runs into its own argument. The chine-runner idea has been built at far larger scale, in Chris Morejohn's Hogfish Maximus, a roughly 38-foot hull often mistaken, at a glance, for a scaled-up Paradox. Morejohn, a Florida sailor and builder of large sharpies, rejects the comparison on exactly the ground above. Traditional sharpies, he points out, have low sheers, low centres of gravity and different sailing qualities altogether. A hull built the way Paradox is built, self-righting rather than low and stiff, earns a name of her own: a "Modified Sharpie," not a sharpie plain and simple. Hogfish Maximus might be a bigger cousin of Paradox or a different animal entirely. Either way, Paradox herself is no plain sharpie.

Measured against her neighbours, and narrow on purpose

Builders who priced Paradox against cheaper, simpler designs mostly came back to her anyway. Jim Michalak, one of the designers Layden himself named among his own teachers, supplied the plainest competition. His AF3, an open-cockpit cruiser roomy enough to carry a family dog, was judged less stable than Paradox by builders who looked at both, though possibly a better boat for lying out in the sun. His Caroline, drawn for the rough coast of Maine, was the design recommended instead for a sailor too tall, heavy or unsteady to fit the hull Layden built for himself. John Welsford, a designer builders also measured her against, drew boats that share a surprising amount with Paradox and still make the trade starkly visible. His 18-foot Swaggie, drawing 2 feet 8 inches and displacing almost 3,850 pounds against Paradox's 9 inches and 1,410, is sailed and reefed from inside with a similar underlying stability, but wants a far bigger trailer and a far deeper slip to lie in. Video of her own launch bore the difference out: towing her was a real undertaking behind a substantial truck, lacking Paradox's easy grace on the ramp. His Fafnir, wider by almost 2 feet and drawing 2 feet 7 inches, was built for open-ocean voyages Paradox was never meant for. Alastair Law, Little Jim's builder in Yeovil, loved Fafnir but chose Paradox anyway: her shallow draft let her cross the shoal bars and beach dry-shod on England's south coast the way Fafnir never could.

Every one of those comparisons runs back to the same decision. Paradox is narrow, just under 48 inches, because a wider hull would cost her the one thing her whole design exists to buy: the certainty of coming back upright. Beam bought for stability is not free. A wider hull drags more wetted surface through the water, and once pushed past her limit she presents more of her own bottom to the wave that knocked her down in the first place; the same beam that resisted heeling then makes her harder to right. A boatbuilder working the question from outside the Paradox community in 2007 put the limit in its bluntest form: to be self-righting after a genuine knockdown in a heavy sea, a ballasted hull of this kind cannot be too wide, because there is "a maximum width for a ballasted boat before it will have a second stability point... upside down." Paradox, at 1,410 pounds displacement, was offered as the worked example: she is "just about as tall as it is wide." A boat-design text Elliott leaned on for the same series puts the two ends of the same trade in its own plain words: "in the last analysis and a total knock-down the slimmer vessel is likely to right herself, where as the beamier boat may remain capsized and upside down," while high freeboard, however costly in windage, "does marvels for maximum righting moments and range of stability." One builder, weighing Paradox against a rival design that existed only as a computer rendering, wished out loud in 2004 that someone would enlarge her by 20 percent; he was far from the only one to say so.

The second half of the trade is a classification, not a measurement. Bolduc classes Paradox and Little Cruiser as coastal cruisers rather than offshore boats, capable of real weather but not built to go looking for it. That classification sits beside real heavy-weather evidence rather than instead of it. Bolduc and Layden have beaten dead to windward in 22 to 27 knots and 8-foot seas in the Tongue of the Ocean, and the original Paradox herself had, by his count, about 3,000 miles behind her with no mishaps. A coastal cruiser, by Elliott's own definition from the same 1996 series, sails within reach of a passage short enough to read the weather for in advance; she does not need to be large. In Elliott's own words, "in fact it's better to be small."

The third piece of the trade is a number the formula gets wrong. A theoretical hull speed for Paradox's 13-foot-5-inch waterline works out to 4.76 knots by the standard rule of thumb. Glen Maxwell, sailing his own Paradox, Zoë, off Clearwater, Florida, logged GPS readings of 5.9 to 6.1 knots sustained for about two hours on a broad reach in 2002. The maximum for that leg was 6.3 knots, about 30 percent over the formula, on one boat, on one occasion. "It is not unusual for sharpies to sail much faster" than the formula predicts, he wrote at the time. Layden's own verdict, from his fullest interview with Small Craft Advisor in 2009, settles what the numbers add up to: these boats, he said, "generally outsail any similar sized moderate displacement cruising boat they come across (like Potter 15s, Compac 16s Peep Hens, SF Pelicans), upwind or downwind, zephyr or gale. Any decent light racing dinghy will walk (but not run!) away from them of course."
