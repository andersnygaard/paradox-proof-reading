Chine runners

A box with nothing underneath

A ridge of plywood about two inches wide, built up from the bottom panel along the middle third of each chine and faired into a shallow, tapered wing, is paired with a rudder big enough to look borrowed from a larger boat: that is the whole of what keeps a Paradox from sliding sideways across the water once her sail fills. Dave Raftery, who compiled the online side-by-side comparison of Layden's designs, gives the compact version of how it works: the chine runner is "a wooden foil shape which extends horizontally outward from the chine of the hull," and "the combination of the air foil shape, the heel of the hull and a large rudder, provide lateral resistance without the need for a centerboard." Nobody has ever given the fuller account more completely, or in better order, than Layden himself did in a magazine interview printed in Small Craft Advisor in May and June 2009.

Asked, in the simplest terms the magazine could put it, how and why the runners work so well, Layden turned first to an ordinary sailboat. The usual arrangement has two foils below the hull, he said: one roughly midship, usually a keel or a centerboard, carrying most of the sideways load, and one aft, the rudder, lightly loaded and there mainly for steering. Some modern boats share the load between the two instead, letting the midship foil be smaller. A few working boats, he noted, get by with hardly any midship foil at all and an unusually large, deep rudder. "So it looks like you can (more or less successfully) divide the leeway-resisting force between two lifting surfaces in about any proportion you like, and put a good share of the load on the rudder with no big problems (as long as its mountings and steering system are up to the forces)."

That is exactly what his own boats do, and the chine runner is not the part doing the heavy lifting. "In this system, my equivalent of the conventional midship foil carries half or less of the total leeway load, and is based on what aerodynamicists have termed a 'lifting body.'" He had first seen the idea in 1970s Aviation Week and Popular Science articles about experimental NASA aircraft: a shape that, moving at an angle to the flow, develops high pressure on one side and low pressure on the other, exactly as a wing does, so that the pressure difference pushes the whole hull sideways against the leeway instead of letting it slide. The chine runner's own job, in that account, is "really a fairly minor part of an integrated system": a narrow fence that keeps the high pressure from bleeding across the chine into the low, sharpening an effect the hull would produce on its own in any case. "The lifting body hull would work decently without them," Layden wrote, "but my rough sailing and towing model experiments show a noticeable improvement in pointing and handling with the runners added."

The remainder of the load, whatever the hull does not carry, goes to the rudder, "sized for the job, as deep and efficient as practical... with a conventional foil section for good lift across a good range of angles." Layden put a number, or rather a range of numbers, on the split: "This maybe 50-50 or 40-60, or even 30-70 distribution of the leeway-resisting forces puts the center of lateral resistance farther aft than in conventional sailboats, and wants the rig to also be placed far aft in the boat for balance, as you see in my designs." That single sentence is the hinge the whole system turns on, and here it only turns halfway. Because the rudder is doing so much of the side-force work that a keel would ordinarily do, the boat's centre of lateral resistance sits well aft of where a conventional sailboat would put it, and the rig has to move aft with it to keep her balanced: that is the mechanical reason, not the full account of what it then costs the sail and the steering built around it. The rudder itself is built to carry up to two-thirds of the load on its own, by Layden's own reckoning.

There is more to his account than the load split, and it answers a question the simple picture leaves open: what happens in a turn, rather than just sailing a steady course. "When the helm is put over, the lifting body hull with chine runners gives an 'equal and opposite reaction' force to counter the side force of the rudder, as does a conventional keel or centerboard. Without this opposing force from near the boat's center of gravity, the boat would try to slide sideways in the original direction of travel, regardless of which way the bow is turned. You may have noticed this if you ever tried to bring a centerboard dinghy up to a dock with the board up." Having the lifting surfaces well aft of the centre of gravity also buys something else, in Layden's telling: it makes the boat track dead straight ahead, "as do the big vertical tail surfaces on airplanes, or feathers on an arrow," while the big rudder still gives enough turning power to manoeuvre. A boat that tracks like an arrow and turns on a sixpence sounds like a contradiction, and in a sense it is, one bought back only by the size of the rudder and paid for in how she handles at close quarters.

Layden closed that first answer with a claim precise enough to be checked and a disclaimer honest enough to be believed. "I'll just stress one more time that in my opinion it's the whole system: hull shape, chine curvature, chine runner shape and size, and a good, big efficient rudder carrying a lot of the side load that makes these boats sail and steer well. I don't claim to understand the finer points real well, but these boats do work..." By his own account they generally hold their own against similar cruisers of their own size and weight on any point of sail, though a proper light racing dinghy will always leave them behind, and the design carries its own trade-offs alongside its advantages.

Where the idea came from

The runners did not arrive fully formed. Terrapin's leeboards had fouled lobster-pot lines and weed and failed Layden once off Maine in a knockdown hard enough to put the boat flat on her side, and the lesson pushed him toward something that would not catch on anything and would not need lowering or raising at all.

Swamp Thing, built next, was his first boat with chine runners. Sven Yrvind, the one witness who has put a date on the idea itself, has written repeatedly that the concept came to Layden in 1982, a verdict he later put in print as the greatest innovation for small cruising boats in a century. No other source puts a year on the idea's birth. 1982 rests on Yrvind's word alone, repeated often enough in his own writing to look settled, but tracing to one man's memory all the same. Swamp Thing herself was built in 1985 or 1986, the record divided between the two dates rather than one simply correcting the other.

Layden's own account of where the idea came from names no particular boat of Phil Bolger's. "Some seemingly random comments by Phil Bolger about a couple of his sharpies with external chine battens also got me thinking about what aerodynamic engineers sometimes call a 'fence,' to separate the high from the low pressure and minimize flow across the chine, and that evolved into my current 'chine runners.'" Married to the lifting-body reading he had already picked up from magazines about experimental aircraft, that is the whole of the documented origin: an unnamed pair of Bolger sharpies, an aerodynamicist's term for a fence, and years of his own sailing and model trials to turn the idea into a working shape.

One genuine parallel exists outside the Paradox family entirely, and nothing suggests Layden ever knew of it. In 1976, nine years before Swamp Thing, a Florida professor named Walter Castles wrote to the Amateur Yacht Research Society's own journal about catamarans he had built and sailed with "neither keels nor centerboards," relying instead on "1 1/2 inch vortex generators on the bottoms." He claimed the arrangement let him "split 90 degree tacks." Castles reached his design from wave-drag theory, not from Bolger or from NASA, and his equations for it, worked out at the society's request, were explicit about depending on both draft and heel angle, the same dependency later confirmed empirically, boat by boat, on Layden's own designs. The society's own editor noted in 2008 that similar fittings had, by then, been "added with some success on a few Wharram catamarans," though they had had little visibility on monohulls until Layden's own boats. Castles's name never appears anywhere in Layden's account of his own invention, and the two ideas, as far as anyone has found, grew up without knowing of each other.

The runners themselves did not arrive at their final shape all at once either. On Swamp Thing, by one builder's recollection, they began small and square, closer to an afterthought bolted onto the chine than to a true wing; only years of sailing, scale-model trials and small corrections turned them into the larger, tapered, triangular shape later built into Paradox. Even the same 2008 editorial that dated Swamp Thing's runners to 1986 admitted the design was still unfinished business rather than a solved problem: "most people have seemed to have used flat plates of plywood, more-or-less streamlined, and more-or-less delta shaped, but though these seem to work, one wonders whether they are as good as they could be." Nobody since has taken that invitation up with anything more rigorous than another builder's own trial and error.

Two explanations

How, exactly, does a two-inch ledge of plywood do what a centerboard does?

One explanation treats the runner as a deflector rather than a foil. Alastair Law, Little Jim's builder in Yeovil, put it to a newcomer on the builders' mailing list in December 2002 with a physical analogy anyone could picture: "Imagine a disc, the diameter of a tennis ball, mounted square on to a fluid flow. It will experience a force because the fluid that hits the disc stops, and transfers its momentum to the disc. Now imagine a tennis ball cut in half with the open side towards the fluid flow. Due to the hemispherical shape the fluid enters the ball (or cup) and is deflected back out. This reversal of flow transfers twice the momentum to the cup than was transferred to the disc so it experiences twice the force. You can see this in operation in a rotating-cup anemometer. The chine runner extensions behave a bit like the cup and cause the water to be deflected back, rather than just slowing it down as ordinary chines would." On this account Newton's third law is doing the work, not lift in the aerodynamic sense: the more water the runner throws to leeward, the harder the hull is pushed to windward.

Sven Yrvind built his own near-vertical, ballasted version of the runners on this same deflector principle, and stated it in his own words, writing up his reasoning for choosing them on his own boats: "I hypothesize that chinerunners work as deflectors, not as hydrofoils. Deflectors are concave surfaces that redirect what hits them... When the boat is heeled, the hullside together with the chinerunners creates a lot of lateral area." Unlike an ordinary keel, he judged, "the Bernoulli effect plays no significant role here," which he thought might explain both the runners' efficiency and why they are so widely misunderstood. His version of the idea differs from Layden's stock horizontal winglets in one important way: his own are angled close to vertical and carry lead ballast built into them, and he gave a longer list of reasons for the choice, from protecting the bottom like bilge keels to a small righting effect when the windward runner lifts clear of the water as the boat heels, like a trimaran lifting its windward float. "It's the combined advantages of the many small advantages that makes me choose them," he wrote. None of the seven reasons is decisive on its own; together they tipped the choice. He was still describing the mechanism the same way, in his own spoken words, more than two decades after first testing it: in a video posted to his own channel in the summer of 2025, he explained that the chine runner itself is simply the straight side of the hull where the bottom projects a couple of inches outside the boat, and that when the hull is pressed over, that projection is what does the work.

Yrvind has since drawn one line between coastal sailing and ocean sailing that Layden has never needed to draw. For long ocean passages he now prefers a centreboard or a leeboard instead, since it gives him one more way to control the boat running downwind, judging chine runners well suited to coastal work of the kind Layden does but not to his own reach across oceans. No Paradox has crossed an ocean to test that boundary from the other side.

Layden's own account sits in the opposite camp, closer to a foil than a deflector: a lifting body developing pressure difference the way a wing does, with the chine runner acting as a fence rather than as the lifting surface itself. He gave much the same account again a decade later, more briefly, at the Cedar Key dinner in May 2019, describing a "hydrodynamic fence... with low pressure on one side and high pressure on the other," which lets his sharpies "sort of 'fly' upwind through the water with the aid of a big rudder to reduce leeway."

One correspondent pushed the foil account further still: a chine runner, together with the bottom of the boat, behaves less like an ordinary foil and more like a delta wing, Concorde's wing or a crab-claw sail, generating its force from lift vortices rather than from smooth flow across its surface. On that reading the runner has no true leading edge at all, which is why the whole of it has to stay submerged, with the boat heeled enough to tip its flat upper face toward vertical.

Nobody has settled which account comes closer to the truth, and the design's own author does not pretend the question is closed. What both camps agree on completely, whatever they call the mechanism, is that none of it works unless the boat is heavy enough and heeled enough to put the runner where it can do anything at all.

Heel, weight and depth

A chine runner mounted on a boat that floats flat and light is decoration. It only becomes a working part once the hull heels and settles deep enough to bury it in solid water, and that single requirement runs through every account of how the runners work, deflector and foil alike.

Glen Maxwell gave the clearest number anyone has put on how far a Paradox's draft grows as she heels. Writing to a newcomer in December 2002, he reported: "Paradox's draft goes from 9" when upright to aprox. 23" at 30 degrees angle of heel." That is more than double her upright draft from thirty degrees of heel alone, and it is exactly the depth the lower runner needs to reach before it has solid water to work against.

Bill Serjeant sailed a second Paradox of his own, Minnow, for years on the River Crouch in Essex, and found an actual sweet spot rather than just a trend. At roughly fourteen degrees of heel, with the boat suitably loaded, the windward chine runner sits "just breaking the surface of the water" while the transom clears the drag of trailing in her own wake, a trim he judged her best sailing condition. Restated a few months later on a boat of 640 kilograms (1,410 pounds) displacement, sailing in about ten knots of wind, Force 3, the same observation came back more precisely: fourteen and a half degrees of heel by inclinometer, the windward runner again just skimming the surface, "her optimum." When he handed Minnow on to a new owner, he put the whole finding into four words of practical advice: "She sails best to windward when the windward chine runner is just clear of the water. You'll need a Force 3 for this."

Layden himself has a rule of thumb for which hulls can carry the device at all, recalled by a fellow builder, Jack Brubaker, from a conversation at the Sail Oklahoma gathering: the chine at its deepest point ought to run to roughly a quarter of the bottom's width, or at the very outside a fifth. Jim Michalak, whose own shallow-chined small cruisers fall short of that ratio, and Layden are said to agree that this is exactly why Michalak's hulls do not suit the device, whatever else recommends them. The whole family of chine-runner boats sits inside a narrow beam-to-draft window for the same reason: Paradox herself measures 1.23 metres of beam against 0.23 metres of draft, and Yrvind's own near-vertical YRVIND ½ comes out at 1.3 metres of beam against 0.22 metres of draft, close enough to look like the same proportion chosen twice, by two designers, thirty years apart.

An outside voice makes the same point from a different direction. Michael Storer, an Australian designer known for small plywood boats such as the PDRacer and the Goat Island Skiff, has no stake in the chine-runner debate at all, and reached his own conclusion anyway: "I think it is almost all to do with the depth of hull in the water vs the very modest sail area. The hull sides are enough of a 'keel' by themselves and the rudder and 'lifting body' arguments augment the effect of the deep hull." He put the practical consequence plainly the following year: "It does mean that the method only really works for heavy boats."

None of this is incidental to the rest of the design. A boat this small carries a surprising amount of lead and water ballast, and keeps almost all of it down low near the bottom of the hull, for exactly this reason: without that weight, carried low, the chine runners never submerge far enough to do anything at all.

Two tests, a month apart

The clearest demonstration of how Layden actually works, tested rather than argued, happened in two steps a month apart in the autumn of 2001, on his own Paradox.

The first was informal. Dave Bolduc told the builders' list that Layden had been trying the chine extended forward on his own boat, "to improve the overall performance." After several trial sails, Bolduc reported, Layden "feels that the modification has made very little difference, so he does not recommend the change." That might have been the end of it: a designer's own hunch, tried, found wanting, set aside. Layden did not leave it there.

A month later he went back out, this time with a stopwatch, a compass and a plan. He had extended the port chine runner sixteen to eighteen inches forward of its stock length, and he wanted a real answer, not a feeling. Sailing a stretch of the Indian River with almost no current and little traffic, he laid out a triangular course with legs over a mile long, using charted channel markers and a point of land as his fixed marks, and ran it three times, timing every leg including the downwind one as a check against changing wind strength. He dipped the sail to leeward of the mast at every tack, something he never normally bothered with, specifically to cancel out any asymmetry the lug rig itself might introduce. He wrote up the method as carefully as the result: "I tried to do what I reasonably could to keep inaccuracy from accumulating, but I don't really know how good the results are... I would say that there is a margin of error of at least 1/10 knot in the results, I think doing much better would probably require a towing tank."

The numbers, averaged across the three circuits, came out close on both tacks. Downwind she made 4.53 knots. On the starboard tack, sailing the stock runner, she made 3.63 knots through the water at 51 degrees off the true wind, for 2.29 knots made good to windward. On the port tack, sailing the lengthened runner, she made 3.49 knots at the same 51 degrees, for 2.21 knots made good to windward. The two tacks sailed different runners and came out within a tenth of a knot of each other, which was itself the size of his own stated margin of error. Layden's verdict, in his own words, is as plain as the numbers themselves: "What this mostly does is tell me in numerical terms that yes, she really does go to windward passably well without a centerboard, and no, the as-built chine runners are not dismally bad though they may be susceptible to improvement." Fifty-one degrees off the true wind is not a tacking angle between two tacks, which would come to roughly double that; it is the angle made good on a single tack, and the two figures should not be read as the same kind of measurement.

The two tests together, tried by feel and then measured with a stopwatch a month later, are Layden's own method in miniature: try it, sail it, and if the numbers do not clear your own margin of error, say so plainly rather than claim an improvement you cannot prove.

Builders have worked out at least six separate ways to shape the compound curve a chine runner needs, from pre-cut solid blocks shaped on a bandsaw to a single length of timber kerfed and bent into the curve, and none of the six is judged particularly difficult.

What other sailors found

Layden's own trial is the most precisely measured test anyone has run, but it is far from the only one, and the others reach much the same moderate verdict from very different directions.

Sven Yrvind supplied the first outside test, sailing Paradox to windward for a month in October 1997 and coming away convinced she pointed at least as well as his own boats.

Glen Maxwell supplied a harder test under worse conditions. Cruising the Cumberland Sound near St Marys, Georgia, in December 2002, he spent nearly five hours short-tacking Zoë against a foul current and winds of about twelve knots to reach his anchorage, covering eighteen nautical miles at an average of 3.6 knots, tacking through as little as ninety degrees when he chose to point and a steadier hundred and ten when he chose to keep her moving. "To all those skeptics of the chine runners I can now attest to their performance," he wrote to the list that evening.

The sharpest test of all came from a man who expected the boat to fail. Duncan Gilchrist, a member of the Dinghy Cruising Association who had doubted in print that a keel-less, board-less flat-bottomed hull could sail to windward at all, borrowed Little Jim for a trial at Chew Valley Lake Sailing Club in October 2006, with the club's own rescue boat standing by in case his scepticism proved justified. It did not. Writing up the day for the association's Bulletin, he reported that "she does go to windward at 45° to the wind, and at close to her hull displacement speed of 4.5k. She tacks through 90°," never failing to complete a tack in the light, patchy breeze that day gave him, though he noted that she drops level onto her flat bottom and slows sharply as the wind pressure comes off the sail between tacks, which left him unconvinced she would beat up a narrow river with any confidence. A sceptic's trial, conducted under a club's own watchful eye, with the doubt and the result both printed honestly, is about as clean a piece of outside evidence as a fourteen-foot amateur design is ever likely to get.

Two more witnesses owe the design nothing at all, having come to it from entirely outside the Paradox family. B&B Yacht Designs, a working boatbuilding firm in North Carolina, looked seriously at fitting chine runners to their own Core Sound 17 after she proved weak to windward in an Everglades Challenge. Their designer, Graham Byrnes, worked out a test as rigorous as Layden's own: hot-glue a single runner to one side of the boat only, sail her, and read the runner's contribution directly off the gap between the GPS course made good and the compass heading on each tack. He never carried that test through to a finished result, but he did sail a visiting Layden-built Enigma that same month and came away impressed enough to write it down: "I am still amazed that the chine runners work as well as they do but my best guess was that she tacked through about 120 degrees," a rough figure taken by eye, without instruments, from inside an unfamiliar boat. What his firm's own research turned up mattered more than any single number: the runners are not something that can simply be bolted on. Layden's hulls are flat-bottomed with vertical sides, which is why the runners work set out dead horizontal; a V-bottomed hull with flared topsides, Byrnes judged, would need them angled below horizontal instead, a difference that has to be found by trial on each new hull. A second designer who had tried the idea across several of his own hulls put the finding in blunter, general terms: forms with plenty of chine bury and bottom rocker respond well, while flatter, flatter-running hulls do not respond nearly as well.

The other outside witness ran an actual experiment rather than a thought about one. Alan J, an Australian builder with no Paradox of his own, fitted temporary horizontal winglets to one side only of a Bolger Teal, a far lighter and shallower hull than anything Layden ever drew, specifically to test whether the idea could help a daggerboard he judged too small for the boat. Sailing her in March 2008 with channel markers as his leads, he found the difference "simply stunning": close-hauled leeway fell from an estimated twenty degrees on the bare side to about five degrees on the winglet side. But further sailing tempered the result honestly rather than overselling it. "The winglets on their own are not big enough to beat to windward," he wrote. "That's ok, they were only ever intended to supplement the too-small centreboard." He found, too, that the winglets induced weather helm rather than lee helm, which told him that the forward third of the fitting, not its full length, was doing most of the work. He made them permanent anyway, judging the gain real even if it fell short of a proper board. Independent of Layden's own design, with no reputation riding on the outcome either way, Alan J's small boat reproduced exactly the limit Layden states about his own: the device helps, sometimes by a lot, but it is not a substitute for a keel.

Chris Morejohn's Hogfish Maximus put the same mechanism to the hardest test any Layden-family hull has faced: an ocean crossing, at nearly three times Paradox's length. She crossed the Atlantic from Florida to Portugal by way of Bermuda and the Azores in the summer of 2004, with Glen Maxwell aboard as crew. His report from Horta, in the Azores, gave the first firsthand account of chine runners proven at ocean scale: "The chine runners work great even at this size range and we only use the daggerboard if we are really strapped in and clawing to weather. It has probably not been used for more then 48 hrs on the whole trip as she goes to weather just fine without it." Thirty-eight feet struck him as more boat than he personally wanted, but the crossing left him with a settled opinion about the right size for the idea: "I'm thinking that a 21 to 25 foot version of the Paradox would be pretty grand for ocean voyaging." Hogfish Maximus and her near-sister Jubilee both pair their runners with a centerboard rather than relying on the runners alone, and their narrower, "wing" style of runner proved more effective than their own designer had expected, adding little turbulence even sailed with the board fully raised. A hull nearly three times Paradox's length crossing an ocean on chine runners, with her centerboard needed for less than two days of a six-week passage, is the strongest proof anywhere that the mechanism scales.

Owners who sail stock Paradoxes have reported a real scatter of tacking and pointing figures over the years, and the scatter itself is a finding rather than noise to be smoothed away, because not everyone who posted a number was measuring the same thing.

These numbers do not collapse into one answer, and they are not supposed to. Some report the angle made good against the true wind on a single tack, which runs from about forty-five to sixty degrees across these accounts; others report the full angle turned through between one tack and the next, which runs from about ninety to a hundred and fifty. A true wind angle and a tack-to-tack angle measure related but different things, and the two kinds should never be read against each other as if they were the same figure. A Paradox tacks somewhere between ninety degrees on a good day in light air and something closer to a hundred and fifty in a chop with a tired or inexperienced hand on the helm, and no single figure replaces that range.

They do not work alone

A newcomer reading all this praise could be forgiven for thinking the chine runner does more than it really does. Layden himself has corrected that impression directly, and in terms sharp enough to be remembered: "It seems a minor internet mythology has grown up around the misconception that chine runners, in themselves, replace a centerboard or other conventional foil for leeway resistance. That's not the case, they're really a fairly minor part of an integrated system."

The hull, the runners and the rudder share a load no single one of them could carry alone, and every outside witness who has tried the idea on an unrelated hull, favourably or not, has rediscovered that same limit on their own. What nobody has settled, after thirty years of sailing, is which physical account of the mechanism comes closest to the truth, and the design's own author has never claimed to know.

One builder is known to have cut his chine runners away for good. A photograph that surfaced on the builders' mailing list in August 2015 showed a Paradox carrying a Bermudan sail and no chine runners at all. Her builder, Tom Steinmetz, had cut them away and fitted a leeboard in their place. He never explained why, and nobody who ever sailed her left a word about how she went. A fellow builder who researched her history afterward summed up the change in four words: once the runners were gone and the leeboard fitted, "there is no way back."
