Chapter 10

Rudder

Paradox is an exception, since her large rudder acts as a keel by providing lateral resistance.

Bill Serjeant, Bill's Log, "Macwester 28's Rudder," 6 May 2014

Dave Bolduc got a tape measure on Paradox's rudder in the winter of 1993, sailing Little Cruiser alongside her across the Bahama Banks while her designer slept. Matt Layden had already trimmed the blade down once by the time Bolduc caught up with it, and said he preferred the shape of Little Cruiser's own elliptical rudder because it was better balanced. Even reduced, Paradox's blade measured 29.13 inches from the pivot hole and 15¾ inches wide. Little Cruiser's own rudder measured 25⅜ inches from the pivot hole and 16¼ inches at its widest. Bolduc traced the comparison onto a sheet of construction paper so it could be copied. The size was meant to buy windward ability and straight-line tracking, and on that crossing it did: Layden slept through much of the night passage because Paradox held a fairly straight course on her own, while Dave and Mindy Bolduc took turns at Little Cruiser's helm because she wandered more. "The big rudder acts like a brake," Bolduc wrote afterward, "and if you just slam it over while tacking you'll stop."

The blade

Built from two half-inch sheets of plywood glued face to face into a full inch, the blade is tapered fore and aft and top to bottom into something like a foil. Don Elliott, who built his own Paradox to the plans and wrote up every step of it for Messing About in Boats, shaped his blade's cross-section to what he called "a NASA airfoil shape." He radiused the leading edge and cut the trailing edge off in a small flat rather than a sharp point, a shape he judged had "proven best for use on underwater foils." A mailing-list measurement taken in 2003 instead matched the blade against a NACA 0006 section, a good deal thinner than the NACA 0012 or 0015 sections usually quoted for a small boat's rudder. The two readings do not agree on a name, but they agree on the shape. It is flatter than a textbook foil, built for grip at a heavy cruiser's walking pace rather than for speed. The blade hangs on a single bolt that pins it to a fixed stock, the piece that actually passes through the hull and carries the tiller. The bolt is left deliberately loose so the blade can swing on it. Lead cast into the blade's trailing edge means that gravity, not a lock of any kind, holds it down against its own buoyancy. The sailor never touches the blade directly. It answers to a continuous rope loop, worked like a pair of reins from a seat inside the cabin.

Why she is slow to tack

That same size has a cost. Dave Bolduc has sailed Little Cruiser and watched both Paradox and Swamp Thing at close quarters for three decades. He named it plainly: Paradox "is indeed very stiff tracking and at times hard to tack at slow speeds," a direct consequence of her wide transom and large rudder. Swamp Thing was an earlier Layden boat built with chine runners and no centreboard at all. She never showed the same trouble, which rules out the missing board as the cause. It is the wide stern and the oversized rudder, the same deliberate trade that gives Paradox her tracking, that make her slow to turn. "Matt's emphasis in Paradox was to get as much directional stability as possible," Bolduc wrote. The rudder is not only a steering blade but a working third of the boat's lateral resistance, sized and placed to hold a course rather than spin on a dime.

A wide transom and a big rudder work the same on either tack and cannot by themselves explain why coming about from starboard onto port is worse than the other way round, a detail every owner of two or three Paradoxes eventually notices. Bill Serjeant, who built Faith, thought he had the answer. Writing up her early trials, he traced the asymmetry to the one part of the boat that genuinely differs from one side to the other: "Because her mast was set to port of her centre line and her sail was hung to the port side of her mast, the resulting drag on that side could make tacking from starboard tack to port tack problematical." He said the same thing two years later, in a short piece on Layden's whole family of designs, naming it the design's "least obvious" fault. Owners learned, over the years and mostly by watching Layden sail his own boat, a technique for coaxing her through the turn instead of fighting it.

Bearings and the pivot bolt

Underwater, the load the rudder carries comes down to two small fittings: the pivot bolt itself and the lower gudgeon it swings in. UHMW, ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene, has been the standard answer since early in the design's life. Builders machine it from a solid block where they have the means, and cast it in place as a heavy fibreglass-and-epoxy shell where they do not. The plans call for six layers of roving laid up on the bias so the cloth drapes into the mould, then poured with a dense filler once it cures. Rob Kellock, a New Zealander who built Mini at Kirwee, near Christchurch, and launched her in December 2017, cast his own gudgeon in 2019 with chopped strand at the core and four layers of cloth wrapped around it. He judged the result stronger than anything bought: "that gudgeon is so strong, it leaves any metal gudgeon for dead." A builder worried that the bolt hole itself will let water into the surrounding wood can bush it with nothing more exotic than epoxy: drill it oversized, fill it solid, then re-bore it to the bolt's true diameter.

By the Bolducs' own count, Little Cruiser had logged about 10,000 miles under her flat bottom, many of them in deep water. The original Paradox, which they judged probably a little more seaworthy, had done about 3,000 miles on her "with no mishaps."

Against that record stands one clean failure. Alastair Law, Little Jim's builder in Yeovil, was finishing a week's cruise off England's south coast. On the last day he was running downwind for eight miles in a Force 5, with a foul tide pushing against him near Lepe Spit in the Solent, when Little Jim's blade let go. "I didn't hit anything except the turbulence," he wrote to the Facebook group that by then carried the conversation, "but the rudder blade sheared off, very neatly, through the pivot bolt. 'Well, oh dear' I thought, or words to that effect." Little Jim had been launched in 2003, so the bolt had done seventeen years of work before it gave out, and it did no other damage when it did. Asked afterward whether the rudder was the system's weak point, Law gave the most measured answer on record. He called it not weak, but the weakest part, since it is the one part with moving parts and the stress points that go with them. Compared with most fourteen-foot production boats, he said, it is "immensely strong."

Kick-up and grounding

A kick-up rudder earns its keep the moment a Paradox touches bottom. An upward force on the blade, from a grounding or a beaching, simply pushes it up against the underside of the hull rather than loading the pintles. Bouncing on a falling tide does no structural harm as a result. Left to swing down freely, the blade clonks the corner of the transom on its way, then clears enough for the tiller's full travel. A hauling line can hold it at any shallower angle a sailor wants for shoal water. Raised fully, the blade turns through something like 180 degrees end to end, far enough that it tucks out of the way rather than sticking out astern when the boat is anchored or trailered. That full stowing swing is a different motion from the ordinary helm throw while under sail. The plans put the helm throw at 30 degrees each way, a figure builders scaling a tiller from the plans have confirmed independently. The plans draw an uphaul to raise the blade; Elliott's advice to builders was to add a downhaul too, "for ease of operation and safety," since the plans leave the blade to fall under its own weight once released. Most builders have taken his advice. Raised in shallow water, the blade costs the boat some of her grip, which brings noticeable lee helm. It buys real capability in return: a Paradox can work to windward with the rudder raised to 90 degrees and less than half its area still in the water, sailing to windward in as little as two feet of it.

Not the blade itself but the lower gudgeon and the pintle that turns inside it take the worst of this load. On Little Jim, a pintle bedded in epoxy and keyed only by roughening its surface worked loose after six years and turned up floating free inside the rudder stock when the rudder came off to add a fresh layer of glass to its bottom; it was re-bonded with saw cuts filed into the rod for a proper mechanical key, and an annual check has been standard on that boat ever since. The leading edge takes its own punishment from ordinary bottom contact, and owners have tried most of what a hardware store offers to protect it: a length of copper pipe split and hammered around the edge, sound after ten years of service; a webbing strap wetted out in epoxy and folded over it; a sacrificial length of rope glued in to be sanded off and renewed. None is preferred over the others, and all of them answer the same wry complaint: an unprotected leading edge makes "the default depth finder" on too many groundings.

The idea of simply building a smaller rudder keeps resurfacing. Sailing Whisper, David Beard once realised about 45 minutes into a passage that the blade had never actually been lowered. The boat had tacked a few times and reached without anyone noticing. That led to the tentative verdict: "I believe this boat sails better with the rudder up!!!!" Further testing in stronger winds would decide whether to cut the rudder's area by half or more. Nothing came of the plan beyond that single data point, but the suspicion that Paradox's rudder could be smaller than Layden first drew it has never quite gone away.

The stock also carries one fitting that has nothing to do with steering: a small step built into its bottom, on the port side. It is meant for boarding from a beach, where it works well. It is nearly useless for hauling oneself back aboard after going over the side in open water, since there is no way to get a foot on it while also pulling up over the stern; a trailing line with a loop tied in it, left ready, is the better aid for that harder case.

The slot in the transom

The rudder stock passes through the transom, not over it: a slot is cut through the hull's aft face for the stock and its tiller arm to swing in, wide enough for the blade's full travel. The plans draw a flexible boot over the tiller where it crosses that slot, so the opening can be closed off. Left open, a slot like that in a small boat's stern still looks like an obvious way for a following sea to climb aboard. One prospective builder, Tom Sailor, called it a "wave-gulping slot across the stern" that "gives me the heebie geebies." Layden himself has crossed the Gulf Stream in Paradox more than once with that same open slot in her stern, and shipping water through it is not something owners report. A baffle built into the transom behind the boot catches whatever gets past it and drains it straight back overboard, so that nothing that comes through the slot runs on into the cabin.

Not every builder has sailed waters the baffle has been proven against. Alberto Callejon built Cachivache on the Patagonian coast of Argentina, the southernmost Paradox on record. He closed up her transom slot rather than sail with it open as drawn. Asked about it on the Facebook group, he answered in one line: "The waters of the South Atlantic are rough enough to leave it open."

Self-steering

A Paradox will often hold a course on her own in light air with no added gear at all, the tiller ropes' own friction enough to keep her on track while a sailor makes a sandwich below. That steadiness has limits, and builders have spent two decades looking for something to extend it. By Dave Bolduc's account, the same large-rudder idea lay behind Sven Yrvind's own 15-foot Bris, which reportedly burned out two autopilots crossing the Atlantic. That is a hint that a rudder loaded this heavily asks more of self-steering gear than most small boats do.

The obvious fix, a trim-tab wind vane of the kind that became standard equipment on cruising boats generally, runs straight into the rudder's own design. A trim tab has to bolt onto the blade itself, and Paradox's blade has to be free to fold up to clear a beach; there is no way to hang a hinged tab on a blade that must do that, and the lightest commercial vanes built for a boat this size have the same problem. Anything mounted on the transom, trim tab or vane alike, also sits exactly where the mainsheet sweeps on every tack and gybe.

Three builders have tried to get around the problem, and none ended up bolting anything to the blade. Sven Yrvind worked out an integrated system for one of his own boats, judged suitable for Paradox too. It had a false transom hinged to the real one, carrying a short mast, a trim tab geared to double the rudder's own angle and a friction peg to hold a chosen helm. The whole assembly raised together to clear the ground when needed. It has never been built on a Paradox. Roselt Croeser, a Western Australian builder, fitted a horizontal-axis vane of plywood to his own Planktos in 2017. He built it from a sketch found on a catamaran-building website, with ropes carrying the vane's force to the tiller. It worked, after a fashion. But once the boat was finished and sailing, he judged it an unnecessary complication: Planktos needed so little steering that he could feel his way by hand without it, and he set the vane aside. Glen Maxwell came closest to a finished design. He built a horizontal-axis vane for Zoë that mounted its servo tab on standoffs beside the rudder, clear of the blade's own kick-up travel. He sea-tried it at the Cedar Key gathering in 2007, short-tacking down Crystal River until the mainsheet caught the gear's oar carrier and broke it off; the mechanism itself worked fine, and a fuller trial that July held a steadier course than hand-steering could. The drawings he meant to publish were never finished. Years later, after he had sold her, Zoë burned in transit to her new owner with the gear's own documentation still aboard.

Running downwind is the hardest case of all, on a Paradox as on most small boats carrying only the one sail. Squared off downwind, most of the sail's area lies outboard of the boat, so a slight shift in wind speed swings a far larger turning moment than a sail concentrated close to the mast ever would. No self-steering scheme on record has managed it short of reefing hard. What builders actually fitted, in the end, was simpler than any of the three attempts above: a small electric tillerpilot, coupled not to the tiller itself but to the steering line. The coupling is a rolling hitch Bill Serjeant worked out for his own boat in 2008, and others have copied it since. Mounted inside the hull, it asks nothing of the folding blade, needs no mounting point on the rudder at all, never fouls the mainsheet and only has to do a job the tiller ropes already do most of in light air. Kellock, after enough long open-water runs in Mini to feel the real cost of holding the helm by hand, concluded in 2022 that he needed to buy one of his own.

Kellock had already learned, the hard way, how closely sail and rudder work together. Grounded on a sandbar in the Christchurch Estuary after a lull cost him his pointing ability, he pumped out half his water ballast to drag Mini free. Once light, though, she could not point at all, crabbing sideways instead of gaining ground to windward. Scandalising the sail moves its own centre of effort forward. Raising the rudder moves the boat's own centre of grip forward by very nearly the same amount. A boat that will not point in a lull with the rudder up may do no better, and perhaps worse, than the same boat with the rudder left down. Sail and rudder, in other words, are not two separate decisions a sailor makes one after the other. They are trimmed as one.
