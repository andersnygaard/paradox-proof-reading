Ballast and self-righting

Some have declared the Paradox unsailable due to trying to sail it empty, bobbing on top with no chine leverage for directional stability and no immersion to cause stability.

Larry Whited, Paradox builders mailing list, 19 April 2003

Lift the lead and the water out of a Paradox and she stops being a small yacht and becomes something closer to a box that happens to float: she rides high, her runners clear of the water they are shaped to grip, and a hand laid on the mast is enough to put her over. Bring her back down onto her lines with four hundred pounds of lead, a tank or two of water and whatever food, anchors and gear a cruise calls for, and she turns into the opposite kind of boat: stiff enough to carry sail in a real wind, deep enough to point and able to come back up from a dead knockdown before her sailor has done anything about it.

That is the paradox restated in its plainest form. A fourteen-foot boat built to be beached, trailered and handled by one person carries, by design, as much dead weight as a large outboard motor. She needs every pound of it just to work.

A boat that needs to be heavy

Don Elliott, The Edge's builder, gave the fullest ballast budget on record, answering a New Zealand builder's plain question about trailered weight in a mailing-list reply from July 2001. Elliott had built his own Paradox deliberately heavy, and his boat is still the one most builders reach for first when they want to know where the weight actually goes. Four hundred pounds of lead, cast as ten forty-pound pigs and strapped down in the bilge. Seventy-five pounds of fibreglass roving and resin, there mainly so a builder who runs up on a rock or a reef has something to thank for still floating. A hundred and fifty pounds of fresh water in two tanks, which Elliott insisted stay topped up whenever the boat is actually cruising. A week's food ran eighty to a hundred pounds, though a sailor runs out of room before reaching the full hundred, Elliott noted. A forty-five pound battery and forty pounds of anchor and line came next, doubled for anyone who carries two anchors, as Elliott himself did. Add a hundred and twenty pounds of plywood and lumber in the tank structure at the very bottom, and the weight sitting in the bottom ten inches of the hull comes to about nine hundred and thirty pounds.

Above that line sits far less: two hundred pounds of plywood and structure, thirty-five pounds of mast and deck gear above the cabin top and two hundred pounds for the crew and whatever they carry. Elliott advised moving that last weight down onto the floor in bad weather for maximum stability. The whole total, on a boat built deliberately heavy, came to one thousand three hundred and sixty-five pounds. Forty-five pounds of margin was left over for whatever did not fit the plan; Elliott guessed the rudder alone might account for it, or perhaps half of it. Close to two thirds of that total sits in the bottom ten inches. "Paradox is a small, heavy boat with a lot of stability," he wrote, and left the point there.

Dave and Mindy Bolduc, Little Cruiser's owners since 1992, describe the same logic running through all of Layden's designs: lead, water and whatever stores a cruise carries all count as ballast together. As a passage eats into the food and the water, something else quietly takes up the slack: an anchor, a battery, a week's tinned goods. The boat never loses her trim for long. It is a scheme inherited from Little Cruiser, and it explains why no single number for "how much ballast" has ever settled the question for good.

How much is enough

Elliott built heavier than the plans strictly require. The design itself allows a range: between 70 and 180 kilograms of lead (154 to 397 pounds). The lower figure suits a boat that will otherwise travel loaded with gear and stores; the higher figure suits one that travels light. Whatever the total, the hull's own waterplane punishes any guesswork: she sinks about an inch for every 200 pounds added, which is too little to judge by eye from the dock. The plans give builders a better test than any scale. The sailor sits aboard while a second person watches from outside: the bow should sit about 50 mm below the surface against the mark drawn on sheet 2, and the transom should be just kissing the water. Lead goes in, or comes out, until both marks read true, a check worth repeating every season as more gear finds its way aboard and shifts the trim. A boat brought up short of that line sails wrong before she sails light: she is tender underfoot, slow to find her bite to windward and reluctant to point. Dave Bolduc told one hesitant new owner in 2004 not to hesitate: ballast her all the way up to her full cruising displacement, since a Paradox "loves the weight." Keeping the chine runners submerged as deep as possible is what buys her the ability to point at all.

Lead is not the only material that has gone into a Paradox's bilge, though it remains the fleet's overwhelming first choice, for a reason that comes down to simple density. A given weight of lead takes up roughly a quarter of the space the same weight of concrete would need, which matters on a boat this tightly packaged. Stephen Yahn, a Puget Sound builder, launched his own Paradox, Ironsides, in October 2008. He bought his way around the problem instead of solving it: seventy-nine pre-cast concrete bricks from a garden store, five pounds apiece, just under four hundred pounds total, for about twenty dollars. The bricks matched the plans' required centre of gravity well enough, but not their gyrational inertia. A lighter, less dense material rolls a hull faster than the same weight in lead would, a trade Yahn accepted deliberately. He kept a reserve of lead billets from an earlier boat on hand in case trim ever called for it. Steel plate and old gym weights have both been weighed and mostly set aside. Appendix C gathers the other boats' measured ballast, built heavy or light, lead or something else entirely.

Little Jim: seventeen years of trading lead for gear

Little Jim went into the water in April 2003 carrying the plans' full ballast: 70 kilograms of water in her two built-in tanks and 180 kilograms of lead in the bilge, a loaded total of 640 kilograms (1,410 pounds). Within a year her owner, Alastair Law of Yeovil, England, had worked the lead down to a precise figure, 120 kilograms, 96 of it just forward of one bulkhead split evenly to each side and the rest behind another, a number he rechecked every spring as gear piled up and changed the boat's overall weight.

Then the water tanks began to fail him. Their screw-in inspection covers kept leaking, and by 2007 Law had given up on water ballast altogether. He replaced it with permanent lead, keeping back only ten kilograms of bottled water for fine trim. A folding bicycle came aboard around the same time, the last big piece of gear he ever added, and as it and other kit took over part of the ballast's job the fixed lead kept falling: to sixty kilograms, then to thirty-six. A storm anchor or a good battery does the same ballasting work while actually being useful, he reasoned, so why carry dead weight when gear would serve instead. By 2010 he had finally found inspection hatches that held properly, and water ballast came back for good, now pumped in and out electrically rather than carried as a fixed load.

Seventeen years after launch, describing the boat to the Amateur Yacht Research Society in 2020, Law reported the lead settled between fifty and seventy kilograms. The rest of the design's weight was carried by a battery, three anchors and their chain, a week's tinned food and the bicycle, every one of them doing double duty. He called her, by then, an old man's boat: sailing so undemanding that winding the trailer winch ashore was the most strenuous part of a whole cruise. No other Paradox's ballast has been tracked so long.

Stability, stated exactly

The plans themselves carry a stability curve, and more than one builder has had sight of it over the years. Asked on the mailing list in 2015 whether anyone knew a Paradox's angle of vanishing stability, Alastair Law answered in one line: "The stability curve that came with the plans says 165 degrees." That is the designer's own figure, reached before any builder had a finished hull to test it against. Elliott's own first article on the subject, back in 1996, had guessed at something lower, about 120 degrees. Later builders read the plans' actual curve rather than estimating from a sketch, and corrected it upward to the figure that has stood ever since.

It did not stay unchecked for long. Bert Vercauteren, a Belgian boat designer running his own boat-design software, modelled a Paradox from the plans in a program called Nautilus at the start of 2004. He reported back to the list that the program confirmed Matt Layden's stability calculation was "100% correct," provided the boat's centre of gravity sat as low as Layden believed it did: about 120 millimetres above the design waterline. Vercauteren doubted Layden had used a computer for the job himself: a full hand calculation of a hull's stability curve is, in his words, "a monstruous task" rarely attempted before computers made it straightforward. What the software confirmed was not a new number. It was that the number already on the plans held up under an outsider's independent check.

The clearest account of why a boat with no heavy external keel can carry a figure like that at all came from entirely outside the Paradox community. Sven Yrvind, the Swedish designer who had sailed Layden's own boat for weeks at a time and become a close friend, wrote up the mechanism for the Amateur Yacht Research Society's journal Catalyst in October 2008: "Unlike the ordinary cruising boat, Paradox has no heavy ballast keel making her stability obvious. Matt uses less obvious means, like her cruising load kept low and her flat bottom, which, a bit like a multihull, moves a lot of buoyancy to lee at small angles of heel. At large angles of heel, Paradox's righting moment comes from a good height-to-beam ratio and the buoyancy of her superstructure. This shape gives Paradox positive stability at up to 165 degrees of heel, better than most conventional keelboats." Two mechanisms, not one: form stability from the flat bottom doing the work at small angles of heel, and the tall, narrow cabin's own buoyancy taking over once she is far over. Yrvind had no stake in selling the design, and the figure he arrived at by his own route was the same one already on the plans.

The one measured trial on record came at the Beale Park Thames Boat Show in June 2009, where Little Jim stood as part of a Dinghy Cruising Association display. Law's friends rigged a 25-pound spring balance to the masthead and hauled it down. In full cruising trim, with the sail hoisted and Law himself at the helm, they pulled until the water stood a couple of inches below the hatch. The balance ran straight off its own scale. "Unfortunately the reading was well over 25 pounds so I still don't have an accurate value," Law wrote at the time. What the test proved was not an angle but a force: that a correctly ballasted Little Jim needed more pull than a hand-held 25-pound gauge could register to hold her down at 90 degrees, and that released, she came back up on her own. Law recalled the same afternoon eleven years later, in an Amateur Yacht Research Society talk that draws on memory rather than a fresh measurement. He worked the reading into an implied righting moment of "well over 350 foot-pounds": more, he said, than it takes to undo the wheel nuts on his car.

Four knockdowns

Nick Bell, building Thor in South Australia, asked Adelaide University's engineering department before he ever launched her whether to bolt his lead along the inside of the hull, outboard against the chines, or keep it centred low in the bilge. Outboard, he was told, improves the dynamic feel of a roll underway but does nothing for the boat's static righting moment, since each side's weight simply balances the other as she heels. What decides whether a capsized boat comes back up is only that the heavier side ends up on top once she is over, wherever in the bilge that weight happens to sit. Bell bolted his lead outboard anyway, for the feel of it at sea, and the knockdowns that followed gave him every chance to find out whether the advice held.

Little Jim's knockdown off Hurst Narrows in 2003 came with a telling aftermath: across the following six years of ordinary sailing, the only real water to reach her bilge was from that one event, about two litres of it. Everything else was no more than the odd splash a sponge could handle. A second knockdown came later still, broadside onto breaking surf, with the same result. What a knockdown actually looks like from inside a Paradox while it is happening is best shown by Bell's own boat, sailed on the other side of the world more than a decade later.

He sailed Thor into four knockdowns between 2016 and 2018, all in open water. The first, in February 2016, came in about 25 knots of wind: she went over, lay briefly on her side, popped back up unaided, turned into the wind and sat hove to until Bell got the sail and tiller sorted out again. The second, a year later, was worse. Cutting a corner close to a cape in South Australia's Spencer Gulf, he was hunting for a favourable current on an otherwise slow, light day. He ran into a wave that broke just five metres off, parallel to the hull, in water barely deep enough to float her. It rolled Thor hard onto her side while Bell stood in the open hatchway, holding on. She came up immediately, pushed a little toward shore with her masthead briefly under water, and her sail sprang straight back upright as the wave passed. "The boat righted itself immediately, God bless the ballast and the rugged design," Bell wrote to the mailing list a few days afterward. The third, during a wooden-boat regatta some months later, was Bell's own doing: he set the boat up to take a sharp wave abeam under full sail, next to the judges' boat, to see what she would do. She rolled to what Bell judged almost a right angle, sat for a second and rolled abruptly upright again. The fourth found him instead. He was sailing in shallow water at the end of a day out, hatch open, an anchor laid ready and a fishing rod out for dinner. He never heard the wave that hit until it broke. Thor rolled past ninety degrees, her sail buried and the open hatch scooping water into the bilge, as the wave carried her along for several seconds before letting go. She came up hard enough to bruise him against the hatch side.

None of the four called for anything beyond holding on until the boat decided the knockdown was over, not a sea anchor or any other deliberate heavy-weather tactic. Only the third was Bell's own doing; the rest found him.

The two failures that pushed Layden toward this kind of hull in the first place were Terrapin's knockdown off Maine, saved by her own buoyant mast, and Swamp Thing's rollover in the Gulf Stream. Paradox inherited the lesson rather than the failures: a hull meant to be markedly unstable fully upside down, and just as markedly determined to come back from anything short of that.

The limit

No stock Paradox, correctly ballasted, has ever been recorded going all the way over. Builders who have proposed hedging against the possibility, with a bolt-on keel or a buoyant mast fitted with its own air bottle, have mostly been talked out of it. "You are trying to solve a problem that doesn't exist," Alastair Law told one such newcomer in 2016. "The worst that will happen is a 90 degree knock-down, but the boat will be up again before you can do anything." Thor came closer than any other boat on record to proving him wrong, and even she stopped well short of it, by her own owner's judgement.

Self-righting is not a law of physics a Paradox gets to keep regardless of what happens aboard her. It is the outcome of a short list of choices, each one checked against the others: a narrow hull rather than a wide one, fixed lead and water carried low in the bilge rather than weight the crew supplies by leaning out, and a cabin that stays closed. Graham Byrnes found the edge of that list directly. Sailing a visitor's Enigma, Layden's own lighter sister design, at his shop in North Carolina in March 2016, he took a bad jibe in strong wind. The boat capsized. Enigma shares Paradox's chine runners and much of her hull form, but not her ballast: she relies on sand, gear and her crew's own weight to hold her down, rather than fixed lead and a water tank. "It was windy and during a bad jibe she capsized and came close to a complete inversion," Byrnes wrote afterward. "It would have been easy to swim out through the submerged hatch..." He stayed in, mainly to keep a phone dry, with enough of an air pocket to breathe comfortably. The boat did not come back up on her own. His companion worked her onto her side from outside. A dinghy came alongside, the sail came down and she was bailed out with a cut-down water bottle as the dinghy towed her back to the dock.

Her hatch, when Byrnes went over, was open. Nearly every knockdown on record happened to a closed boat, ballasted as the plans call for. Take either assumption away, even on a sister design built around the same chine-runner idea, and a boat that is supposed to right herself from 165 degrees without being asked can instead lie on her side until somebody from outside helps her up. A Paradox sailed as designed keeps both halves of that bargain at once: the weight that holds her down, and the hatch that stays shut over her while she does her work. A boat that rights herself must also be closed.
