Chapter 13

Vent trunk

Alastair Law was seasick most of the way across, bent over a bucket, while Little Jim beat into a tide race running hard against the wind. Solid water broke clean over the boat again and again. An open boat met with the same conditions would have swamped almost for certain. Not one drop of it reached him inside. He recalled it years later as one of his earliest trips: "when I didn't have any idea what I was doing." Green water crossed the cabin top the whole time, and he spent the crossing "throwing up in a bucket, which is also fairly normal." In six years of sailing her that way the stern opening has let in water only once, about a mug full, while she lay beached with waves breaking against the transom. The real defence turned out to have nothing to do with any baffle. It was a door mat just inside the hatch, catching the sand and water a sailor brings aboard on his own feet. A single box built onto the second bulkhead keeps the sea out while still letting her breathe: the vent trunk.

Four jobs, one box

Stephen Yahn recalled, in a 2008 message to the mailing list, a note in Layden's own capitals on drawing sheet 9 of the plans: the vents, it said, would prevent "SIGNIFICANT WATER INTRUSION in any conditions Including 180 degree knockdown." Whatever comes down the deck opening where the mast passes through, however far over she rolls, none of it is meant to reach the sailor asleep below.

The trunk does four jobs that most boats split among four separate fittings. It holds the mast step and the partners the mast passes through, offset to port, so that water working in around them drains overboard rather than finding the cabin. It carries the one large circular opening in the foredeck that ventilates the whole cabin, with a second vent in the transom carrying the airflow on through the boat. It drains whatever comes down through those openings back out through the hull. Bill Serjeant settled on a 22 mm drain for his own boat, smaller than the plans' nominal inch, and found it carried off a brief spell of force 6 without a wave ever nearing the deck. And inside, on its forward face, it carries the blocks for the furling line and the topping lift, one behind the other, so every line a sailor hauls passes through this one box before it reaches the deck.

Don Elliott reached the vent trunk in the tenth installment of his own construction account for Messing About in Boats, in February 1997, and it stopped him cold: "The more I study this feature of Paradox, the more it interests me. It's actually a plenum chamber, allowing the air to enter the cabin freely at all times, yet ensuring the rain or any other water is prevented from coming inside. All openings in the deck for the mast and control lines entering the interior are not required to be watertight, as they must be on other boats... its only path is overboard." The trunk is drained rather than sealed, which is cheaper and simpler than trying to keep water out altogether.

Bill Serjeant's four operations

Bill Serjeant's second Paradox came to him secondhand in 2013. She had been built as Enuf by Derek Clark, Enuf's builder in Wolverhampton, England. Derek sold her to Robert Biegler, who shipped her to Norway and sailed her out of Trondheimsfjord for several years. Biegler renamed her Minnow and sold her on again in 2013, warning Serjeant before delivery that she leaked somewhere around the vent box. He had never found exactly where. Finding it became Serjeant's first job, and he wrote the search up as a run of surgical "operations."

The mast would not seat fully in its socket. A lump of epoxied woven roving was blocking the way. Serjeant chiselled it out, in his own words removing Minnow's "appendix," and sealed the drainpipe beneath it with epoxy putty. A second operation took him into "the bowels of the boat," where a "ghastly mess of misshapen resin-saturated woven roving," meant to keep water out of the forward locker, was instead letting it straight into the bilge around the drainpipe itself. Out it came, by chisel again, replaced with more epoxy and putty. A third operation, on the trunk's own throat, sewed fresh fibreglass cloth over what Serjeant called "a slash that was haemorrhaging." The fourth and last took him to the drain's exit through the bottom of the hull. He scraped the joint with a penknife blade, by his own description "a biopsy," and found no fracture anywhere along it. He coated the area with epoxy regardless, and wrote the only verdict a secondhand boat's plumbing really allows: "In theory, the boat should no longer leak. In practice we shall see."

Ventilation's trade

Layden drew the trunk for the Bahamas, where a steady draft through a shut cabin matters more than keeping out the cold. Owners in colder water run it the other way. Alastair Law put it plainly: "Here in the UK I have very rarely felt there was too little ventilation. On cold evenings I often resort to stuffing a towel in the vent box to reduce the air flow." Any heater run near that same airflow, a charcoal can or a gas stove with the hatch cracked, risks a sharper danger than a damp cabin: carbon monoxide building up in a sealed space.

At the stern the tiller opening does the same two jobs on a smaller scale, admitting air while letting the tiller through. Jack Gardiner, a Rhode Island carpenter and welder who built Jill, fitted a flexible boot cut from black EPDM roofing rubber, clamped around the rudder. It deflects a shot of water rather than trying to stop it outright, "kind of like a boot you would see on a sports car shifter."

Built before the hull closes

The trunk has no moving part, but it still shapes how the boat gets built. Alastair Law, fitting out Little Jim in a garage too low to swing a mast upright, glued the vent trunk onto the second bulkhead before starting the hull around it. With the trunk already in place he could lay the bulkhead flat on trestles and slide the mast in and out of its partners as many times as the fit needed, rather than lifting it bodily in and out of an assembled hull. He left one side of the trunk open rather than closing it up as the hull went together, so the blocks and lines inside could still be reached once the rest of the boat had grown up around it. Layden's own plans gave the reason in a single line, quoted by Don Elliott while Elliott painted his own boat's interior: do as much inside work as possible while it is still accessible, because after the deck goes on it takes four times as long.

Pete Martin, JoHanna's builder in Cheltenham, asked the list in 2008 how other builders had finished the trunk's side panel, then settled on a hinged hatch of his own, meaning to prove it by pouring buckets of water into the finished box.

The choice of panel, like the choice of drain pipe, is a builder's own to make. The order is not: the trunk goes in with the second bulkhead before the hull closes around it, and that order is the whole trick of building her.
