Chapter 14

Building a Paradox

The plans

There is nothing to loft and no strongback to build once the plans themselves are in hand. Lofting, drawing a hull's curves out full size on a floor before cutting anything, is how most wooden boats still begin; Layden's own dimensioning system gives every part's shape directly, in millimetres, so a builder measures and cuts rather than draws. A strongback, the heavy jig many plywood hulls are built around, is likewise missing, and Don Elliott, who built The Edge and wrote the booklet builders still turn to, explained why in his own words: "Paradox is built without a strongback. It's the best way to build a plywood boat. You can step in its middle, crouch down inside to measure or fit things. You can roll the hull over easily to work on it anyway you like, you have a lot of open space to do your job, besides, a strongback isn't part of the boat. Who needs the extra work?" The sixth sheet of the set gives the cutting layout, which ten sheets of plywood yield which parts, and the order in which those parts go together.

Derek Clark needed little more than that layout to get going. A first-time builder from Wolverhampton, in the English Midlands, he rated himself "a mediocre to moderate woodworker" who had not cut a dovetail in twenty years, and held that the design needed no cabinetmaker's standard of work anywhere on it. He built his own Paradox, Enuf, between 2004 and 2006, favoured plastic-handled secondhand chisels picked up cheaply at car-boot sales over anything that needed power, and closed his own published account of the build with a line still quoted back at nervous first-time builders: "You could make this boat with just an half inch chisel."

A garage, and the order of work

Almost every Paradox gets built in a space barely bigger than she is. Bill Serjeant, working out the sequence for Faith in August 2005 before he had cut a single panel, did the arithmetic plainly: Paradox's beam is four feet, his garage left him only two feet of clearance on either side, and that meant the garage itself, door to door, was about eight feet wide. His conclusion followed from the number: build every small part first, while the garage still has room to swing a plane, and leave the hull itself, which will eventually fill the space entirely, for last. He laminated his deck beams, cut his cabin and hatch beams, and made his spars, tiller and rudder over the winter of 2005, and did not start the hull proper until the following spring.

Alastair Law, building Little Jim in Yeovil in the early 2000s, came back to the same conclusion years later when a newcomer asked what he would do differently on a second build: make all the spars and the rudder first, while the workshop is still empty, so that the day the hull is finished there is nothing left between the builder and going sailing. Derek Clark agreed from the other direction: he had built his bulkheads early and found it frustrating to keep making small parts with a finished hull sitting in the way, and said that if he built another he would do the mast, yard, boom, yuloh, rudder, bottom pintle and boom axle before he touched a bulkhead. Serjeant, Law and Clark reached the same order on their own, three builders in three decades and three countries.

Matt Layden himself gave the rule its sharpest form, in a line Elliott quoted in his own series while painting The Edge's interior: "Do as much 'inside' work as possible while it's accessible. After the deck is on it will take four times as long." The logic runs through the whole build, not only its first weeks. A locker, a plumbing run or a coat of paint is a five-minute job while the top is open to the sky and an hour's crawling misery once it is not.

Modern materials have quietly retired most of the traditional boatbuilder's kit before a Paradox builder has even unpacked it. A finishing plane, a spokeshave or a draw knife has almost no job left on this hull, because epoxy's gap-filling strength means a joint no longer has to be cut to match perfectly. What is left is a short list most builders agree on: a table saw or a circular saw run against a clamped straightedge, for the long accurate cuts a hand saw cannot hold; a disc grinder for shaping and fairing; and a low-angle block plane for the fine work a grinder cannot do.

Wood-and-epoxy construction like this has its own standard reference, The Gougeon Brothers on Boat Construction by Meade and Jan Gougeon, recommended on the mailing list as the most comprehensive epoxy-plywood boatbuilding book available and the text Andrew Graham checked his own frame bevels against while building in Brisbane. The brothers were no strangers to the design's own builders either: both turned up at the Cedar Key messabout in Florida in 2007, the same gathering that drew Matt Layden and Sven Yrvind that year. Alastair Law distilled the book's whole case for the method into a line newcomers still quote back to each other: "always remember that epoxy is stronger than wood so all those gaps in the joints just make it stronger." The power tools make the work faster; the half-inch chisel already proves none of it was ever strictly necessary.

The plywood question

By September 2003 the builders' mailing list had been arguing on and off for two years about whether the plans' plywood specification was good enough, and whether a builder who could afford better should use it, a thread that began with a simple question of what a photographed sheet of scrap actually was. Matt Layden settled the question himself, in a message to the list, one of only two he is known to have written there. The plans call for cheap hem-fir, he explained, not only because it is inexpensive but because it is a renewable resource grown on fast-rotation farms, and because its low density lets a thicker panel be used for no weight penalty. He had no objection to a builder who wanted something better: "If people want to use 'better' materials (generally meaning irreplaceable old-growth tropical hardwood) it won't hurt my feelings if they take 1/8 inch (3mm) off the side planking, but I don't see it as an improvement except to the bottom line. You will save about 20 lbs which is a lot for a canoe or racing dinghy, but is minor in the context of a 1400 lb, 5-knot cruiser." Then he offered the only evidence that mattered to him, his own boat's service record: ten years old by then, two thirds of that time afloat in Florida and the rest ashore uncovered in the sun and rain, her topsides and decks repainted twice, her bottom once, her interior partially once, and otherwise needing almost no maintenance. "She is in perfect shape barring some scratched paint," he wrote. "I have to say I'd be glad if she were made of more rot-resistant plywood, but she's not and she's doing fine." The same letter weighed cored composite, aluminium and steel on their merits before he set them all aside for plywood.

That closed the argument for most of the list, though not for everyone. Builders in wetter climates still sometimes pay the premium for a check-resistant hardwood ply, and the trade they are making, a little more money for a little less annual sanding, is exactly the trade Layden described rather than one he recommended against.

Simas Butavičius, building in Lithuania, met the same trade-off from the opposite direction. Unable to get the plywood species the design assumes, he built in Baltic birch instead, a heavier wood he thinned to twelve millimetres on the bottom, nine on the sides and six on the deck to bring the weight back down, and half-joked that the result was his own "Sport Edition."

Laying out the cut

Ten sheets of plywood have to yield every part of the hull, and the cutting diagram on sheet 6 packs them tightly enough that at least one seam gives trouble on nearly every build. The side panel, the transom and bulkhead 2 are drawn sharing the same sheet, and the fit is close enough that Alastair Law and Don Elliott spent two days in the builders' mailing list's first summer, July 2001, working out by how much: the side panel stands about 670 millimetres tall against the transom's 510, and at worst the two overlap by some two and a half centimetres. Their fix, moving bulkhead 2's own butt joint fifty millimetres off centre, is the one Elliott then wrote into his series and the one the plans' own backup CD repeats as an alternate layout.

The identical sheet caught out a Cornell mathematician named Matthew Noonan five years later, on the second day of his own build in Ithaca, New York, and he wrote up his evening with disarming honesty: "There seems to be a problem with the layout for the sheet with the side panel, transom, and bulkhead #2. The CD that came with the plans had an alternate layout for this panel, but I couldn't get it to work either. In the end, I moved the butt joint on bulkhead #2 to make things fit. Later I noticed that Don Elliott suggested the same thing in his Paradox construction booklet. I could have saved myself some hassle by checking there first."

Joining the short panels into long ones raises the same question every plywood boat asks: a scarf, a long diagonal joint that lets two sheets act as one, against a simpler butt block taped over the seam. Layden himself came down on the side of the scarf, in a note on his own next boat's construction pages in 2005: "After doing these taped butt joints that everybody says are real easy, I confirm my personal belief that glued scarfs are faster, easier, cheaper and bend fairer. I expect either kind is plenty strong." Builders who try both tend to agree with him, though modern epoxy has made the difference smaller than it once was: a scarf cut a little short, or planed a little unevenly, still glues up stronger than the wood around it, for the same reason a gap in a dry-fit bulkhead joint does.

Bending the chine log

If one step in the whole build has a reputation, it is this one. A chine log is the strip of solid wood that runs the length of the hull where the side meets the bottom, giving the plywood something to nail and glue to along that curve and giving the chine runners something solid to bolt through. The plans call for it 38 millimetres thick, and persuading a piece that thick to follow the hull's compound curve, bending along its length and twisting through its depth at the same time, has defeated more builders' patience than any other single task.

Derek Clark met it in Wolverhampton in the English autumn of 2004, building the boat that would become Enuf. He had cut two lengths of sapele, each a modest 19 millimetres rather than the plans' 38, reasoning that the extra thickness looked like nothing more than a wider gluing surface. The wood "curves ok in the length but very reluctantly in the depth," he reported to the list, and he could not see why it needed to be so stout: a boat that had circled the globe twice on nine-millimetre plywood suggested to him that Paradox was "massively over engineered." He fitted it anyway, after a three-week break for illness and a burned-out saw blade. One side went in unglued first, under enough spring tension that it broke free and left a blood blister on his hand. He left it clamped for days, hoping the wood would learn its new shape, and when he finally announced the first log fitted, he could only manage a weary one-line post; the second, a few days later, got the same one-line treatment, with no picture and a joke that readers should use their imagination instead.

The difficulty did not go away with better tools or thicker instructions. Alastair Law, fitting his own, single, unlaminated length, found a dry test-fit left so much stored energy in the bent wood that removing it again to glue seemed too risky, so he forced epoxy into the joint with the wood still under tension and called it the most physically demanding part of his whole build. Pete Martin laminated his in two thinner pieces with steaming. He would go on to finish that boat, JoHanna, in Cheltenham in 2011, and she won Watercraft magazine's prize for the most innovative home-built boat that year, admired since as among the best-finished Paradoxes afloat. Another builder, needing forty-four clamps for one side alone, wished he had more.

Twenty years after Derek Clark's fight with his sapele, in 2025, a builder on the other side of the world was still working out the same problem in front of a camera. Benjamin Bloss, who came to the design from a maritime career rather than from woodworking after years as a deck officer on a NOAA survey ship, was building a Paradox called Pathfinder near Seattle. He laminated his chine logs in place from narrower stock rather than attempt to bend a single thick piece, and summed up the job on video much as Law had summed it up in an email two decades earlier: "I cannot imagine getting them in place without either steaming them or laminating them from the narrower pieces... taking the curve of the bottom of the hull there, in two dimensions, I guess three dimensions, is quite challenging. And so while I got through it, it was not the most pleasant experience, but they're on there fairly solidly." Pathfinder's hull still had no bottom on it when that video was posted.

Thirty years after the design was drawn, the chine log remains the one step nobody has found a shortcut through, only different ways to suffer it. One builder avoided it altogether: Jack Gardiner built his own boat, Jill, by joining the plywood panels edge to edge and taping the seams in fibreglass instead of nailing them to cleats, fitting chine logs anyway only for a fairer curve rather than to carry a fastener, and reported no flex in years of sailing her. His own boat answers Derek Clark's 2004 question about whether the logs were worth their bulk; most builders, even so, keep building to the plans as drawn.

The doctrine that forgives a bad joint

What makes all of that survivable is the same principle, repeated in almost identical words across every decade of builders' own accounts: a dry fit does not have to be perfect, because epoxy thickened with a filler holds a joint together regardless. Builders who have had to fill a bulkhead cut to the wrong curve, or a chine log that would not quite close, report the same thing afterward: the fix holds better than the wood around it would have alone. Don Elliott built his own fillets with a tool no more complicated than a smooth ball ground flat on one side and glued to a dowel, drawn along a line of masking tape to leave a neat radiused bead, and claimed he could run twenty feet of fillet with it in under five minutes. The doctrine and the tool together are why a design with no lofting and no strongback can still come out true: the parts do not have to fit perfectly, because the glue that joins them is stronger than what it joins.

Glassing the hull asks for the opposite touch from filleting, and Elliott was emphatic about the difference: "The most important thing is not to float the cloth." A smooth finish coat of fibreglass cloth is laid dry onto a tacky surface and smoothed down, with no further epoxy added until it gels, or the cloth lifts off the plywood and leaves a hilly mess behind. Woven roving, the coarser cloth the bottom is sheathed in, is treated the other way, flooded and left to soak. Three layers of it go onto the bottom, and the plans do not ask for them for strength: the 18 millimetre plywood underneath is already strong enough on its own, and the roving is there purely so that beaching and dragging across sand have something expendable to wear through first.

Epoxy itself is not without its hazards. Gunther Battista, building a twenty-percent-enlarged Paradox called Manatee in Austria in 2013, found his hands reddening and swelling "like the back of a toad" after repeated contact with the resin, and worked through about ten such episodes before the reaction finally eased. The standing advice is to keep bare skin away from mixed epoxy in the first place, working in gloves and long sleeves rather than leaning on any particular skin cleaner afterward, and a published correction from an epoxy manufacturer's own technical advisor warned specifically against the common habit of wiping uncured epoxy off skin with vinegar, since the acid helps carry the resin through the skin's protective layers rather than removing it.

What nobody tells you until it matters

A handful of problems in a Paradox build are not hard once a builder knows about them, and genuinely hazardous if nobody has said so first. Three traps catch a first-time builder more often than anything else in these plans: a hatch that can lock a sailor inside by mistake, a deck beam that comes out of true no matter how carefully it is laminated, and a question about drinking water the plans never quite settle.

A lock fitted to the main hatch is the clearest case. The hasp's hinged half has to be mounted on the aft window, never on the hatch itself, because if the two parts are reversed a sailor closing up from inside can swing the hasp shut and lock himself in, in a cabin that heats quickly once sealed. One builder's warning to the list in September 2003 ended with three blunt words once the mechanics were explained: "It did happen." A simpler fix, a padlock popped through a hole drilled in the hatch rail itself, avoids the hinge and its orientation problem entirely. The hatch's everyday annoyance is smaller but more constant: built wood on wood and then painted, it drags in its runners until a builder waxes the rails with carnauba, the same polish that works on a saw table, after which it slides as it should.

A second surprise waits at deck beam number 1, the forward-most of the laminated beams that give the cabin top and deck their shape. It comes out slightly below fair at the centreline on build after build, in different countries and different decades, because the plans draw that beam's own bulkhead with a camber that does not quite match the others. More than one builder has spent an evening convinced he had made an error in his own lamination, only to find a shim fixes it in minutes and that the next builder along finds exactly the same gap waiting for him.

The third is not a construction problem at all but a question the design poses and never quite answers: whether to trust the two built-in water tanks with drinking water. The plans call for them, lined like everything else in epoxy, and the dispute over whether that is safe has run for twenty years without a final verdict. Don Elliott tried to settle it for his own boat by telephone, and got two different answers from two epoxy makers: West System's own technical line told him no, while System Three told him it was fine. Derek Clark, working out his own tanks in March 2005, called a third supplier, SP Systems, to ask the same question, and got the bluntest answer on record: "For Christ's sake don't do that; at the least you'll be very sick and at the worst you'll go blind." System Three's own position, relayed secondhand on the mailing list later that year by a builder working from memory, was that its epoxy could be used for a water tank if five coats were applied, though the builder who reported it was careful to add that nobody should hold him to the detail. Faced with that spread of expert opinion, most builders simply decline to choose: the tank compartments get built exactly as drawn, because that is still the easiest way to carry 70 litres of ballast low in the bilge, but the water that actually goes into them is sea water or nothing, and drinking water travels aboard in bottles or collapsible containers instead. The plans never resolved the question the way they resolved almost everything else.

What it costs, in months and money

No two builders report their effort the same way, and the honest range is wide. Layden himself had built the original Paradox in three or four months of full-time work in 1993, for about $2,000 in materials. Glen Maxwell, the Florida boatbuilder who had founded the builders' mailing list, put about 1,000 hours into Zoë over two years of on-and-off work, with a hard push in the final three months, and said plainly that she could have been finished in far less time than that. Alastair Law's full accounting for Little Jim, the fullest cost record kept by any builder, came to £2,751.16 for the boat and a further £1,502.12 for a custom trailer, about £4,250 in all in 2003. Jimmy Lu, building his first boat in Taipei, finished Auspice in under eight months, though that figure is a fellow builder's estimate rather than Jimmy Lu's own, offered as proof that a Paradox can go together quickly when the builder has the time to give it. Pete Martin's JoHanna took the warmer months of four building seasons stretched across four calendar years, for about £3,000; asked for his hours, he refused to count them, writing only, "it would scare me." Every one of these builders, when asked directly, gave some version of the same verdict Pete Martin wrote for his own boat's award entry: "Paradox is very different to many designs on this side of the Atlantic and although she looks a simple build there are many complicated features which extend the build time considerably..." None of them called it a cheap way to get a boat, and none of them called it not worth doing.

Building to plan

Don Elliott put the same advice to his own readers at the end of his series that he had already lived by for years: follow the plans, and clear any change with Layden first. Most builders, most of the time, simply follow the plans, and the plans have proved forgiving of that choice for thirty years.

Full-size digital modelling has had far more luck in private hands than in any shared project. Andreas Westerberg, building in Sweden from late 2021, bought his plans from Don Elliott's widow after an email to Dave Bolduc went unanswered, then modelled the whole boat in three dimensions before he cut a single part, test-fitting a full set of bulkhead outlines within weeks. He routed the bulkheads and the rest of the hull's pieces on a CNC machine rather than shaping them by hand, and modelled the mast components the same way, finding them harder to picture correctly from the drawings than the hull had been. He checked the builders' own folklore as carefully as he checked the plans: warned that clamping an epoxy joint too hard can starve it of resin, he tested the claim himself, squeezing a dab of plain epoxy as hard as he could between two scrap pieces of plywood to see whether it would actually fail. For ballast he did not buy lead at all: he dug up an old steel drill rod that had lain buried on his property since the 1980s, about two hundred and fifty kilograms of it, and planned to cut it into bar stock.

A Brazilian member of the Facebook group, José Roberto Canuto, took the same modelling instinct in a different direction in 2023: a model at one sixteenth scale, printed in pieces on a home 3D printer and glued together, worked up from the plans' own study drawings rather than the full set. Her mast and rudder sit in the wrong places and she carries a keel no Paradox plan has ever called for, because he was reconstructing her from a general sketch rather than measuring off sheet 6, but her hull shape and interior are close enough that she sails, in light wind, and he gave the print files away for anyone who wanted to build one too. It is a model boat, not a building aid, but it is the only Paradox anyone outside the design's own builders has been able to pick up and hold.

A kit very nearly shortened the whole process for one builder, and its failure shows why nobody has managed it since. Alec Jordan, of the British firm Jordan Boats, agreed in January 2005 to supply Bill Serjeant a Paradox kit, working directly with Matt Layden on how the panels should be cut. He let it be known that he was also open to licensing kit production of the design, or of any other plywood boat, for a builder elsewhere who wanted to manufacture them. Serjeant's own interest traced back to trying Alastair Law's Little Jim firsthand at Plymouth, an experience that had deepened his wish to own a Paradox of his own. By July the kit was not ready: despite real effort to make every part fit, the panel design and cutting process needed more research than could be resolved quickly, and the plan was dropped. The ten sheets of plywood already bought for it were handed over anyway, cut to the same layout sheet 6 had specified from the start: two eighteen-millimetre sheets for the bottom, the rudder case and bulkhead 3; four twelve-millimetre sheets for the sides, bulkhead 2, the rudder blade, the cabin sole, the shelves and the transom; three nine-millimetre sheets for the decking, bulkheads 1 and 4 and the storage bins; and one six-millimetre sheet for the cabin roof, the hatch cover and a ventilation baffle. Serjeant built from Matt Layden's original plans after all, still believing a kit could be made to work if the bottom were laid up from three thinner layers of plywood rather than the plans' single thick panel. The boat he built from that plywood is Faith, and no second attempt at a Paradox kit has turned up since.

Ballast, paint and the last fittings

Casting the lead is a trade every Paradox builder learns much the same way, little changed since Don Elliott's own account of it in 1996. Scrap lead comes free, or close to it, from a tyre shop's own bucket of old wheel-balancing weights, the kind a shop otherwise pays someone to haul away. Elliott's advice was to ask for the manager rather than whoever is on the floor, to catch him when he is not busy, and to offer payment on the spot rather than simply asking for a favour; doing the asking before spring helps too, since once the weather turns, fishermen start raiding the same buckets to restock their own tackle. The lead is melted in a thick pail set on bricks, over a plumber's torch or a plain fire, and poured slowly into a bread tin buried in the ground, which holds the tin level, draws off heat to speed the cooling and catches any lead that leaks before it reaches the grass underneath. As the lead melts, the steel clips that held each weight onto its wheel rim float free and are skimmed off the surface, leaving the metal clean.

The job is rarely as tidy in practice as the method sounds on paper. Rob Kellock, building Mini in New Zealand, cast four hundred pounds of scrap lead, some of it painted roofing-iron weights, over two days in 2016. After topping up the pocket cast into his own rudder blade and skimming off the dross that rises to the surface of any melt, he ended up with three hundred pounds of usable ingots: a quarter of the metal simply lost along the way. The fumes off painted scrap, he found, were bad enough to need a proper vapour-filter respirator rather than an ordinary dust mask, on top of goggles, no bare skin and gloves worn doubled, rubberised inside and leather outside. He still burned his fingers picking up an ingot before it had cooled. André-François Bourbeau, casting his own ballast from a propane camp stove in 2021, used a slotted spoon for the same job and reported no trouble. A separate, much smaller pour goes into the rudder blade alone: Layden calculated the figure at three kilograms of lead, and Bill Serjeant, Faith's builder, confirmed it came out accurate when he cast to that number.

Paint goes on last, and its job is armour, not appearance. "Paint's most important value today is to protect the epoxy from the damaging rays of the sun," Elliott wrote. "Without paint's protection the sun will remove the epoxy from your boat." A white or light colour is recommended for that reason in strong sun, marine paint lasts three to five years against latex house paint's one or two, and on a hull this size, repainting every year or two is a Saturday's work, not a hardship. The one part builders are warned to paint rather than varnish is the mast, because it is unstepped and stepped at every trailering and takes the knocks to prove it; redoing a varnished mast in paint later, once the varnish has crazed, is a far bigger job than starting in paint. The electrical system is modest to match: Alastair Law worked out a masthead light that runs a tricolour and an all-round white light off the same two wires, switching between them by reversing the battery's polarity, a trick that needs no more current than a pair of LEDs and nothing thicker than speaker wire to carry it.

Getting the plans has never been the hard part. As one newcomer was told plainly, "buying plans is easy, sourcing materials can be a challenge," since ordinary hardware-store plywood will not do and the right marine grade, along with the epoxy, fibreglass and sailcloth, often has to be sought out from a specialist supplier and sometimes shipped in rather than bought over a counter, a delay that has caught more than one eager first-time builder out before a single cut was made.

The boat that comes off this sequence, whichever decade and whichever shed she was built in, is finished in the same sense every one of these builders would recognise: painted, wired, ballasted and waiting on her trailer for the day she goes in the water for the first time.

What you need

A Paradox does not reduce to a single parts list, because so much of the build is technique rather than material, but the fleet's own accounts agree closely enough on quantities to give a real bill of materials.

MaterialQuantity and notes
PlywoodTen sheets in four thicknesses: one 6 mm, three 9 mm, four 12 mm and two 18 mm
EpoxyFive to seven US gallons by the plans, roughly 19 to 26 litres; most builders report using more once fairing and any rework are counted
FibreglassThree layers of 24 ounce woven roving on the bottom; a lighter cloth for the sides, deck and cabin, biaxial cloth where builders have substituted it
TimberSolid wood for the chine logs, stem, spars, rudder and interior cleats; sapele, Douglas fir and similar species have all served
FastenersBronze ring nails in five sizes, about eight pounds in all for a stock boat
BallastAbout 400 lb of scrap lead, cast as ten pigs, plus a further three kilograms for the rudder blade
WindowsPolycarbonate (Lexan) or acrylic (Perspex), in roughly equal use across the fleet, each with its own trade-off between toughness and scratch resistance
PaintMarine paint for three to five years' service, or latex house paint for one or two
ElectricsA simple twelve-volt system: a battery, a two-wire masthead light and whatever instruments a sailor wants
SailDacron, four to six ounces by weight, bought ready-made for a few hundred pounds or sewn at home
