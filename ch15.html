Sailing and living aboard

Her one real vice showed up early, in the coldest conditions anyone had yet taken her out in. David Beard, who had rebuilt a secondhand hull into Whisper the previous year, took her alone up a tidal river for three days of winter sailing in February 2002, with overnight lows in the mid 20s Fahrenheit and sleet already falling before he cast off. The cabin kept him, in his own words, "high and dry" through all of it, even if sitting still for long hours in the cold grew uncomfortable. The lesson came trying to claw off a lee shore. The boat came about too late, would not complete the turn, and was pushed onto a twenty foot rock ledge, grounding her in water cold enough that pushing her off meant wading into it. "Be careful of a lee shore if you have to claw off into the wind," he wrote afterward: the earliest account on record of the one thing a Paradox genuinely does badly.

The slow tack

Beard's grounding was not a fluke of trim or an unlucky gust. The hesitation traces to her wide transom and her outsized rudder, sized and placed to hold a course rather than spin on a dime.

The cure was learned the same way, on different boats: by watching how Layden himself did it. Dave Bolduc, slamming the rudder over on his own boats the way he had always sailed a small dinghy, found the trouble cleared up only after watching Layden sail his original Paradox from inside her own cabin and noticing that he moved the tiller far more slowly. Copying that alone, Bolduc wrote, made the boat sail much better, and the same patience has worked since on Little Cruiser and on Enigma, failing him only when the boat is already moving too slowly or fighting a strong headwind in a short, confused chop. Bill Serjeant learned the same patience from Alastair Law directly, sailing in company in choppy water: as the bow came up into the wind, Law would ease the sheet so the sail fluttered rather than drove, and move his own weight forward into the cabin at the same moment, which brought the boat's centre of lateral resistance forward just enough to carry her through. Ease the helm, move weight forward, keep enough speed on before starting the turn: a Paradox that is handled this way completes her tacks; one handled like a dinghy does not. Part of the surprise, for a sailor coming from dinghies, is that the sail itself never swaps sides on the turn: Paradox's standing lug is built to stay permanently on one side of the mast, so a tack brings the bow through the eye of the wind without the boom and yard ever swinging across the way a Bermudan rig's would, one more reason the whole manoeuvre feels, and has to be sailed, like a small keelboat's rather than a dinghy's.

What the cure looks like under real pressure, rather than as a rule of thumb, shows up in Alastair Law's own account of a hard beat up a quarter mile wide channel in Poole Harbour, in a full Force 5 with a Force 9 forecast closing in behind him. Every tack up that channel mattered, since losing one meant losing ground he could not easily win back, and the technique he settled on never failed him: just before putting the helm over, duck down into the cabin to shift weight forward, and the boat comes through head to wind, in his own words, "with great alacrity." The same channel also showed him the technique's limit. Further up, where the water narrows into the Wareham river proper, the beat became too tight to tack at all, and dropping sail to scull through left him, for a few minutes, unable to make any headway whatsoever against the same wind, let alone hold a heading into it, until a lighter patch finally let him through. Beating up Wareham Channel itself is no problem, he concluded afterward; continuing beyond it into the river is simply impossible, and no amount of technique changes that.

Geoff Davis, the Western Australian who built her in Fremantle, put a harder number on the ceiling above those figures. Fitted with Layden's taller, shorter footed sail, Small Fry could point to within 55 or 60 degrees of the true wind in flat water and a light breeze; sent out to beat for two days straight against 20 to 25 knots of wind and a two metre sea, she gained only about three nautical miles in the right direction before Davis gave up and ran for port. Alastair Law found Little Jim wanted reefing by about 15 knots of wind, and Bill Serjeant, sailing the same stretch of water, put the honest limit on windward work above about Force 5: past that point a Paradox will not make useful progress to windward at all, and the sensible answers are to find shelter, heave to under a sea anchor, or run the engine rather than keep beating. Inside that range, though, she tracks well enough on her own that a sailor alone can cook, read or sleep underway, exactly as Layden did through the long night of the 300 mile race he won.

Running downwind in a strong wind brings a different discomfort. The furling boom carries no vang, so it lifts in gusts and sets the boat rolling heavily from side to side, a trouble Dave Bolduc met sailing Paradox, Enigma and Swamp Thing alike and solved, on a Bahamas passage under Enigma's rig, with a temporary vang improvised from a slit length of PVC pipe clamped over the boom and tied down to the deck.

Heavy weather

None of this keeps a Paradox off open water; it only sets the terms. Dave and Mindy Bolduc, whose Little Cruiser has logged more sea miles under a Layden rig than any other boat afloat, state those terms as plainly as the design itself ever has: "I am not endorsing Paradox or Little Cruiser as offshore sailing craft... they are tough coastal cruisers that can handle their share of bad weather." Elsewhere they put it more bluntly still: "Paradox, Swamp Thing and Little Cruiser are coastal cruisers, and they probably should be snuggled up some creek when it's blowing that hard out there." Everything the record holds about handling real weather in one of these boats sits inside that disclaimer, not against it.

Their practice tested doctrine is simple and repeated by nearly every owner who has had to use it. A sea anchor of five to six feet across holds the bow into wind and sea far better than lying a-hull, which the Bolducs tried on four other boats, monohull and multihull alike, and found uncomfortable even in conditions well short of a real blow. Retrieving the chute is easier than it looks, more like hauling in a wad of wet clothing than fighting a parachute. Self-righting means nobody has ever needed to be strapped into a Paradox during a knockdown; a sailor caught over has, in every account on record, found himself upright and sailing again before he had time to brace for more. What a Paradox cannot do, carrying no jib to back, is heave to in the conventional sense at all, so whichever tactic a sailor chooses has to be worked out before the wind, not during it.

Short of a genuine gale, the Bolducs' standing preference is simply to avoid the fight altogether. Layden's own term for the tactic, by their account, is "bathtub sailing": run for an island's lee and wait the weather out there rather than press on offshore, on the reasoning that "how big can a wave get in a few feet of water?" Alastair Law found the same shelter built into the hull itself. A tide race he once sailed hard against the wind, caught by his own navigational error, left the cabin as dry inside as it had started; he recalled it years later, in another boat on a passage of her own, as one of his early trips, "when I didn't have any idea what I was doing."

A rougher yardstick comes from Bill Serjeant, who crewed a forty foot yacht through a genuine Force 8 in the Bay of Biscay and judged, afterward, that Faith would have given him a rough ride in the same gale but a considerably drier and warmer one, with her short mast and closed cabin working in her favour rather than against her. None of this weather doctrine requires a sailor to stay outside and tend the boat. The cabin is sat in just as readily in a blow as on a calm afternoon, often with a book open, and closing the hatch is as much a comfort as a defence: the Bolducs describe the feeling as driving a convertible, since when it gets rough the hatch simply closes and the boat keeps going, which is also why neither of them bothers carrying foul weather gear at all.

The design's one true open water proving ground is the Gulf Stream crossing to the Bahamas, roughly 65 nautical miles of open sea that the Bolducs have made seven times in Little Cruiser, once in company with Layden's own Paradox. The method they worked out has two parts. The weather half is routing, not raw strength: the Stream itself sets north at up to three and a half knots, so the direct line from Florida would sweep a small boat north of Bimini, and the crossing instead starts well south, from Key Largo or Angelfish Creek, riding the normal wind rotation ahead of a cold front from southeast through south and west to northwest, and never crossing against a north wind, which blows straight against that current and builds short, breaking seas. The second half, and the one that surprises most newcomers, is that the real danger is not weather at all but ship traffic. "The odds of you being hit by something big and fast are rather high if you don't stay out of their way," the Bolducs write, since most large ships run on autopilot and may never see a small boat even on radar. Their method is a hand bearing compass and patience: take a bearing on any approaching ship, check it again a few minutes later and treat an unchanging bearing as a collision course, never trying to cross ahead of a ship's bow. The stakes are not theoretical. A couple were run down at night in the middle of the Stream by another sailboat whose skipper had switched off his own navigation lights to save power and whose lookout never saw them; daylight crossings are now the rule rather than the exception. Dave Bolduc made a solo crossing in 2013 that tested the same bearing and patience method directly among dozens of ships at night, in his lighter Enigma rather than a Paradox and without an outboard aboard; on the way home the wind died twelve miles from land, leaving several hours of sculling to finish the passage under oar alone. The method has had to cope with the full range of what the Stream can do on a given day: one crossing met Atlantic swells as tall as Little Cruiser's own fourteen foot mast and had to be motor-sailed the whole fourteen hours, while another, timed well against the front, took only nine and a half.

The anchor worked from the hatch

Anchoring a Paradox from the bow, the way almost every other small cruiser does it, fights the boat's own logic. The anchor is effectively ballast, stowed low and aft rather than hung off the stem, and Little Cruiser's own experience shows why: an eleven pound Bruce once carried on her bow roller upset her trim enough that only a light lunch hook stays there now, with the working anchors moved below the floorboards. Layden's own method, learned and repeated by owner after owner since, keeps the whole job inside the cabin. He carries his main anchor, an eleven pound Bruce, in a bucket at the stern. To anchor, he drops it straight off the back of the boat and walks calmly forward, paying out the rode as he goes, and cleats it off at the bow; to leave, he hauls the line back to the stern, lifts the anchor into its bucket, and sails off. Glen Maxwell works the same job differently, entirely from the companionway: rode cleated aft, he carries it forward through the bow chock at anchor, then hauls it straight back in from the hatch as he leaves, stowing it in its bag as it comes in. David C. Smith proposed going a step further, with a separate inhaul line clipped to the rode on a ring that rides forward as the rode pays out, so the whole job could be worked from the cockpit without even that much walking about on deck; Maxwell, who had tried his own plainer method and liked it, answered that there was "a lot to be said for the simplicity" of doing without it. Maxwell's own practice on Zoë carries the anchor itself in a canvas ice bag rather than a dedicated well, one bag in the plans' own locker and a second, identical spare stowed aft; leaving Cedar Key one afternoon with a current setting the boat toward the piers, he dropped the bag on the afterdeck to scull clear and watched it slide overboard, still cleated to the stern, just as he reached for it, clearing the pier under sail alone "in front of all the fishermen" while he reeled the whole rig back in by hand. A bungee cord stretched across the afterdeck has prevented a repeat. The standing advice on how many anchors to carry is at least two, generally three, a well regarded primary size being an eleven pound Delta Quickset or Bruce, on the plain reasoning that any anchor can be lost, whether let go by accident or deliberately slipped to avoid being dragged down on by a bigger boat.

However it is rigged, the point of the system is speed and safety rather than elegance, and Alastair Law put a number on it in the Dinghy Cruising Association's Bulletin: "four minutes from dropping the anchor to getting your head on the pillow; ten minutes from bed to sailing." Drying out on a falling tide is just as unceremonious. A flat, heavily built bottom lets a Paradox take the ground upright and without supports, on sand, mud or a riverbank, as a matter of routine rather than an emergency, tested from Patagonia to Western Australia.

Two owners, two trailers

A loaded Paradox weighs close to half a ton even with her ballast reduced for the road, and that weight makes the trailer every bit as much a part of the sailing experience as the boat herself. Two owners, sailing two different trailers, show the whole range of what that means in practice. Bill Serjeant, launching and recovering Faith alone, found it "not the most spontaneous sailboat for launching and recovering from the water, particularly when being managed by a lone crew," and concluded that casual day sailing straight from the trailer was simply not worth the time and effort it cost him. Alastair Law, sailing a trailer built to his own specification, tells a different story from the same design: in his late sixties, he launches and recovers Little Jim single handed in about fifteen minutes "without breaking into a sweat," which means, in his own words, that "I end up sailing much more." The boat has not changed between the two accounts; the trailer has.

Glen Maxwell had already reached a harder verdict in 2002: a popular budget trailer advertised that year for under $500, he judged, "would not even carry an empty Paradox," since a boat loaded for a couple of weeks' cruising would weigh around 1,000 lb; he built his own instead, rated for 2,500 lb and costing $525. His later rebuild of Zoë's trailer shows what the difference is made of: a longer axle and heavier leaf springs rated for 2,000 pounds, the axle remounted on top of the springs rather than under them and the spindles welded to the top of the axle rather than coming out its ends, each change shaving an inch or more off the loaded height until the hull's bottom sat just fourteen inches off the ground, low enough to launch at most ramps without the car's own wheels going anywhere near the water. A stock trailer sized for a boat of five hundred pounds, which is roughly what a bare Paradox hull alone comes to, is simply the wrong tool for a boat loaded for a week's cruising at closer to nine hundred; building to that heavier figure from the start, and carrying a spare tyre as a matter of course, is cheaper in the long run than the alternative. David Beard found out what the alternative costs on an ordinary drive home from a weekend out: a tie-down strap had worked loose, and when he stopped to tighten it he found the winch itself bent and nearly pulled out of its mount, with four or five teeth broken out of its gears. Serjeant, looking back, guessed at part of the difference himself: docking arms fitted either side of the trailer's bunk, which his own lacked, would have caught the hull and held it straight coming on, instead of leaving a lone sailor to steer half a ton of drifting boat by hand down a steep slipway exposed to waves and a crosswise current.

Engine, oar and sail

The argument over an outboard motor has run among builders for two decades and never quite settles, but it drifts in one direction. A heavy motor hung on the stern throws off the boat's balance and her tacking alike: Bolduc found his own 3.3 horsepower, 28 pound Mercury probably too much for Little Cruiser's narrow transom, and preferred a lighter 2 horsepower motor, or the oar, for anything short of a long motoring stretch. The transom mounted mainsheet makes the problem worse than the weight alone: it is almost guaranteed to tangle with whatever sits on the transom beside it, and in the worst case a violent gybe can tear a motor clean off and into the sea. The drift across two decades of this argument runs toward the lightest motor that will do the job, petrol or electric, carried as insurance rather than relied on, and toward keeping as little fuel inside the cabin as the trip allows. Faith, fitted later with a Honda 2.3 four stroke, used it well on longer coastal stretches without ever becoming the boat's main means of getting about. Builders have found several independent fixes for the sheet catching on the rudder head, the mooring cleats or a fitted motor rather than one settled answer, among them a small cap fitted over the top pintle, a wire bridle running from the rudder head to the deck and a brass rod spanning the transom with its own fairlead; none has displaced the others as the standard fitting. Electric power has its own honest limits rather than solving the weight problem outright: a 25 pound battery paired with a small trolling motor is good for roughly four hours at two knots or two hours at three, workable for an emergency but no real substitute for the petrol-and-yuloh combination most owners settle on, and a 2018 field test found a 100 watt solar panel and an 80 amp hour battery adequate for a calm river cruise under electric power but unable to push the boat against a flooded current.

That main means, for owners willing to learn it, is the yuloh: a long, curved sculling oar worked one handed over the stern, facing forward, that Layden fitted to Paradox from the start and that owners have proved again and again is not a calm day trick but genuine auxiliary propulsion. The stroke is a whole body motion rather than an arm exercise, feet spread and knees bent, driving forward from the back leg and leaning into the oar; the free hand is left to hold a water bottle, work the tiller, or read a chart. The usual first attempt, a figure of eight motion copied from sculling a dinghy with a single blade, simply does not work on a yuloh; what works is moving the shaft horizontally and twisting it at the end of each stroke, a knack that typically takes a couple of days to click and months to make truly fluid. Once learned, the same oar converts instantly into a push pole for working off a grounding or poling across a shoal, without changing gear. Dave Gatan, the Keys liveaboard who had owned Swamp Thing, practised the stroke for its own sake on calm nights when he could not sleep, and it served him once for real when he sculled his boat a hundred yards clear of a freighter bearing down on him while becalmed in the Gulf Stream.

The clearest evidence for what that practice buys is still Layden's own hours long stints at the yuloh during the 2003 race, a stamina nobody else in the record has matched. Pete Martin supplies the fullest cruising figure: 140 miles down a river aboard his own JoHanna, ninety of them made under yuloh rather than sail or motor. Little Jim's own owner reports a comfortable two knots all day, enough to scull some fifty of the fifty five miles of the Dinghy Cruising Association's annual Thames raid without the blisters that troubled the conventional rowing boats around him, since sculling lets the body shift position instead of repeating one fixed stroke, and Dave Bolduc's class winning finishes in the 2009 and 2010 WaterTribe challenges, sculling through a night passage against a foul tide at Gasparilla Pass, were made in his lighter Enigma rather than a Paradox.

Food and water

Sailing a Paradox well is one discipline; living aboard one for a season is another, and the fullest published account of it is still the Bolducs' own, set down in 2001 under the title "Secrets to Cruising in Small Boats" and refined in a trip inventory they updated again in 2007. Little Cruiser carries no refrigeration of any kind on her three month winter trips to the Bahamas, and the whole provisioning system is built around that one fact. Fresh produce keeps for weeks in open baskets rather than a cooler; condiments travel in squeeze bottles rather than jars that would need chilling; everything else is canned or dried, with dried fruit, beans and jerky alongside, and fresh meat, fish and vegetables bought locally and eaten within a day or two rather than stored. Water comes aboard split across many small containers rather than one large tank, chosen so that a single contaminated jug never spoils the whole supply, and so that the boat's trim shifts gradually and predictably as the crew drinks their way down through the stores rather than all at once. The toe rails channel rain straight into the built in tanks without any separate catchment gear, and a hand pumped reverse osmosis watermaker rides along for the handful of truly isolated anchorages where neither rain nor a shore tap will do. Many Paradox owners choose otherwise, wary of what the built-in tanks' epoxy lining might leach into standing water, and keep those tanks for ballast alone while carrying their own drinking water separately. The 2007 list runs to the small and the odd alongside the practical: two pet bird cages, a hand cranked coffee grinder, an mp3 player holding 170 songs on a single disc, twenty individual one quart water jugs topped up by a few larger ones for about twelve and a half gallons in all, four anchors from four to twenty five pounds and a thirty six inch sea anchor, everything packed, in Mindy Bolduc's own description, the way a grocery stockboy racks a case rather than left to rattle around loose. Cooking runs on a gimballed Gaz stove burning one pound cylinders small enough to tuck into the bilge, ten of which see the Bolducs through a whole winter, with a three and a half quart pressure cooker doing most of the work: rice in about ten minutes, a bean or vegetable stew in twenty, which matters when the fuel budget for a three month trip fits in a space the size of a milk crate. Salt water goes straight into the pot for anything that does not mind the taste, spaghetti among it, to spare the fresh supply. Trolling supplements the stores wherever the coastline allows it: a cheap vibrating "Atom Bomber" lure takes striper bass, bluefish and weakfish along the American east coast, while a heavier rig modelled on commercial albacore jigging, a tuna feather trailed behind a homemade shock absorber of surgical tubing, has filled a freezer with sierra, bonito and dorado on the Pacific side.

Heat and condensation

Keeping warm asks less of a Paradox than most newcomers expect, and the fleet's own experience settles the question boat by boat.

Matt Layden set the baseline himself. One winter he let Little Cruiser freeze into a saltwater pond on Cape Cod and simply stayed put, warm enough through it on body heat alone, sun through the windows and the two inches of foam lining her sides and deck. Dave Bolduc only learned of the stunt years later, stripping old paint from Little Cruiser's sides and finding cuts up forward in the shape of a bow wave: physical evidence that Layden had eventually sailed her back out through the ice.

The owners who went looking for a heater are, almost without exception, the ones whose boats were built without that foam. Bill Serjeant built Faith to the plans in every other respect, and her second owner, James Hall, made up the difference with a charcoal heater built from a two inch copper pipe with the ends brazed shut, no bigger than a beer can, standing in an old army mess tin to catch the ash and burning a teaspoon of charcoal or wood scrap at a time through a length of flexible exhaust tubing for a flue. It raises the cabin only a few degrees, which is usually all a boat this size needs, and Hall kept it clipped to the cabin side on a quick release cleat so a passing wake could not tip it over. Asked once why he needed a heater at all, he put the difference down to the boat herself rather than his own hardiness: "my little boat is fully insulated by the junk all around the inside."

Glen Maxwell left the plans' foam out of Zoë and reached for a different fix. On a cold Cumberland Sound cruise, with the overnight temperature in the low thirties, he set an inverted clay flower pot over the stove for about five minutes, "all I could stand," and the cabin stayed comfortable well after the flame itself went out, at the cost of heavy condensation on the windows by morning once every vent had been plugged for the night.

Alastair Law, sailing with the plans' own foam fitted, has never needed a heater at all, through snow and through a night that left an inch of frost on the deck. A coffee made before bed is enough to see the cabin through until morning. André-François Bourbeau, wintering in the Saguenay as ice begins forming on the lakes, heats Iota with a propane heater fitted with two separate automatic shutoffs, one for stale air and one for a tip, a safety margin he calls priceless; the hatch still has to stay open to let the heat regulate itself.

The pattern the fleet shows is plain: insulation beats heating, with all the hazard and fuss a flame brings into a cabin this size, and the plans already leave room for a gimballed stove that does the job twice over. Every one of these heaters carries the same warning. Any flame burning inside so small a sealed cabin produces carbon monoxide, odourless and dangerous, and the gas gives no notice before it disables a sleeping sailor who will not wake again to save himself. A chimney, a vent left open and a cheap monitor are not optional extras on a boat this size. They are the only real safeguard.

The head

Sanitation aboard a Paradox is unglamorous and universal: a bucket, used and stowed, the same practice Matt Layden uses himself. The Bolducs wait for a shore facility where one is to be had, and reach for the bucket only when that is not an option. Bailing works the same way, kept simple: a hand pump discharges through the tiller opening rather than through the vent trunk's own drain, which only clears water while the boat is actually moving and was never meant as a bilge pump.

Dave Skaife's comfortable microcruising

No one has written more carefully about what it takes to live well aboard a Paradox than Dave Skaife, the Hawaiian builder who sailed four engine free seasons between 2014 and 2017, from Hawaii to the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway to the Bahamas and on to Cuba, setting down what he learned in a series of essays he called, simply, comfortable microcruising. His padded cell post describes lining the cabin floor with interlocking foam puzzle mats, three quarters of an inch thick and cut to the shape of his own floorboards, durable, waterproof and grippy enough to crawl and sleep on for a season. His own proudest fitting, which he named the Universal Paradox Equipment Mounting System, is a set of simple rails run around the cabin at the top edge of the side bins, onto which a phone, a GPS, a VHF radio, a first aid kit and a spotlight can all be clipped, moved or stowed in seconds; he was surprised to find that even in rough conditions "things never jumped off the rails," and the system let him reconfigure the whole cabin for whatever the day asked of it rather than build in fixed mounts he might later regret. Reflective window covers, folded Mylar and bubble wrap rated at about R3 a layer, cut the tropical sun without giving up the cabin's light entirely; he traded cotton clothing for quick drying polyester once he realised how much faster it dried overnight in a boat with nowhere to hang a wet shirt. He chose the design in the first place, by his own account, because "I consider Matt Layden an evil genius when it comes to boat designs, he thinks so far outside the box that you forget there was a box," and four seasons living with the consequences of that choice did nothing to change his mind.

His most useful contribution, though, is the honest coda he wrote after a summer spent on the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway in 2015. "Don't do it! Or at least try and avoid it," he opened, and gave his reasons under their own headings: heat, since "the heat had entirely too much sway over many daily decisions" and even a swim offered no real relief from water in the high eighties; the absence of other cruisers, since he recalled sharing not a single anchorage the whole trip; and bugs, deer flies, mosquitoes and biting midges that forced him to close the boat up before dusk and rely on the vent fan's positive pressure to keep them out. None of it was damage to the boat itself, which came through the whole summer with no chafed lines, no broken hardware and no torn sails, every mark on her traced to a powerboat's wake against a fixed dock rather than to sea conditions. It was simply too hot, too buggy and too lonely a season to recommend, and he said so plainly.

Who fits aboard

There is no fixed weight limit on who can sail a Paradox, since the ballast that holds her down to her lines can be reduced pound for pound to suit a heavier sailor; the real constraint is height, set by the cabin's fixed geometry rather than by anything a builder can simply add more lead to solve. Owners describe a boat that fits well as "wearing the boat," the way a kayak fits its paddler rather than merely carrying him, and a cardboard mock-up of the cabin, built on the garage floor before a sheet of plywood is cut, is the standard and repeatedly reinvented way to test whether a given sailor's own frame will fit before committing to the build. Anders Nygaard, a Norwegian builder six foot five, 196 centimetres, measured himself against a cardboard mock-up of his own and found he did not fit a stock hull; he built his own boat ten percent larger throughout so the salon would fit him. The Bolducs put the design intent plainly: Layden's boats were "originally designed as solo boats with room for the occasional guest." Day sailing two up works well enough, but most couples who try sleeping aboard settle for camping ashore instead and use the boat for sailing and cooking by day.

Skaife, looking back on his own four seasons of exactly this kind of living, put it best: "I'm just comfortable, sustainably comfortable." That particular comfort, learned boat by boat and season by season rather than bought ready made, is what let these sailors keep going.
