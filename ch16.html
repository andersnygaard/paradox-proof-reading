What Paradoxes have done

Cape Leeuwin and north to Shark Bay

Geoff Davis set out from Fremantle in February 2011 to do what no other Paradox is recorded as having attempted: take Small Fry around Cape Leeuwin, the point on the continent's southwestern tip where the Indian Ocean meets the Southern Ocean and the weather of both. The approach alone took the better part of a week. He sailed out through the bridges at Fremantle by night, on a favourable wind and tide, and made good progress down the coast to Bunker Bay, near Cape Naturaliste, where he anchored. Bad weather held him there a second night and slowed him further; nearing the cape, conditions turned hard enough that he rode them out some distance offshore before working in to Hamlin Island, about ten miles short of his goal. There his anchor dragged in the night and left the boat aground on the beach. He freed her the next morning and pressed on south.

The rounding itself, on 22 February, took several hours of repeated attempts against a southeast wind gusting to about 20 knots and a building sea, broken by one lull that let him carry more sail and finally claw around the last stretch of coast. Of the whole passage, not the rounding alone, he wrote to the builders' mailing list: "I found the whole area to be the most menacing place I've ever sailed." Small Fry had sailed in stronger wind and bigger seas before, always well out to sea; what made this different was everything happening close in at once, reefs and islands scattered everywhere, waves breaking where no reef was visible, conditions changing by the minute and an ugly lee shore under her stern the whole time. Once round, she anchored at Seal Island on a rock bottom for two nights, riding out a 20-knot gust in the island's lee on the second and wishing, Davis admitted, that he had brought more chain and a spare anchor.

Over the years that followed, Small Fry ranged the shoal coast north of Fremantle on her own terms, and tested it harder than the rounding alone suggests. Her worst conditions on record ran to four or five metres of sea: her reefing gear broke loose through a string of accidental gybes, and she still made three to four knots under bare poles alone. Clear downwind, Davis raised full sail again for a reach toward shelter and watched the GPS repeatedly register double-digit boat speeds, in what he called "moments of semi control." She brought him through it, he judged afterward, "quite happily enough," with some acknowledged luck alongside the boat's own capability. Cruising, he carried about 100 kilograms of lead and a 30-kilogram battery, enough to put her down on her lines before he even stepped aboard, with no ill effect on sailing beyond a slower response to gusts and an anchorage made comfortable even where otherwise unremarkable. Asked how dry she stayed through conditions like these, he gave a full accounting. No water comes through her hinged hatch at all. Water only finds its way in through the rudder hole in the transom, and only when a wave breaks clean over the stern; on one such occasion he bailed about ten litres after a dawn-to-dusk sail in rough weather, never more than a discomfort. Going to windward, even in very rough seas, no water gets aboard at all. He would not suggest changing the design over it: "Matt has things just about right."

Endurance ran further than any one passage. The longest she went without dropping anchor was six days, helped by a self-steering trick worked out through trial: a loop of elastic "occy strap" cord added to the tiller line let her hold a course on almost any point of sail, except running downwind faster than about four knots. On an earlier two-week cruise he had used only about a quarter of the food and water he carried, which left him reckoning a stock Paradox could supply a solo sailor for a month, and perhaps for two with careful packing. A full month alone at anchor in Shark Bay taught him a simpler discipline: eat when hungry, sleep when tired, add a layer once the sun goes down, obvious rules that take real, repeated attention once there is no longer anyone ashore to remind him. He was not entirely alone even then. Other boaters checked in on Small Fry when she lay at anchor there, and well out to sea a large fishing boat once altered course to come alongside, her skipper's first guess being that she was a stray Indonesian vessel far from home. A visit further east to Esperance showed water that "did look rather enticing," though he doubted he would ever sail Small Fry that far; trailering her there seemed the more sensible plan.

Of Shark Bay itself, where she dried out overnight as a matter of routine rather than incident, Davis wrote that it "must have been created with Matt's design in mind," recommending it "to anyone looking for an exciting wilderness in which to go exploring with an ultra shoal draft sailing boat." Jurien Bay's anchorage, open only to the shallowest of boats, he judged "so good to cruise in a paradox" for the same reason. In the Abrolhos Islands, thirty miles offshore and visited by few boats her size, a Brahminy Kite once settled near her and then took off. The force of it rocked her, Davis noted, about as much as a sailor moving around on deck: a small, exact measure of how little force it takes to disturb a boat riding that light. The reef country along this stretch is safe to explore only in light, offshore conditions. Even then the worst a flat-bottomed, keelless Paradox risks is a few scratches to her bottom paint, where a boat with a keel could never have followed her in at all.

She made the longest of these passages in 2014, 370 nautical miles (685 kilometres) up the coast from Fremantle to Shark Bay in five days. There she had an encounter only a boat this small and this far from anywhere could have had. Anchored off a beach ringed with four-wheel-drive camps, the crew of Ashiki, a 35-foot junk-rigged schooner cruising the same coast, watched what they took at first for a dinghy sailing toward them out of nowhere, far too small to have come any real distance. It rounded up alongside and its skipper called across: "Hi there, I saw your Benford Badger and have read your blog." It was Geoff Davis, five days out of Fremantle, with 110 litres of fresh water and two months of food stowed aboard, steering by a line that ran around the cabin to the rudder, which the watching crew could only describe as "from bed." Leaning out from the mast to test how stiff she was, one of them got her to heel about 25 degrees before she firmed up and would go no further; they put her displacement, by eye, at something like 620 kilograms. Before he sailed on, Davis mentioned that Cape Leeuwin was already behind him, the first of the great southern capes rounded. The other four, he said, he would leave to somebody else. On reflection he suspected he had found his own limit at Leeuwin rather than the boat's: Sven Yrvind, he supposed, would likely have just kept going.

Two Paradoxes to the Scilly Isles

Bill Serjeant, Faith's builder, left the River Crouch in Essex in early April 2008, bound for the Isles of Scilly off England's southwestern tip, further than the design had then been sent on an English coast. Five days out she lay weatherbound at Ramsgate with snow in the forecast, and Serjeant reported to the builders' mailing list that he had one consolation: "but I have a Woolworths Hot water bottle - my trump card!" The passage on down the coast ran through Dover, a first lock at Eastbourne and a gale-bound spell at Brighton, where an ailing outboard kept her moving when little else could. Asked why a boat built to sail and scull needed an engine at all, Serjeant answered from exactly where that question found him, stuck: "There's no way I would be at Brighton without the engine." The honest answer served well enough to carry her on past the Shingles Bank, into Poole Harbour and on to Lymington on 16 April. The blog Serjeant kept of it all drew readers well beyond the Paradox community: a West Wight Potter owner in the San Francisco Bay area, following the reports from the other side of an ocean, admitted the cruise had him seriously weighing a small-boat voyage of his own.

Off Sussex that same week, five miles out, a solo sailor circumnavigating Britain in a nineteen-foot Mirror dinghy spotted her under sail and could not place what he was looking at: a lobster-pot marker, he first thought, then a square-rigger, before working out she was a small boat making excellent progress. He asked the mailing list, by way of his own unrelated circumnavigation blog, who she was and where she was bound. At Lymington a local sailor came aboard for coffee, his first time inside a Paradox, and found her "very dry warm and snug," which he judged "just about the opposite of outside." Working on west through Dartmouth, Salcombe and the Tamar, Faith reached Plymouth in time to anchor off Cawsand and watch the start of the Artemis Transat on 11 May; Alastair Law, Little Jim's builder in Yeovil, launched her at Mount Batten the next day to join Faith for the push west, a natural companion for a stretch of water neither boat had crossed before. A reader following the blog from New Zealand judged the cruise probably a record for the design: nobody but Matt Layden himself had likely sailed a Paradox further, in time or distance. Gavin Atkin, a boat-design writer with no part in the voyage, followed the cruise and logged it on his own weblog as it happened.

The two boats crossed the shallows between Samson and Crow Point on a falling tide on 18 May, "both boats scraping their rudders on the golden sand below," and anchored off Tresco. Her storage proved ample for a cruise of this length: the forepeak was never completely filled, and a spare two-gallon can of drinking water went untouched. The lazarette's own pump-fed can was simply topped up every second day instead. Curious visitors asked the same two questions at anchor for days afterward: how old the boat was, and how far she had come. A week later a force 9 gale warning for New Grimsby sent both boats' anchors down fore and aft; Faith was aground within minutes as the wind built, and did not come off again until midnight. Three days after that they sailed on to St Agnes, then Newlyn, and rounded the Lizard. With the wind failing, they towed each other in company from Fowey to Plymouth on Serjeant's outboard, the one working engine between two boats built to sail and scull proving, for once, the more reliable machine of the pair. They hauled out at Queen Anne's Battery on 4 June. By Serjeant's own cruise log the whole trip ran to nearly two months and about 800 miles from the Crouch; the abridged account he later published in Messing About in Boats put the same passage at some 500 sea miles. They had hoped to be back at Plymouth by 31 May for the start of the Jester Azores Challenge, and the weather cost them that. What they reached instead mattered more: two Paradoxes, in company, at the farthest point either had sailed, hauled out forty-five minutes later with the mast unstepped, the ballast drained and the trailer ready for the road.

Hilo to Kauai, and on to Cuba

Dave Skaife, who built his own Paradox in Hawaii, spent the summer of 2014 working his way up the main island chain under sail and oar alone, with no engine aboard at all: Hilo, on the Big Island, by way of Lanai, Molokai and Oahu, to Kauai. It was the first of four engine-free seasons in the boat, each one sharpening a living-aboard routine he had already made second nature. He visited, in his own words, every spot he had set out to reach "and then some." Afterward he plotted the whole cruise on a chart and found it came, door to door, to only about 415 nautical miles (roughly 770 kilometres), far less than it had felt from inside the boat. His longest stretches without a stop were two separate four-day passages, on which, he said, his biggest problem was growing bored with the food he had brought. Fifty watts of solar panels on the hatch top barely kept pace with a battery worn down by shading under sail and by running more gadgets than he strictly needed; doubling the panel area was the fix he planned for the next cruise. He called the whole season "full circle," not because the route doubled back on itself but because about a week after the boat was shipped off from Kauai, it was "safely back in my garage." The cruise ended where it had started. Asked afterward whether the boat's reputation as the most comfortable he had sailed held true underway and not only at anchor, he said it held either way: no sitting in the rain, no late-night reefing, no banging shins on a ladder, no wet oilskins, no sleeping in a harness. A boat only needs a place long enough to lie down in and a place to sit and steer from, he said, and if both of those are warm, dry and ventilated, "you win."

Three years later he sailed the same boat to Cuba. He cruised the southern Bahamas, George Town, the Jumentos and Ragged Island, before a seventeen-hour passage brought him to Puerto de Vita on Cuba's north coast; the sail itself, he wrote, was "pretty easy," while checking in proved "one big snafu after another." Cuban officialdom demanded patience rather than seamanship: paperwork, fees and the near-total absence of internet made reprovisioning and clearing in slower than any passage he sailed there. He worked his way west along the coast to Cayo Guillermo and inland to Morón, and decided against pushing on toward Havana through the same permit-bound bureaucracy, choosing instead to "save the rest of Cuba for a land based tour." For the way home he picked a light-weather shortcut up the western edge of the Great Bahama Bank, anchoring one night forty miles from the nearest land in fifteen feet of water. Then he turned for the Gulf Stream, the powerful warm current that sweeps north between Florida and the Bahamas strongly enough to carry a becalmed boat wherever it is going whether she wants to or not, and for Caesar Creek, south of Miami, on a crossing he expected to take about 25 hours. It nearly did. The wind died completely just as he entered the strongest part of the Stream. For ten hours he drifted northeast at 2.5 to 3 knots on the current alone, still without an engine, working every puff of air he could find at right angles to the flow to claw his way clear of it. He finally made Caesar Creek hard on the wind, on a night with no moon at all.

In each voyage the boat herself was the easy part, and the sailor's own judgement, weighing when to round a point, when to anchor and when to press on through the dark, was what no plan drawn in 1994 could supply.

Races

Nine years after Paradox won the 300-mile Everglades Challenge outright, Michael Beebe's first Paradox, built at Rockport on the Texas coast, was launched in February 2012 with her cabin top still unfinished and deliberately left removable; Beebe put it plainly on the mailing list the day she went in the water: "it's not pristine to be sure but she sails nice." She sailed well enough that she ran the Texas 200, a multi-day coastal raid down the Texas Gulf coast, that June. A fellow competitor filmed her making a particularly difficult tack under sail alone, in close quarters, at the Padre Island Yacht Club in Corpus Christi, a manoeuvre few other boats in the fleet managed that day. Two years later Sean Mulligan sailed the same race in Scout. He had founded the Havasu Pocket Cruisers Convention years before he owned a Paradox of his own, and Scout herself was a boat Lezlie Henson had built in the Houston area as Tardis and sold unsailed before Mulligan finished and renamed her. Entered as a fundraiser for the Livestrong Foundation, she carried the names of cancer survivors and the lost on her sides. Scout covered the 200 miles in five days, in conditions from light air to 34 knots apparent. Along the way she towed a fellow competitor, Rick Landreville, and his boat, Crippled Duck, through 20 to 25 knots of wind.

The Race to Alaska, an engine-free race of some 750 miles up the Inside Passage to Ketchikan, framed the boat's ambition and its limit more sharply than either Florida race. No Paradox entered the first running, in 2015, but the builders' mailing list argued the question out while that year's fleet was still on the water. John Gilbert, who had reasoned about the design from his own years of engine-free cruising in British Columbia's tidal waters without ever building one himself, judged that Paradox would finish the course but go "way outclassed" by the leaders. Rob Kellock, partway through building his own Paradox in New Zealand, replied that her knack for sailing almost continuously, the same trait that had already won the Everglades Challenge, might carry her to windward in anything up to 25 knots.

Eleven years later a Paradox actually entered, and it was not her sailor's first attempt at the course. Jolyn Barley of Comox, British Columbia had entered the 2024 race solo on an eighteen-foot-six pedal-driven trimaran, a Hobie Tandem Island. She withdrew partway north after the main hull and both floats began taking on water faster than she could manage, and her drysuit and sleeping bag soaked through along with everything else aboard. She announced the withdrawal plainly: "I'm very sad to be leaving Race To Alaska due to some unfortunate boat/equipment malfunctions but you can bet I'll be back to complete it one day! ... I will be just that much more prepared for next time!" Two years later the Paradox was that return. She registered Team Forget Me Knot, a stock Paradox of 13 feet 10 inches, for the 2026 running, among seventy-two registered teams; her own team page carried no further biography and did not even give the boat a name. Her declared propulsion was a yuloh and a Hobie Mirage Drive together, the only Paradox on record entered with both. The design's own builders had tried a Mirage Drive twice before, in 2006 and 2008, and rejected it both times, since its trunk would rise above the waterline through the flat cabin floor and a pedal fin does little against a hull this heavy. The race's own social media put the contrast plainly to a wider audience: "Smallest boat: 13 feet. Team Forget Me Knot ... sailing a Paradox. What's a Paradox? Google it." She never started. The official results record her as DNS, did not start, the only such entry in a results sheet of seventy-one boats, of which sixty-seven finished the qualifying leg to Victoria and three retired. The race gives no reason.

Gatherings

Cedar Key, on Florida's Gulf coast, organised each year by the West Coast Trailer Sailor Squadron, has drawn Paradox owners on the first weekend of May since at least 2003, the community's longest-running meeting point. Nine sharpies, including the Bolducs' own, turned up for the 2007 gathering alone, remembered as a record year for boats attending. That weekend put Layden on the same beach as Sven Yrvind, invited from Sweden as the cookout's guest speaker, and Meade and Jan Gougeon, the authors of the standard text on epoxy boatbuilding. One attendee, meeting all three, called it "a special treat... to meet some people that I have admired for a long time." The 2008 gathering drew more than fifty boats, homebuilt and production mixed, sailing most of the day in a strong ocean breeze. It was there that Layden himself stood on the beach encouraging a stranger sailing a fast twelve-foot boat, averaging better than five knots with bursts near six, to enter her in the Everglades Challenge.

In England, Faith and a second boat matched a fleet of Dinghy Cruising Association dinghies on a reach and did slightly better than them to windward at a week-long meeting at Cobnor, Chichester Harbour, in August 2007. They then rode out a force 8 near-gale at anchor on good holding ground, while an adjoining activity centre's youngsters kept on training through the blow regardless. Six years later all three of Britain's Paradoxes then afloat, Little Jim, Faith and JoHanna, met at Poole Harbour on the same July weekend, the sun out and the wind light. It prompted Alastair Law to ask the list: "Is this a world record?" Sail Oklahoma gave a Paradox's builder and her sailor a stage together for once, in October 2014: Sean Mulligan and Lezlie Henson presented Scout's building and her sailing side by side. At the same event Matt Layden inspected the Enigma 460 built by Chris Curtis, an Idaho sailor who would later own Scout himself, and, in Curtis's words, "quietly gave me the OK."

What strained a Paradox hardest

Across all of it, the voyages that strained a Paradox hardest were not always the longest. Ten becalmed hours in the Gulf Stream tested patience more than strength. A few hours against the reefs off Cape Leeuwin tested nerve more than endurance. Boat after boat, what the fleet asked for was time and judgement rather than heroics.

"She is not fast but can keep sailing in any weather," Layden had written on his entry form for the Everglades Challenge, and every voyage since has borne him out. One fact holds steady through Jolyn Barley's bid for Alaska, Geoff Davis off Cape Leeuwin, Sean Mulligan's tow line and Dave Skaife's ten becalmed hours in the Gulf Stream alike: no Paradox has a confirmed ocean crossing. The design's working benchmark for open water remains the Gulf Stream. The Bolducs crossed it seven winters running, once in company with Layden's own Paradox, and Skaife crossed it twice more, all of it under sail and oar with no engine at all. Susan Davis, a Lake Ontario sailor writing to the builders' mailing list as early as 2002, pointed out that this crossing, made routinely, was already "longer and rougher" than the one from Southern California out to Catalina Island. Whether a stock Paradox could go further than that, nobody has yet had occasion to find out. Between them, these voyages touch three continents and four oceans: the Indian, the Southern, the Atlantic and the Pacific. Not one of them asked the boat to carry anything she did not already have aboard.

The fleet those voyages come from is small. At least 69 Paradox hulls have been begun in seventeen countries on six continents, and at least 29 of them are confirmed to have sailed; both figures are floors, since the plans have always sold far faster than boats were finished.
