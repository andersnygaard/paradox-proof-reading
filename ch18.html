Chapter 18

Changing the boat

Ironsides and Stephen Yahn

Stephen Yahn began his Paradox at Manchester, Washington, on Puget Sound, in July 2006. He named her before she had a hull: Ironsides, after the one change he made to the plans and never painted over. The stock third bulkhead carries plywood webs and bins that narrow the berth. Yahn replaced the webs with a thin strip reinforced in steel rather than plywood, set in epoxy, and built the bulkhead without bins at all. The result was a cabin sole 41 inches wide: wide enough, he hoped, to sleep two across the hull instead of along it. He also mounted a small outboard outside the hull near bulkhead 4, worked from the hatchway without ever climbing onto the transom, because yuloing had grown hard on his hands.

Ironsides launched on Puget Sound in October 2008, two years and three months after Yahn cut his first panel, ballasted with concrete rather than lead. She tacked cleanly all the way round on every pass that first afternoon, and what struck Yahn most was not having to hold the tiller: long stretches went by hands off. Alastair Law, Little Jim's builder in Yeovil, had heard the same disbelief about his own boat before. "It is funny how people don't really believe that about the tiller," he wrote back, "until they have experienced it."

Years later, sailing with his wife aboard, Yahn found the real use for the widened sole. The stock foredeck gave her nowhere to sit and stay in the breeze without making the boat tippy, and the wide cabin sole gave her a stable seat instead. Nobody had built it for that purpose. It earned its keep anyway.

Yahn sold her in October 2012 for $3,000 with her trailer. André-François Bourbeau, a Québec wilderness-travel professor who had read the mailing list for two years without finding the courage to build a boat of his own, bought her sight unseen from more than 5,000 km away rather than let the chance pass. He drove to Puget Sound and back, over 10,000 km round trip, and picked up a boat whose trailer broke clean in half the first time she went into the water. "It's the boat that was holding up the trailer and not vice versa. And at the front and back only! That Paradox is tough!" He renamed her Iota.

She has not stayed in one place since. Bourbeau trailered her to three cruises over the following years: Lake Amistad in Texas, the St Johns River in Florida and Albemarle Sound in North Carolina, each running from a few days to five weeks. Between the first two, Yahn's own non-stock roller-furling arm failed on Quebec's Saguenay Fjord in a rising gale. The failure forced a ten-mile run under half-set sail that touched 7.5 knots, planing against both tide and current at a speed Bourbeau would never have chosen to sail at voluntarily. He rebuilt the fitting afterward to the plans' own half-inch bronze rod specification and had no more trouble with it. Years afterward he confirmed the widened bulkhead from the inside, in plainer words than any builder's blueprint: "iron ribs for the middle bulkhead, which permits a much wider inside sleeping area. Nice."

Paraplastic and Kjell Karlsson

Kjell Karlsson, a Swedish sailor who had known Yrvind personally since the 1970s, had spent a decade on a failed small-boat project in Yrvind's own foam-sandwich method before he had ever heard of Paradox. Yrvind introduced him to Layden's design in 1998, having by then met Layden, sailed his boat and written about it for a Swedish magazine. The following year Karlsson's family vacationed in Florida with a meeting arranged so he could see Layden and the boat for himself, but a summer of 40°C heat and some 1,200 forest fires sent the family north instead of south, and Karlsson missed a meeting he still regrets. He got the plans from Layden directly regardless, and when he came to build he kept Yrvind's method and adopted Layden's design: not plywood but Divinycell foam core, glassfibre inside and polyester outside, over bulkheads held to the plans' own exact measurements, since, as he put it, "almost the whole boat is constructed around them." Both designers blessed the choice, a decision Karlsson later described as one "blessed by Sven and through Sven also by Matt."

The foam-and-glass construction let him do what no plywood builder could do as easily: omit the lower bin shelves and the separate flotation foam the plans specify, since the sandwich hull floats on its own, and widen the berth to the bulkheads' own outer dimension, 1,140 millimetres: wide enough, he thought, to sleep two "at least if they love each other." A bronze sheet, 70 kilograms and 3 to 4 millimetres thick, formed the chine runners' undersides in place of lead ballast, doubling as a lightning-protection ground plate, and the mast, yard and boom were carbon fibre rather than wood.

She came out heavier than Karlsson hoped. Finished and weighed complete except for mast, boom, yard and battery, she came to 326 kilograms, including the bronze bottom plate, roughly 100 kilograms heavier than an equivalent plywood hull. Yrvind had warned him. Visiting partway through the build and finding Karlsson's bulkheads laminated in two layers of glassfibre, he pronounced them too heavy, declared he had "never never never never never EVER seen or heard of a boat that became too light," and wrote on Karlsson's own shop wall in coarse ink: "TÄNK PÅ VIKTEN, Yrvind." Think of the weight.

The rudder gave Karlsson a second lesson in buoyant material. Launched twice before she ever sailed, she revealed on the second launch that her rudder floated like a cork, too buoyant for any line or bungee to hold down; melting lead into the Divinycell core failed, and a drilled cavity could hold only a third of the lead needed, so Karlsson clad the rudder's lower section in leftover bronze sheet instead. Her first actual sail came after that fix, in light conditions on a nearby lake, twenty-one years, by Karlsson's own count, since he had sailed anything at all. A gusty weekend sail not long afterward ended badly: the wind forced her into reeds, the rig fought coming down, and the haul-out afterward buckled a trailer fender.

She sails on today under a different owner and a different name. Johan Bloom keeps her on Sweden's west coast, in Bohuslän, as Tetris, and sails her year round; a 2022 outing took her to Lake Siljan in central Sweden. He describes her hatch as built "to ocean standards," a phrase Karlsson, who built that hatch against Yrvind's own ocean-crossing method rather than Layden's coastal one, would probably not argue with.

Planktos and Roselt Croeser

Croeser built his Paradox in Perth, Western Australia, across four locations between early 2013 and the end of 2016, working the project alongside owner-building a house at the same time. The result was deliberately a working-boat finish, strong and simple, using what he already had on hand wherever possible: her mast came from a sunken boat and her sail was a hand-me-down from another project. The whole build ran to about $2,500.

He changed from the stock lug to junk rig only after sailing a friend's stock-rigged Paradox and finding the same fault twice over: a sail hard to raise and a reef taken too slowly when it mattered. He had already converted an unrelated eighteen-foot keelboat to junk rig once before and knew which way he meant to go before he ever cut a Paradox panel.

She launched into 15-knot winds in November 2016 with no windows yet fitted and only 90 kg of lead and 30 kg of chain aboard against an estimated further 100 kg still needed, and she sailed well and pointed well from her first afternoon. A shakedown cruise took her 800 km north to Shark Bay, tacking through 110 degrees, matching her own prior boats sailed under Bermudan rig in the same waters.

She was towed in from 12 nautical miles offshore after eight hours of Force 6 conditions, her rudder straining audibly under the bigger waves; heaving to felt safe throughout. "Sailing a Paradox is a very good way to be kept honest and humble," Croeser wrote afterward. He sold her in 2023 to Kevin Williamson, who fitted a folding mast and then extended it for more sail area, and renamed her Skye; the Junk Rig Association had already named Planktos its boat of the month that December.

Cachivache and Alberto Callejon

Alberto Callejon began Cachivache in 2008 at Rawson, on Argentina's Patagonian coast, where plywood itself had to be trucked some two thousand kilometres to reach him: an early argument for building in steel that he set aside as too costly. A journalist who also worked as an online translator, he wrote to the list mostly in Spanish, carried across in machine translation good enough to show his sense and clumsy enough to show his distance from everyone he was writing to.

The clearest measure of building that far from a supply chain is the tape measure. A cheap import, marked "Made in China," read "99" on its centimetre scale where it should have read 100, a fault that went uncaught until every bulkhead already cut with it had to be checked, found wrong and done again. Callejon recovered the only way the story allows: after, in his own words, "many hours of meditation," he destroyed the offending tape with a hammer and carried on, reporting a few days later that "now if all the measures are consistent with the plans." She finally went into the water on 23 January 2011, by his own description "el Paradox mas austral," the most southerly Paradox anywhere.

He founded the Paradox builders' Facebook group that August. Her cabin was widened the same year, a decision made with full knowledge of what it cost, and her transom rudder slot was closed against the sea rather than left open as the plans allow.

Vandals broke her windows and stole a fender that December; Callejon thanked the friends who wrote in sympathy and judged the thieves lucky he had not caught them at it: "today would form part of the seabed." Days later, sailing with the wind on her quarter, an unexpected shift jibed her without warning while his wife rested below on the weather side; she luffed up on her own and righted herself, in Callejon's words, "like a buoy."

She passed Argentina's maritime inspections the following January and was registered as the country's first authorized Paradox, and the most southerly one anywhere. Her chine runners were reshaped in 2013, shorter, wider and tilted downward, and the same year her mast, originally wood, gave way to a 63.5 mm aluminium tube. She was still sailing the Río Chubut and the open Atlantic a decade after her launch.

Informed change, uninformed change

Law had very nearly made an uninformed change himself before he ever cut a panel of Little Jim. He had modelled the whole boat in a CAD program first, precisely so that nothing he altered later would surprise him, and when a visiting correspondent pushed back in January 2008 against what he read as a closed-minded insistence on building strictly to plan, Law did not retreat from the principle; he sharpened it. "I have no argument... with informed modifications to a design," he wrote. "My grump is with those who decide to make changes without any real understanding of what is going on." He had nearly been one of those builders himself, he admitted, which was probably why he felt so strongly about it, and he pointed out, as the saving grace, that few of the people proposing changes before they had even seen the stock plans ever actually finished a boat.

Don Elliott had said as much seven years earlier, in a reply buried inside a technical question about nailing down a plywood bottom panel, and his version of the rule outlasted everything else argued in that thread. "It is important when building any boat to follow the plans instructions to the letter," he wrote. "Anyone that plans to build a Paradox should read the notes and instructions on those plans and follow them completely. If you want to change something or not follow a instruction or do something a different way, then it will serve you best if you clear it with Matt first before proceeding. I never asked him a thing as I figured if he said it in the first place...he meant it."

Layden tested the same rule from the other direction in 2003. He had cut a new sail for his own boat that February, shortening the foot and raising the peak for a sharper, higher pointing shape, and when he reported the change to the list that May he weighed it as plainly as Elliott's rule asked of anyone else: the new sail pointed better and reefed more awkwardly than the old one, and the stock sail, well cut, still did its job and needed no replacing, though he would cut the new shape again if he built another boat. Free to redesign his own rig from scratch, the man who had drawn it chose instead to test a change, say exactly what it cost and stand by the sail he already had.

The same January 2008 exchange asked a harder question: does a changed boat keep the name of the one it started as? The builders decided it does, provided the idea that made the boat worth building in the first place survives the change, pointing to the West Wight Potter, redesigned from plywood to fibreglass and rerigged more than once under a different builder's hand within a few years of her original drawing, and still called a Potter by everyone who owns one. Past a certain point the opposite holds: a hull substantially widened or lengthened stops being a Paradox and becomes a Paradox derivative, whatever name her builder gives her. Real change, understood before it is made, takes four forms among the Paradoxes that have actually been built: to the cabin, to the construction, to the rig and to the scale.

The cabin

Michael Beebe built two Paradoxes at Rockport, on the Texas Gulf coast, and the second exists because the first taught him what not to do. His first boat carried side decks pushed out to the sheer line and a lengthened tiller, so he could sit sideways and steer as on a conventional sailboat. Bourbeau, the Québec sailor who would later buy Iota, sailed alongside that first Paradox for six days in cold rain and judged the change had cost more than it gave: the wide decks ate the storage a stock Paradox uses for its yuloh and mast, and climbing on and off the cabin top got harder, all for a seating position nobody had actually asked the boat to provide.

Beebe's second boat, later named Oogway, went back to the stock cabin. He kept only the one change that had worked on the first boat, a removable coachroof, and added hatches at bulkheads 2 and 4, the second doubling as a cockpit seat, and an all-locker subfloor in place of water ballast tanks. He sold her in January 2017 to Bobby, an Oklahoma City builder who posts as OkieBob, who drove a 1,200-mile round trip to fetch her and reported she "towed like a dream."

Bobby sailed Oogway for the first time that May, nervous after reading other builders' complaints about tacking and furling. He needn't have worried about clearing the dock: she rounded up cleanly on a starboard tack and carried him out past a regatta already under way. Her first real tack failed outright, forcing a fall-off and a jibe instead, and by his own count at least three or four went the same way before most of the day's tacks began going fine. "About this time I'm thinking damn Matt really designed a great little boat and Mike nailed the build," he wrote that evening, crediting Beebe's own care in setting the halyard's attachment point for the balance he felt under way. The return leg was harder, a full 180-degree turn needed to clear a leeward marina in gusty, shifting wind, and retrieval turned into a small drama of its own, the boat sliding home between a pair of freshly welded trailer guide posts with not a speck of paint lost. A crowd gathered on the bank for a fishing tournament gave the odd-looking boat and her odder-looking skipper a standing ovation as she came out of the water.

Bengt Genell built his Paradox at home in central Sweden, two hours from the nearest coast, on a tight budget: exterior plywood rather than marine grade, screws where the plans called for ring nails, no lightning protection and no built-in water tanks. He named her Ladikan, old Swedish for "the little box," and launched her on Lake Mälaren in July 2006, making up her ballast with sandbags to bring her down to her marks. Then the weather failed him. A windless summer left no sailing report of her anywhere on record, though a builders' registry republished years later lists her as tested.

What changes little

Most changes are smaller than an identity question, and most of it answers one of a handful of recurring problems: getting in and out of a low, sealed cabin, seeing out while sailing from inside a closed box, and fitting a body or a climate the stock drawings did not anticipate. Greg Lockhart, building his fourth Paradox in 2011 and naming her Endeavour, chose port-hole style windows and a one-piece lift-out hatch over the plans' continuous window and sliding hatch, purely for the look of it; the oval port set into the hatch itself doubles as a way to watch the boom from inside with the hatch shut. None of it touches the cabin's identity the way a wider hull would; it touches only what was already the builder's own to decide.

The fullest single record of how many of these small changes one well-sailed Paradox can carry belongs to Little Jim. A conversation on the beach with a stranger who took her for a stock, unmodified Paradox prompted Law, in the autumn of 2008, to write out every way his own boat actually differed from the plans, sorted into what he had changed during the build, what he had changed from experience afloat and what he would still like to try. No lightning rod, on the reasoning that the weather the system guards against is not much of a problem in England. The sail's fastening grommets moved to the side of the sleeve rather than the plans' own corners. A window let into the hatch lets a sailor watch the sail with the hatch shut, at the cost of being the one place condensation collects and drips overnight. From sailing her he had learned to split the floor hatch into four covers rather than two, so the forward sections open without moving the seat; to fit a shelf under the aft bins to keep their contents clear of the bilges; to drill a small drain hole at the back corner of each side window; to move the yuloh's pivot about four inches nearer the handle; and to hang a shackle on the rudder's up-haul line, between the last fairlead and the cleat, so it cannot drop out of the cleat's groove when the rudder kicks up. Ideas he had considered but never built: toe rails fitted outside the hull rather than on the deck, to double as a rubbing strip; a permanently plumbed electric bilge pump; stowage hoops built without any vertical section, so lines slip over them instead of catching; and lift-out floorboards in place of the hatch hinges that foul with grit, as Bill Serjeant had done on Faith. Taken together it is a long list and a small one: nothing on it touches the hull, the rig or the ballast, and Little Jim, after everything on that list, is still unmistakably a Paradox.

The rig

Rob Kellock, Mini's builder near Christchurch, New Zealand, made the change himself, trading her standing lug for a four-sided sprit rig in 2018 only after sailing both rigs side by side for months to be sure of the trade he was making. Tom Steinmetz went further, cutting his chine runners away entirely and fitting a leeboard and a Bermudan sail in their place; no message of his own survives to explain the change, and everything known about the boat comes from other builders studying a photograph, and later a video, of a hull none of them had sailed. One of them later recalled being told the runners were cut to reduce lee helm, and warned, from what he could piece together of her history, that the change runs only one way: "there is no way back."

The change that is never small

Enlarging the boat, or lightening her, is a different kind of change from a hatch cover or a drain hole, because nothing about Paradox moves alone. Make her bigger and the ballast has to grow to keep her on her lines, which changes how deep she sits, which changes how far the chine runners bite at a given heel, which changes how much of the side load is left for the rudder to carry: one part of the whole system pulling at every other part. Make her lighter instead and the same chain runs in reverse: less ballast fits in the space the plans allow for it, so she rides higher, sinks less at a given heel and asks her chine runners to do their work half-submerged. A scale-up, or a scale-down, is never only that.

Layden's own sanction for a twenty percent enlargement is documented four separate times. Glen Maxwell worked out the figures for it in 2003 and filed them with the group: a length overall of 16 feet 6 inches against the stock 13 feet 10, a beam just under 5 feet, a sail area of 168 square feet against the stock 100, a displacement of 2,321 lb against 1,410. The gain inside is modest set against all that extra weight, fifteen more inches of sleeping length and ten more of width, not enough on its own, Maxwell judged, to need a new interior layout. When a later builder dug the file up again in 2013, Maxwell confirmed from memory that he had written it and repeated that Layden had given the idea his blessing. Pressed further the same week, he went beyond any specific figure and recalled Layden telling him in person that Paradox was "infinitely scale-able," with the condition that a builder have the knowledge to handle the problems an increase in scale brings. Dave Bolduc gave the fourth account in 2009, in nearly the same words: Layden had told him a twenty percent scale-up would be fine without increasing the dimensions of the lumber involved, though Bolduc knew of nobody who had actually built one.

Set against all four accounts is one that caps the sanction lower. "Matt OK'd a 10% scale-up when he was asked about it," one builder reported in 2008. "He felt anything past that would be designing a new boat." Layden's own enlarged design went no further than the sketch he called Big Cruiser, drawn as early as 2003 and never turned into a full set of plans. No twenty percent Paradox and no Big Cruiser came from the designer's own hand.

The ceiling builders kept reaching on their own, independent of any blessing from Layden, sits close to ten percent too, for a reason that has nothing to do with sailing and everything to do with plywood. Past that mark the side panels need an added scarf joint to reach their new length, and the bottom panel, already a snug fit on a standard sheet at stock size, grows wider than four feet once the chines are added, forcing a seam into a panel that used to come off one sheet whole. Pete Hodges, a builder who planned a ten percent hull of his own and named her Paradox 1.1, worked through exactly that problem, deciding on the project in December 2001 and planning it through the following spring.

Jim Michalak, a sharpie designer among the teachers Layden has named as his own, worked out the reason in a published essay: stretch a hull by a fifth in every dimension and her panel area grows with the square of that fifth, her displacement with its cube, so the weight penalty runs well ahead of the length one. The worked numbers above bear that out: to put a twenty percent larger Paradox on her new, deeper lines, her sail area has to grow by 68 percent and her displacement by 60, far more than the one fifth she actually gained in any single dimension. The ten percent figure the list settled on for itself, independent of whatever Layden might allow a particular builder, is the same arithmetic kept small enough not to hurt.

An outside voice made the same point more bluntly in 2009, on a different forum entirely, with no connection to the Paradox list at all: dreaming in print of a boat roomy enough to take his sons sailing, the writer worked through the idea of a bigger Paradox and talked himself out of it. A plus-size hull big enough for two or three, he reckoned, would be enormously heavy, since her stability and pointing depend on a flat bottom and vertical sides immersed just so. Eight years later, the test of that prediction finally got built.

Eerik Mee, in Estonia, built the twenty percent Paradox the mailing list had argued over roughly forty times in fifteen years and never once built. He scaled every dimension on the plans by exactly 1.2, and the finished hull came in at a length overall of about 5.05 metres and a width of about 1.5 metres, within centimetres of the paper figures Maxwell had worked out a decade before. The stock scantlings stayed the same; only the spars grew with the hull, the mast reaching 6 metres. Launching her alone meant tying her to his car and reversing the last stretch into the water, a job the boats built close to the plans never need.

Her rig change forced two more changes she had not set out to make. The cambered junk sail Mee cut for her, built with the centre of effort held as far aft as he could manage, still moved forward of where the stock rig would have put it, so the mast moved forward too, and the chine runners with it: proof, worked out in a finished hull rather than on paper, that nothing about Paradox moves alone.

Her first days on the water, in July 2017, tested the one thing the theory most feared. Paradox has a reputation for a slow, reluctant tack, and most sailors learn a handful of tricks to coax one out of her, turning slowly into the wind and carrying extra weight forward and to windward. Mee tried those tricks first. "No success." The second time, he tried without any tricks at all, "and it was a success. As were all next tackings." A twenty percent larger Paradox, carrying a sail nobody on the list had ever sailed, tacked cleanly once her builder stopped trying to coax her the way a stock boat needs coaxing.

Not everything settled so quickly. One tack showed a real lee helm, cured only by adding water ballast forward and tightening the tack parrel to drag the centre of effort back where he wanted it. "I tested with 240kg of ballast," he told a correspondent the following year, "about 100kg of lead and the rest was water." She carried more besides than any other changed Paradox on record: an electric outboard, two solar panels, a depth sounder, a VHF radio and LED cabin lighting. She was day-sailed on an inland lake, never cruised, and Mee moored her rather than trailer her, very nearly the outcome the sceptic on that other forum had feared, arrived at from the opposite direction, by a man who built the boat instead of only dreaming of her.

No one sanctioned the next boat at all. A builder who signed his messages David Nemo introduced himself to the mailing list in January 2009 by announcing he had already spent roughly £8,000 translating every measurement on the stock plans to exactly double scale, 28 feet by 8 feet against the stock 13 feet 10 inches by about 4 feet, with her hull, sides and decks already built and glassed. Only after describing the whole project did the scale of what he had done seem to strike him. "Is it actually feasible to simply double the size of a Paradox and expect her to even float OK let alone sail," he asked, admitting he knew very little about boat design and had already spent too much to easily stop.

Her construction departed from the plans as far as her scale did: Divinycell foam core for the sides and decks, framing of reclaimed hardwood salvaged from a church and chine runners hand-carved from two solid pieces of oak, hollowed out and filled with cast lead, so that ballast and lateral plane shared the same timber. Nemo had been living out of a van for months before the build began; he sold it to raise cash for the build and moved aboard the half-finished hull itself, sheltering under insulated canvas through a difficult winter while construction continued. She was complete by December 2009 and had crossed the English Channel under electric power, bound for the Mediterranean by France's Canal du Midi. A fellow sailor who crossed the Channel aboard her that weekend wrote to thank him, calling the finished boat "a real miracle" of a build, recognisable at a glance as a scaled-up Paradox with a rear cockpit added. Nobody ever confirmed her actually sailing rather than motoring, and nobody heard from Nemo again.

One more scaled-down Paradox never got the chance to answer anything at all. Bobby, the same OkieBob who later bought Beebe's Oogway, had set out in 2010 to build a hull reduced to eighty five percent scale, 357 centimetres long against the stock 420, and named her for the number; she was never finished.

A hull without plans

Will Robertson, a Texan who had never built or sailed a boat, found a different way around the whole question of informed and uninformed change: he skipped the plans altogether. Inspired by a video of David Bolduc sailing Enigma single-handed around the Bahamas, Robertson set out to build his own small cruiser from nothing but what he could find online, working out the shape, the chine runners and the rig by eye rather than from a drawing he had paid for. He sewed his own sail, built a hollow birdsmouth mast and worked out a roller-furling boom that could reef at any point along its length. He mocked up a cabin top partway through the build, liked the look of it, then never got around to finishing it; the boat sails to this day without one: a decked hull with an open cockpit, a Paradox without a cabin.

He sailed her in the Texas 200 in 2020 and again in 2021, reaching Corpus Christi both times after a first attempt that cost him a tiller repair, a night anchored alone in a bay with the bugs bad and no way to call for help, and a second camp spent listening to barges pass in the dark along the Land Cut. He added a leeboard afterward, apologising for it a little: "it wasn't in the original design." A photograph he took of her at Bird Island Basin, taken almost by accident, became a cover of Small Craft Advisor.

Building in something else

Layden had closed the materials argument for his own satisfaction in September 2003, in the same message that defended his choice of plywood. Builders had asked, and would go on asking for two decades, whether a Paradox could be built from something other than wood, and he addressed the question directly. "As for cored composite, aluminum, steel, thermoplastic sheet, whatever your personal preference of material; sure, do the research, work out your scantlings or pay an expert to do so, and go for it. The boat was designed to be built quickly and economically from sheet materials, and any of the above or others will work decently if intelligently engineered. But I doubt any of them will produce a better mix of economy, ease of construction, ease/cost of maintenance & repair, sailing performance, interior comfort and life-cycle environmental impact than good old 20th century plywood."

For the next twenty two years nobody built a Paradox hull in anything but wood, except Karlsson's hull in foam. Metal stayed purely theoretical, argued over on the list every few years and never once tried, until 2025, when two builders made it real within a few months of each other. Alex McGavin, welding in his own shop, cut and joined a hull in 3 mm plate of 5005 grade aluminium early that year, using a MIG spool gun. A friend he called Scotty built a second hull alongside him that spring, this one in 4 mm plate, which both men found easier to work than the thinner sheet had been. Neither boat had sailed by the end of the year: McGavin's own, the lighter of the two, was, in his words, "just about to put mast and sail on"; Scotty's heavier hull was "half done." The test itself, like the verdict, is still being written.
