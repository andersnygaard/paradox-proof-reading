How the design survived without a company

Paradox never had a company behind her. No dealer stocked her, no factory cut her parts, and nobody was paid to keep her plans in print or her builders talking to each other. Everything that lets a stranger find the plans today, ask a question and get an answer, or learn how many Paradoxes exist survives because particular people decided, unpaid, to do the unglamorous work of keeping a design alive. What kept her going, in the end, was not an institution but a plan that kept reaching new hands.

The plans

Dave Bolduc, the Greensboro carpenter, has sold the Paradox plans by hand continuously since at least July 2001. He is the only person Layden has ever authorized to do so. The price has moved only twice in that quarter century: thirty-five dollars from 2001, drifting upward through the following decade. It settled back at thirty-five dollars by January 2025, when a Minneapolis builder named Josh Richard reported, "Received plans from David after sending $35 via paypal last week. They look great."

A free overview exists for anyone who wants to see the shape of the boat before committing to the real drawings: an outline set called the Study Plans, offered explicitly "for inspiration only." Alongside it sits a four-page beginner's guide. Derek Clark, one of the plans' earliest first-time builders, wrote it and gave it away copyright-free once his own boat was finished.

No kit has ever reached a builder, the one attempt having needed more research than anyone had time to finish, and no CAD files or cutting files have ever been distributed or sold either. A builder today still works from the same drawings Don Elliott, the Tomah builder, worked from in the 1990s. Other small-boat designs have grown into finished kits over the years. Paradox has not, and a builder who wants one still starts from a set of plans rather than a box of parts.

The drawings themselves have occasionally found their own way to a new home. A Swedish man named Styrbjörn Lundström posted to the Facebook group in March 2023 that his father had bought a set of Paradox blueprints from a UK source years before, meaning to build her in retirement, and had died before he started. Going through his father's workshop, Lundström found the drawings still in their tube and offered them for the cost of postage to anyone who would finish what his father had begun; within days they were on their way to a builder in Buenos Aires.

A used Paradox, by contrast, almost never comes up for sale at all. Owners who have one tend to keep it. The handful of sales on record over two decades have mostly settled somewhere between two and seven thousand dollars, depending on condition and country. Don Elliott's old boat, The Edge, went to a new owner for $2,800 in 2017. She was found within about two weeks of being listed. A boat in California called Raffles was remembered selling for around $7,000 in 2016. That scarcity is the plainest reason the design has spread the way it has. On this record, a newcomer who wants a Paradox has essentially one path open: it runs through the plans rather than around them.

It still runs through the same door it always has. More than two decades after he first answered a stranger's letter about a boat he had never built, Dave Bolduc was still opening the same kind of email in 2025. He was still asking for the same thirty-five dollars and still sending back the same fourteen sheets that had once reached Don Elliott, Glen Maxwell and Bill Serjeant in their turn.

The guide

Don Elliott died in 2008. His widow Wanda kept the booklet moving: for years she answered orders by email, took payment by PayPal or cheque and mailed the fifty pages out herself. The price crept from eight dollars toward eighteen by 2013, though she had no boatbuilding knowledge of her own to add to it. Their son Josh built her a website, smallboatmanuals.weebly.com, that took payment and sent the files automatically. Within a few years, a buyer needed no person at all on the other end of an order. Three generations of one household, father, widow and son, kept a thirty-year-old construction guide in print without ever meaning to run a business.

The UK website and the registries

Bill Serjeant, the Essex builder of Faith, gave the design's British builders a web presence of their own. The plans reached him in October 2003, and within days he had added a Paradox section to his existing site, Small Sailboats. He later split it off into its own address, paradox-uk.co.uk. The site closed at the end of March 2009, after three months' notice.

While it ran, Serjeant kept the closest thing the design had to a worldwide build registry, a running table of builders, their boats and their countries. He published it first in 2006, when it already held 29 boats. When he put the same table on his blog in October 2014, the figure had not grown. Only two of the original builders had since died, and a few boats had changed hands. His own comment on the gap between the two numbers that matter most, plans sold and boats finished, is as blunt as anything in the record: "Hundreds of plans have been sold, but nowhere near as many boats would appear to have been built, probably because the costs are high, both in terms of expenditure and of effort."

Bobby, an Oklahoma City builder who posts as OkieBob, found the published builder counts stale and built his own registry in July 2015, a crowd-corrected list he called "a starting place." He kept it current himself for three years before handing it to the group's own moderators.

The list and its rescue

The builders' mailing list, paradoxbuilders, had opened on Yahoo Groups under Glen Maxwell, Zoë's builder, on 7 July 2001. A smaller sibling, paradoxbuilders2, followed in May 2004 as an overflow for photographs the first group's storage could not hold. A volunteer moderator set it up while Maxwell himself crossed the Atlantic.

Then, on 16 October 2019, Yahoo announced it would delete every message on every Yahoo Group, Paradox included. By then the mailing list held something like 9,500 messages going back eighteen years, and nobody on it had a complete copy. Members argued for days over whether to give up on email and move the conversation onto Alberto Callejon's Facebook group, or to build a self-hosted forum that would keep every subject sorted the way Facebook never could.

The member who actually solved the problem had never built a Paradox at all. He signed his messages simply as Matt, a long-standing correspondent distinct from the designer whose boat the list existed to discuss. Within hours of the shutdown notice, he had found that Yahoo's own API could be reached directly with a script. By the same evening he had already pulled down about eighty percent of the archive.

Getting the rest of the way took longer, because the files and the group's membership still belonged to Yahoo. Only Maxwell, as the group's sole owner, could grant the access the new home on groups.io needed to finish the transfer. Matt emailed him privately first, then publicly: "Glen, are you about? Please check your email." A week later, with still no reply, he tried again: "Glen! Please check in. We need you!" Maxwell had just come back from a trip to the Azores and had been putting out other fires ever since; he apologized to the group and granted the access on 6 November, eleven days after the first quiet request. Eight days after that, on the new groups.io list he had built and was paying for out of his own pocket, Matt announced the result in four words: "Yay! The transfer is done!"

Keeping that new home running has cost money every year since. Matt funded most of the first two years himself before asking the membership to help carry it. By 2024, unable to keep paying alone, he put the group's future to its own members, who chose to keep it on the platform's free tier rather than let it go. The archive he rescued in those eleven days had grown, by 2025, to 10,065 messages reaching back to 2001. It is still the fullest record anywhere of the design's first two decades.

The Facebook groups

Alberto Callejon's Facebook group of 2011 was no longer the only one. A second group, the Paradox Sailboat group, followed in February 2019. Its founder was Tim McKinney, a Texas sailor who had just bought a Paradox built by Michael Beebe. It has grown into the busiest record of what the community has done since, with well over eight hundred posts from more than 180 different authors.

A few more keepers

Chuck Leinweber founded and still publishes Duckworks Magazine, an online small-boat magazine and plans outlet with readers well beyond the Paradox list. He had built his own Paradox, Tennessee, by the summer of 2001. He carried techniques worked out on the list, among them a bottom-painting method, to that wider readership.

Dave Raftery built a side-by-side technical comparison between Paradox and Layden's smaller Enigma family, posted online for any builder to consult, and founded the Enigma group's own replacement when it closed in 2011.

Keeping the design alive did not always mean building one. Matt rescued the whole archive without ever owning a Paradox himself. Raftery kept its technical record the same way, building an Enigma instead. The design was argued over, tested on paper and carried forward by people who never picked up a saw for her, just as surely as by the ones who did.
