Epilogue

Building one today

In a garage in Porsgrunn, on Norway's southern coast, a Paradox enlarged by ten percent on every dimension stands on trestles, unfinished. I am Anders Nygaard. I have been building her, on and off, for about two and a half years. Her name is Miramis. Thirty-three years after Matt Layden sailed his own Paradox down a Connecticut ravine into the Salmon River, the same ten sheets of plywood, each measurement multiplied by 1.1, are still going together by hand in my garage. I build with a diesel heater for company. A Norwegian winter that falls to minus twenty Celsius and a Norwegian summer that climbs past thirty each cause their own trouble, for epoxy and for the builder.

I first heard of Matt Layden's boats from a fellow Scandinavian, the designer and voyager Sven Yrvind. Before I owned a workshop I built a model in cardboard and glass fibre in a small flat in Oslo, to understand the idea of the whole chine-runner system: the shape of the hull and the horizontal winglets that run along each chine. I bought Layden's plans about ten years later, and I held off naming the boat, following a convention from the mailing list, until she had "gone 3D": until she existed in three dimensions rather than as a stack of flat panels. When that day came I named her Miramis, after Mio's white horse in Astrid Lindgren's children's novel Mio, min Mio. I like the small joke in naming a boat after a horse. The sailing world has never run short of boats called Rosinante, after Don Quixote's own nag.

Ten percent larger, and why

I am 196 cm tall (6 ft 5 in), and a stock Paradox will not let a man my height lie flat in the salon or pass comfortably through the doorway in bulkhead two. I built a cardboard mock-up before I started and confirmed it myself: at the stock size I would not fit; at ten percent larger, I do, with room to spare. I considered a more conservative fix: building a stock-size hull and simply moving the salon bulkheads fore and aft to buy the length. But years of the builders' own argument persuaded me that changing the proportions would cost the boat other qualities it was drawn to have. So I left the ratios alone and scaled the whole boat instead.

Ten percent sounds modest. It is not, and I said as much myself once to a fellow builder turning the same number over: "10% larger is actually a big thing, because it means 1/3 more displacement, meaning more lead ballast, meaning more trailer weight." Multiply every dimension by 1.1 and you multiply the volume, and so the weight, by 1.1 cubed. That comes to about 1.33: a third more boat, not a tenth. Twenty percent is the next step up, the one Eerik Mee built in Estonia. It comes to 1.2 cubed, about 1.72: closer to a different class of boat than a larger version of the same one. I worked through that arithmetic myself before I cut a single panel, and it settled the question for me: ten percent buys the twenty centimetres of cabin length and the doorway I need, with more storage besides. Anything more starts costing weight I cannot carry on the road. Mee's boat lives at a dock. Mine lives on a trailer by choice. A trailer gives me freedom: beautiful inland canals and one of the world's finest archipelagos both lie within an hour's drive of my home, and I can visit either on a whim.

The one hard limit is the trailer's permitted weight. More boat would mean a bigger car too. I cleared a trailer with Statens vegvesen, the Norwegian road authority, rated for 1,250 kg (2,760 lb) and eighteen feet (5.5 m), and everything about the boat's size has had to live inside that number since before I started. Miramis is meant for the Skagerrak. If anyone asks, though, I say I am sailing her to Hardangervidda, launching at the shallow Møsvatn boat ramp to cross to Mogen, Norway's largest tourist hut. Since Møsvatn is famously shallow, you need a boat exactly like Miramis to do it. There are also standing invitations from the Dinghy Cruising Association, to sail the Limfjord in Denmark and the waters around Koster in Sweden, whenever she is actually afloat to accept them.

Birch, glass and printed parts

The hull itself is planked in exterior-grade Baltic birch, not the okoumé the plans assume: okoumé plywood had become impossible to buy in Norway just as I started, ahead of a general price surge that would have made the build far more expensive if I had waited. Baltic birch is heavier and less forgiving to bend than okoumé, so I did not scale its thickness up with the rest of the boat. A straight ten percent increase on the stock 18 mm bottom and 12 mm sides lands on sheet thicknesses nobody actually sells. Instead I kept to the lighter scheme a Lithuanian builder, Simas Butavičius, had already worked out for his own boat: 12 mm on the bottom and 9 mm on the sides. I had planned 6 mm plywood for the deck too, but I built it in fibreglass instead: a sandwich of pre-made 3 mm glass skins, laid over and under the deck beams. I told him on the Facebook group that I had taken great inspiration from his videos and his posts there, and that is the plain truth of it: a fix worked out in Lithuania solved a problem I had in Norway, between two builders who had never built a boat before and have still, as far as I know, never met.

Thinner plywood needs help to carry a third-heavier boat, so I made it up with glass, and with Sven Yrvind's advice. He urges every builder to glass the inside of the hull too, not only the outside, and demonstrates why on his own channel: by the same principle that holds up a steel I-beam, a blow to the side has to stretch the inner skin before it can dent the hull, just as the beam's lower flange must stretch before the beam itself sags. The core in between, whether plywood or foam, is only filler, like the beam's web. So the whole inside of my hull is glassed in 600 g cloth right up to the deck. The bottom and about twenty centimetres above the waterline carry, in addition, a layer of 1708 biaxial cloth and an extra layer of 600 g; the bottom is the right place to carry that extra weight. Every junction inside the hull, bulkheads to sides, bulkheads to bottom and the chine log itself, is filleted and glassed. I used construction screws only as clamps while the glue cured, then backed every one of them out and filled the hole, so that there is not a single nail or screw left anywhere in the finished boat. If I built her again, I would build her stitch-and-glue instead. The method I actually used worked, but it took longer than it needed to.

The deck beams are glassed in rather than simply screwed down. I pre-produced the glass skins myself, three millimetres thick, laying them up flat on a bench and cutting them to templates rather than building the layers up directly on the curved deck. That let me work sitting down, instead of lying on my back under a half-finished hull.

Where the plans call for a shaped piece I could not easily buy or carve by hand, I have leaned on a 3D printer. I designed and printed jigs for cutting scarf joints, clamped to the edge of a board with a collar that stops the router bit from touching the jig itself, and shared the nine-millimetre version on Thingiverse for anyone who wants it. Wherever a steering rope or a wire has to pass through a bulkhead, most importantly at bulkheads two and four, I print a bushing to protect the sealed plywood end grain from chafe. I cast the lower rudder gudgeon in MDF first, then printed one instead once I judged it would be more accurate than anything I could carve by hand. I enjoy drawing for myself, so I draw every part I design in Onshape and share what might be useful to others on Thingiverse. The masthead is one such part: I designed and printed it myself, a template first for cutting the sixty-degree angle of the sheave slot, then a masthead cap that routes the power cable up past the sheave and the lines to the masthead light.

The rudder is the one place I knowingly departed from Layden's own design rather than simply adapting it to scale. The blade is 3D-printed and clad in two layers of 1708 cloth, one layer of 600 g carbon fibre and three coats of epoxy, worked wet on wet. The stock design hangs the blade from pintles on one face of the transom; mine, in an arrangement borrowed from John Welsford's boats for extra strength, sits instead in a cassette that braces it from both sides at once, so a sideways knock has two bearings to resist it rather than one. It answers, as well as an untested boat can answer anything, a question raised and left open in 2011. A builder planning his own ten-percent enlargement asked whether an enlarged rudder's pivot would need reinforcing the way Sven Yrvind had reinforced his own, and nobody on the mailing list ever settled it for a boat actually built at that scale. Miramis's cassette is my own, provisional answer. I will not know how good an answer it is until she has sailed.

A sail sewn at home

I sewed the sail myself rather than buy one, to the Mk3 pattern already sailing on other Paradoxes. I cut it from 4 oz Dacron, the cloth run horizontally to the foot, every seam triple-stitched in a zig-zag and the corner rings backed with Dyneema webbing rather than anything that could corrode. Rather than trust flat panels to a pair of curved spars, I worked from the drawings of the Mk2 sail to shape the camber, reduced seam by reduced seam, with the help of an AI chatbot standing in for a sailmaker's eye. It is a small, modern echo of something Layden did himself, long before any of this. He once cut a dead-flat first sail that would not set properly against the curved yard and boom. He built a second, shaped one that did, and said so plainly rather than letting the first attempt stand unremarked. Measuring your own mistake and writing down what you did about it is a familiar habit by now. I am simply the newest person to have it.

Not the first Paradox in Norway

Miramis will not be the first Paradox to sail in Norwegian waters, and I want to say that outright rather than let the shape of my own story imply otherwise. Derek Clark built a Paradox in England and sold her within weeks of her 2006 launch; she left for Trondheim days later as Minnow, under her new owner, Robert Biegler, who sailed her out of Trondheimsfjord for years. She returned to England in 2013, into the hands of Bill Serjeant, Faith's own builder. For the better part of a decade, then, a Paradox built in England had already sailed home waters in Norway that mine has not yet reached.

The design's clear clusters lie in the United States, Britain and Australia: its two home countries and the third where the fleet has grown deepest. Builders are at work too in Sweden, Estonia and Lithuania, countries some real distance apart from each other. Most of them found their way to the design through the same mailing list and the same Facebook group that found me. Sven Yrvind, who has watched this design longer than anyone else alive, said as much himself in 2009, years before any of this: "Paradoxes are slowly spreading from Patagonia to northern Norway." Mine is simply the latest place that spreading has reached.

How someone starts today

A builder starting today starts exactly where I did: the plans still come from Dave Bolduc for the same $35 the mailing list quoted in 2001; Don Elliott's guide is still sold through the order page his family keeps up; and the same free study plans are there to look at before committing to the real drawings. The Facebook group that carried my own build, McGavin's and the Lithuanians', is still there, still answering the same questions a new builder has always asked.

My own advice to anyone thinking about starting, for what it is worth, is not to start with a Paradox at all. Build an Oz Goose first, a simple, cheap, stitch-and-glue plywood dinghy that a first-timer can finish in a fraction of the time. A Paradox asks a beginner to learn joinery, lamination, rigging and ballast all at once, on a boat that does not forgive a bad decision cheaply; a small dinghy teaches the same lessons on a scale you can afford to get wrong, before you commit years to a boat that will not let you start over. I am a programmer by trade who has made a good deal of furniture over the years, with a garage and the tools for it. "A maker at heart" is nearer the truth than boatbuilder, and Miramis is my first boat. I do not think that disqualifies anyone from trying. I think it is simply honest to say it takes longer than you expect, whichever boat you start with.

The thumbs up

I had already compiled a short written history of the Paradox once before, in December 2023, for the design's thirtieth year. A great deal has been done since. Miramis has not been launched. I expect the whole project to take about two thousand hours in the end. I have cast roughly 170 kg (375 lb) of lead for her ballast, melted down from more than 175 kg of scrap lead I collected piece by piece over the years and poured into bread tins to make pigs of about seventeen kilograms each. That is curiously close to what a stock Paradox carries rather than the third more my own scaled-up arithmetic would suggest, and I have not yet decided whether that is a deliberate choice or simply where the casting happens to stand. The sail is sewn. The rudder is built. The boat is not yet in the water.

Through all of it, I have kept sending photographs of Miramis to Sven Yrvind, the man who pointed me at this design in the first place and who sailed Matt Layden's own Paradox to windward back in 1997. I do not write often, only when there is a photograph worth sending, but the answer has never once failed to come back: "he always answers with a thumbs up and a few encouraging words." He is the design's oldest living witness. After thirty years of builders scattered across six continents working through chine runners, ballast and tacking vices, it comes down, in the end, to that: one man, who has watched this boat longer than anyone, still looking at the newest photographs and still approving of what he sees.

When Miramis is finished, I will send him those photographs too. In the meantime, if you are building one of your own, send me the photographs.
