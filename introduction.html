Introduction

Paradox at first glance might be rejected as not seeming correct or practical because of its unusual features, small size, etc. However, after studying the design in detail and its realizing it in the building process, it becomes plain that it is in fact completely right. That by the way is the dictionary definition of the word "paradox".

Don Elliott, Messing About in Boats, 15 September 1996

Paradox was first launched by Matt Layden in 1993. She is a microcruiser 13 feet 10 inches (4.2 metres) long and a shade over 4 feet (1.23 metres) wide, and boats built to her plans have completed a wide range of voyages. They have cruised the Florida coast and crossed the Gulf Stream to the Bahamas, as Layden's own boat did in her first two seasons. They have sailed to the Isles of Scilly and back, to Cuba and home again, and 415 miles through the Hawaiian islands from Hilo to Kauai. They have cruised the Patagonian coast of Argentina. One Paradox rounded Cape Leeuwin, the great south-western corner of Australia, and kept on past it for several days without putting in. No single boat did all of this. These voyages belong to different hulls, built by different owners in different countries over thirty years. That is a stronger claim for a design than any one boat's logbook could make. Every one of them was made within reach of a coast. She is a capable coastal cruiser, built for the coast and proved on it. No Paradox has crossed an ocean.

She does not look like a boat that does such things. On her trailer she is a plywood box with a flat bottom, hard corners and a low cabin roof. Fully loaded she draws 9 inches (23 centimetres) of water. Where a keel or a centreboard would be there is nothing. Only two shallow strips of wood a couple of inches wide run along the bottom edges of the hull, where the sides meet the bottom. Her one sail rolls up around its own boom like a window blind, and the three lines that work it lead inside. The sailor sits on a seat under a sliding hatch and hoists, reefs, steers and sails from there, hatch open in fine weather and shut in foul. She sleeps aboard on a berth 930 by 1,900 millimetres, a little over 3 feet by 6 feet 3 inches.

She sat on a beach near St Petersburg, Florida, on the morning of 8 March 2003. She was one of thirty entrants in the Everglades Challenge, a race of some 300 miles down the Gulf coast to Key Largo with no motors allowed and no outside help. The others were sea kayaks and decked canoes built to be paddled day and night, a handful of small sailboats and two multihulls. Beside them Layden's boat, her hatch shut, looked like a packing crate somebody had rigged with a sail. For ten years she had done nothing more adventurous than cruise the waters near his home. Three and a half days later she crossed the line at Key Largo first of the thirty.

Before the start, he had written on his entry form what kind of boat she was: "She is not fast but can keep sailing in any weather and run hairy inlets safe & dry."
